In 1949 a Canadian psychologist wrote down, almost in passing, the rule
that sits under every learning machine on this trail: a connection that is
used gets stronger. He could not show that it was true, and he said so.

## A psychologist's postulate

**Donald Olding Hebb** (1904–1985) was born in Chester, Nova Scotia, and
set out to be a novelist before he turned to psychology. He studied under
**Karl Lashley** at Chicago and Harvard, worked with the neurosurgeon
**Wilder Penfield** at the Montreal Neurological Institute on what brain
surgery did to thinking, and wrote his book while working with Lashley again
at the Yerkes primate laboratories in Florida. _The Organization of
Behavior: A Neuropsychological Theory_ came out from Wiley in New York in
1949, after he had returned to McGill as a professor. Its aim was to explain
behaviour and thought in terms of what the brain's cells actually do.

On page 62 is the passage everyone quotes. Hebb introduced it as an
assumption:

> When an axon of cell A is near enough to excite a cell B and repeatedly or
> persistently takes part in firing it, some growth process or metabolic
> change takes place in one or both cells such that A's efficiency, as one
> of the cells firing B, is increased.

The most probable mechanism, he went on, was that "synaptic knobs develop
and increase the area of contact" between the axon and the cell it feeds:
the knob magnified in the drawing above. Then he added: "There is certainly
no direct evidence that this is so."

## Cell assemblies

The postulate served a larger idea. Hebb proposed that any frequently
repeated stimulation leads to "the slow development of a 'cell-assembly'", a
diffuse structure of cells in the cortex and diencephalon "capable of
acting briefly as a closed system", and that a series of such assemblies, each setting off the next,
makes a _phase sequence_: "the thought process". On this view a memory is
not kept in one place. It lives in the strengthened connections among a
group of cells, so that part of a pattern can call up the whole. That idea,
later called _auto-association_, comes back on this trail in 1982, when a
physicist builds a network that completes patterns in just this way.

## The slogan he didn't write

The summary everyone knows, "cells that fire together wire together", is not
Hebb's. It is usually credited to the neurobiologist **Carla Shatz**, who
wrote in _Scientific American_ in September 1992, describing how the visual
system wires itself before birth: "In a sense, then, cells that fire
together wire together." A similar line, "neurons wire together if they fire
together", had appeared in _Science_ that January, in a paper by **Siegrid
Löwel** and **Wolf Singer**, and Shatz had been using her version in
lectures for some years before. The linguist Mark Liberman remembered
hearing a version in a 1966 lecture, but could find no trace of it in print
and concluded the memory might be a false one. So Hebb had the idea in 1949;
the phrase belongs to around 1992, and exactly who said it first is
uncertain.

The slogan also loses something. Hebb wrote that A must "take part in
firing" B, which means A fires just before B, not merely at the same time.
That emphasis on order anticipated what neuroscientists now call
_spike-timing-dependent plasticity_. The traces at the top of the drawing
show it: each spike on A's line is followed, a moment later, by one on B's.

## From synapse to weight

In a machine, Hebb's rule becomes arithmetic. The weight between two units
grows when both are active together; in its simplest form it is the product
of their activities, averaged over the patterns to be learned. Biology later
found support for Hebb-like strengthening: work in **Eric Kandel**'s
laboratory on the sea slug _Aplysia_ found that Hebbian long-term
potentiation is one of the mechanisms its synapses need to learn. When the
Nobel Committee for Physics explained the 2024 prize for neural networks, it
began with Hebb's hypothesis, which it said "is still used as one of the
basic rules for updating artificial networks".

What Hebb's rule does not do is correct itself. It strengthens whatever
happens together, whether or not it was the right answer. The next step came
from two engineers at Stanford in 1960, with a rule that learns from its
mistakes.
