The image generators that reached the public in 2022 did not work like a
painter, adding strokes to a blank canvas. They started with static, pure
random noise, and removed it a little at a time, until what was left was a
picture. The idea came from physics in 2015, took five years to produce
images as good as the best methods of the day, and then in the space of a
few months in 2022 became the engine of the new image generators.

## Destroying structure, slowly

In 2015 **Jascha Sohl-Dickstein** and colleagues at Stanford and Berkeley
published _Deep Unsupervised Learning using Nonequilibrium Thermodynamics_
at the ICML conference. Their idea, borrowed from statistical physics, was to
"systematically and slowly destroy structure in a data distribution" by
adding a little noise over many steps, a _forward diffusion_, until nothing
but noise remained; and then to train a network to run the process
backwards, one small step at a time. Because each step is small, each
reversal is simple enough to learn. Their first demonstration was a
two-dimensional spiral of points, the "swiss roll" in the drawing.

In 2020
**Jonathan Ho**, **Ajay Jain** and **Pieter Abbeel** at Berkeley changed
that with _Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models_ (DDPM). They trained the
network to predict the noise that had been added at each step, found a link
to an older method called score matching, and used 1,000 steps. On the
CIFAR-10 benchmark their samples scored a record FID of 3.17, and on larger
images their samples were similar in quality to ProgressiveGAN, one of the
leading GANs.

## Text to image, 2022

What made diffusion famous was steering it with words. On 6 April 2022
OpenAI announced **DALL·E 2**, which turned a caption into an image
embedding from its CLIP model and then used a diffusion model to decode that
embedding into pixels. In May, Google's **Imagen** paper reported that the
bigger the frozen text encoder (Google's T5 language model), the better the
pictures, more so than enlarging the image model. DALL·E 2 opened slowly,
through a waitlist, and Imagen was at first described only in a paper.

The model that spread furthest came from Germany. In December 2021 **Robin
Rombach**, **Andreas Blattmann**, **Dominik Lorenz**, **Patrick Esser** and
**Björn Ommer**, of LMU Munich and Heidelberg University (Esser was at the
video company Runway), published _latent diffusion_: run the diffusion not on
pixels, where training "often consumes hundreds of GPU days", but in the
compressed space of an autoencoder, which cut the cost sharply, and condition
it on text through cross-attention. On 22 August 2022 **Stable Diffusion**, a latent
diffusion model developed by CompVis at LMU Munich and Runway with compute
donated by Stability AI, was released with its weights, under a licence that
allowed commercial use. It needed a graphics card with about 7 GB of memory.
Anyone could run it, change it and build on it, and front ends, add-ons and
fine-tuned versions soon grew up around it.

## Whose pictures?

Stable Diffusion was trained on subsets of **LAION-5B**, a public dataset of
some five billion image and caption pairs gathered by the German non-profit
LAION from the Common Crawl archive of the web. The pictures in it belonged
to someone.

On 12 January 2023 the artists **Sarah Andersen**, **Kelly McKernan** and
**Karla Ortiz** filed a class action in the United States against Stability AI,
Midjourney and DeviantArt, arguing that training on their work without
consent infringed their copyrights. In 2023 Judge William Orrick indicated
he would dismiss most of the first complaint but let the artists file a new
one, and in August 2024 he allowed their copyright claims to proceed; the case was set for
trial in September 2026.

In England, **Getty Images** sued Stability AI the same month. Getty dropped
its central claim about training during the trial, accepting that there was
no evidence the model had been trained in the UK. On 4 November 2025 the
High Court rejected Getty's remaining copyright claim, finding that "the
model weights are not themselves an infringing copy" of the photographs, and
found only limited trade mark infringement where the model had reproduced
Getty's watermark. As Getty's claims narrowed, commentators noted, the case
could not be "the silver bullet which many had originally anticipated": the
central question, whether training on copyrighted work is lawful, was left
largely unanswered.

Image models learned to draw what they were asked. Language models had a
harder lesson ahead: to answer the way people wanted, which took human
judgement, turned into a reward.
