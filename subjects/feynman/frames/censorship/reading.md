Censoring the mail of people inside the United States was not something
the Army could simply order. At [Los Alamos](kloom:e/los-alamos-national-laboratory) it began, according to the
laboratory's official history, with a rumor in 1943 that letters were
already being opened. The director protested to General [Groves](kloom:e/leslie-groves), whose
investigation found nothing; but by then many of the scientists wanted
official censorship rather than a suspicion of the unofficial kind, and it
began in **December 1943**. It was done by a standard military censorship
office in [Santa Fe](kloom:e/santa-fe-new-mexico), under the laboratory's intelligence officer, Captain
**[Peer de Silva](kloom:e/peer-de-silva)**.

Because it was delicate, it was set up as a voluntary agreement. Outgoing
letters were left unsealed; the censor sealed those that passed and sent
back the rest, citing the paragraph they broke. Incoming letters could be
opened and read but, the scientists were promised, not otherwise touched.
Criticism of the administration was expressly allowed. [Feynman](kloom:e/richard-feynman) told the
story of what happened next twice, in 1966 and in 1975, and it is the
comic half of his letters with **[Arline](kloom:e/arline-feynman)**, who wrote to him from her sanatorium
bed as often as he wrote to her, several times a week.

## Codes without keys

On the first day, by his account, he was called in to explain a letter
from his father covered in dots above and below the lines, and one from
Arline in five-letter groups of nonsense. They were codes, he said, and he
did not know the keys: it was a game, in which they tried to send him a
cipher he could not break. The censors could hardly stop incoming mail
under the agreement, so it was settled that the key would be enclosed and
that they would take it out before he saw the letter.

| What happened                                             | The rule, as the censor saw it         |
| --------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------- |
| Codes from his father and Arline, without keys            | enclose the key; the censor removes it |
| A word blotted out in Arline's letter                     | incoming mail not to be touched        |
| Her shopping list: litharge, glycerine, hot dogs, laundry | taken out as a code without a key      |
| His note on the digits of one divided by 243              | paragraph 17B: no codes                |
| Arline mentions the censor                                | do not mention censorship              |
| A letter on a jigsaw puzzle                               | "confine herself to ordinary letters"  |

Arline's letters came blotted, then cut about. The list was real: she
wanted litharge and glycerine to make a cement and mend a box. The note
bounced under paragraph 17B, which listed the languages letters might be
written in and banned codes. He had been playing with a desk calculator,
and noticed that the decimal of one divided by 243 runs 004 115 226 337
448 559 670 781 893 and repeats, each group 111 more than the last except
where a carry intervenes. The plate draws the cycle. He answered that there
was no more information in the digits than in the number 243 itself, and
asked leave to use Arabic numerals. Curiously, the 1966 transcript and the
1975 talk as printed both give the divisor as 273, whose decimal is quite
different; only 243 gives his digits.

The next paradox took longer. Arline was not to mention the censorship,
but only he could tell her so, and a letter saying so was itself sent
back. The censors' answer, which he admitted was reasonable, was that a
letter from Albuquerque might be intercepted on the way. So he told her on
his next visit, and in his 1975 telling they agreed a code after all, a
dot after his signature to mean that he had been in trouble again. She
answered with a letter on a jigsaw puzzle, and they had ready a letter
full of powdered Pepto-Bismol, never sent.

## Knowing what would pass

Being the censor's most frequent customer, he said, he knew better than
anyone what would get through, and won bets on it. When one of his
group's young men, **[John Kemeny](kloom:e/john-g-kemeny)**, was woken at night and questioned by
Army security about his father, Feynman described it in a letter as a
complaint about the administration, which was allowed. When he found holes
in the outer fence that the construction workers used as short cuts, he
went out through the gate and back through a hole until the guard noticed,
then described the holes in a letter, with their positions. The censor
could not stop it without admitting the holes, so, he said, the only thing
to do was mend them.

The official history confirms the censorship and its date, and his
letters to Arline from these years were published in 2005 in [_Perfectly
Reasonable Deviations from the Beaten Track_](kloom:e/perfectly-reasonable-deviations-from-the-beaten-track). The skirmishes are his
telling.
