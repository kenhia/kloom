Feynman ate in the students' cafeteria at Cornell, he admitted, partly to
look at the girls. One day, in the week after he had decided to stop
worrying about being important, a student threw a plate into the air. It
spun, and as it spun it wobbled, and the university seal on its rim went
round too. The two motions, he saw, were related. He had nothing to do, so
he set out to find out how.

## His telling

He told the story twice for the record, and the two agree closely. In an
interview with the historian **Charles Weiner** in 1966 he said he worked
out the motion from the equations, found a simple ratio when the wobble was
small, and then spent an afternoon finding a way to see it from Newton's
laws alone, particle by particle, without the formal machinery. He ran to
**Hans Bethe** with it. Bethe asked what the importance was. There was
none, Feynman told him; it was fun, and that was all he was going to do
from now on. In _"Surely You're Joking, Mr. Feynman!"_ (1985) the seal has
become the red Cornell medallion, and the story ends with a larger claim:
the diagrams and the rest of the work that won him the Nobel Prize, he
said, came from that "piddling around with the wobbling plate".

The claim is not idle. Within a week, he said in 1966, rotation had led
him to the spinning electron, and to the old problem of fitting spin into
his path integrals. He read Hamilton on quaternions in the mathematics
library and made a relativistic electron work by path integrals in one
dimension of space, though never in three. He put the date in his second
year at Cornell, the academic year 1946–47.

## What the plate does

In both tellings the seal goes round twice for each wobble: two to one,
and the wobble at half the speed of the spin. A plate in the air does the
opposite. The physicist **Benjamin Chao** pointed this out in _Physics Today_
in 1989, and a 2007 paper in the _American Journal of
Physics_ by **Slavomir Tuleja** and colleagues, which found an elementary
explanation of the kind Feynman described, notes that nobody knows whether
the slip was his memory or one more joke. It is only fair to add that the
right ratio is still two to one. He simply had it upside down.

The plate draws the reason. In the air nothing twists the plate, so its
angular momentum, _L_, keeps one direction in space. A flat plate is
twice as hard to turn about its axis as about a diameter; for any flat
shape the moment about the axis is the sum of the moments about two
perpendicular diameters. Because of that factor of two, the plate's axis cannot line up
with _L_. It swings round _L_ on a narrow cone, the wobble, and the angular
velocity, _ω_, sits on the far side of _L_. Solve the motion (Euler's
equations for a free body) and, for a small tilt, the axis goes round the
cone twice while the seal goes round once.

The ratio is the plate's moment of inertia about its axis divided by that
about a diameter, so a thicker object wobbles more slowly. The numbers
below are computed for this frame from the moments of a uniform cylinder,
not measured.

axis, so its axis simply turns with it. A real plate, dished and rimmed,
comes a little under two.

## Playing

What mattered to Feynman was not the ratio but the mood. He had spent two
years believing he was finished; now he was doing physics again because
it amused him, the way he had as a boy with his radios. Within the year he
was calculating in quantum electrodynamics with methods nobody else used,
and in the spring of 1948 he was to show them to the most distinguished
audience in physics. That meeting is the next frame, "Dirac asked if it
was UNITARY."
