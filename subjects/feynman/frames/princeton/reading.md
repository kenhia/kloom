Before Princeton took Feynman as a graduate student in 1939, it looked at his examinations and asked a question about his family. His scores were extraordinary: a perfect result in physics, by the usual account unprecedented, and an outstanding one in mathematics, with poor marks in history and English. The head of the physics department, **Henry DeWolf Smyth**, wrote to **Philip Morse** at MIT to ask, "Is Feynman Jewish?" The department had no rule against Jews, Smyth went on, but kept their numbers small because they were hard to place in jobs. Morse replied that Feynman was Jewish, and offered the reassurance that his appearance and manner showed no sign of it. The exchange is quoted in **James Gleick**'s biography, _Genius_ (1992). Feynman was admitted. Columbia's refusal four years earlier had usually been put down to the same thing.

## A tea, a gown and a basement

He arrived that autumn and lived in the **Graduate College**, where students in every field ate dinner together in academic gowns. On his first day, by his own telling, he went to the dean's tea, and when the dean's wife asked whether he took cream or lemon, answered "both". Her reply, delivered with a nervous laugh, became the title of his memoir forty-five years later: _"Surely You're Joking, Mr. Feynman!"_ It is a story he told Charles Weiner in 1966 as well as in the book, but it is still only his.

He told Weiner too why he had come. MIT's cyclotron was grand, with its controls behind glass in a separate room, but the results in the journals came from Princeton's. When he went to see Princeton's, he found it at the end of the basement in the physics building, in a room strung with wires, dripping with water and hung with sealing wax, and recognised at once his own boyhood laboratory. Research, he decided, was fiddling. The wiring later caught fire.

## Wheeler

He had expected to be research assistant to **Eugene Wigner**, and found himself assigned instead to **John Archibald Wheeler**, an assistant professor only seven years older. At their first meeting Wheeler set a pocket watch on the table to keep time. At the second, Feynman laid a dollar watch beside it, and the two of them laughed until they could not work. Wheeler gave him problems in scattering theory and invited him home to dinner; there were no required courses, and Feynman remembered taking only Wigner's on solid-state physics and Wheeler's on nuclear physics, for which he wrote up the notes. He learned, he said, from problems and from other students.

To prepare for the qualifying examination he went back to MIT for a month or two, where nobody would interrupt him, and rebuilt the whole of physics in a notebook titled "Things I Don't Know About", reducing each subject to its kernel. He passed, though his account admits a stumble over which colour is on top of a rainbow.

## The war arrives

In the summer of 1941 he turned down a place he had wanted for years at Bell Telephone Laboratories to work at the **Frankford Arsenal** in Philadelphia, where the Army was building a mechanical director for anti-aircraft guns. A director has to aim not at the aircraft but at where it will be when the shell gets there. He began by checking drawings of non-circular gears, the cams that let a machine compute, and ended the summer in private conversations with his chief about the design of the whole machine. The plate draws the aiming problem and a pair of such gears.

Back at Princeton, some months later, **Robert R. Wilson** came into his office with a secret. Wilson had a scheme, the **isotron**, for separating uranium-235 from uranium-238 with electric fields, a step without which there could be no uranium bomb, and wanted Feynman for the theory. Feynman said no; he had a thesis to finish. Then, by his account, he sat and thought about what it would mean if Hitler got a bomb first, put the thesis in a drawer, and went to Wilson's meeting at three o'clock. He worked on space charge and the limits on current with **Paul Olum**, a mathematics student, while the experimenters stripped their own apparatus to build the thing. The isotron was abandoned in favour of Lawrence's calutron, and in 1943 Wilson's group went together to Los Alamos.

The drawer held the idea he and Wheeler had been working on since 1940: that half of every light wave runs backwards in time.
