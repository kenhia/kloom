On 10 July 1897 the Badische Anilin- und Soda-Fabrik, **[BASF](kloom:e/basf)**, began to sell **[indigo](kloom:e/indigo-dye)**, the blue that had come for two thousand years from India's indigo plants, and not one molecule of it had grown in a field. The label said "Indigo Pure B.A.S.F.". Helmut Schmidt, who ran BASF's indigo plant for twenty years, counts seventeen years of work and about 18 million gold marks spent before the first sale, more than the whole share capital of the company.

## A formula in a letter

The first chemist to make it was **[Adolf von Baeyer](kloom:e/adolf-von-baeyer)**, who began work on indigo in 1865. He made it in the laboratory in 1878 from isatin, one of the fragments indigo breaks into. In 1880 he found a second route from cinnamic acid, and BASF bought the patent for 100,000 marks. His third route, from 2-nitrobenzaldehyde and acetone, is the one chemistry students still run. Schmidt dates it to 1882, when Baeyer and Viggo Drewsen published it, while Wikipedia's article on the dye puts it in 1880 in one place and 1882 in another; we follow Schmidt.

What indigo actually was took longer. On 3 August 1883, in a letter to **[Heinrich Caro](kloom:e/heinrich-caro)**, head of research at BASF, Baeyer broke off from family news to write down the formula. The plate draws it: two identical halves, each a benzene ring fused to a five-membered ring holding a nitrogen, joined by a double bond at the center. Each half's N–H sits beside the other half's C=O, and the two dotted lines on the plate are the hydrogen bonds between them. Swap one hydrogen on each benzene ring for a bromine and the molecule becomes 6,6′-dibromoindigo, the **[Tyrian purple](kloom:e/tyrian-purple)** of the murex snail. Two bromines take the mass from 262 to 420.

## Why dyeing indigo is chemistry

Indigo does not dissolve in water, so it cannot soak into a fiber. The dyer has to reduce it first to a soluble form, leuco-indigo, "white indigo", which is yellow. Cloth dipped in that comes out yellow, then goes green and blue in the air as oxygen turns the leuco form back into indigo, locked inside the fiber. For centuries the reducing agent was fermentation, stale urine or zinc; from 1906 BASF sold sodium dithionite, Na₂S₂O₄, for the job. Written out, and balanced by our arithmetic from the atomic weights (C 12.01, H 1.01, N 14.01, O 16.00, Na 22.99, S 32.06):

| Step       | Reaction                                                         | Per 262 g of indigo         |
| ---------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------- |
| In the vat | C₁₆H₁₀N₂O₂ + Na₂S₂O₄ + 4 NaOH → C₁₆H₁₀N₂O₂Na₂ + 2 Na₂SO₃ + 2 H₂O | 174 g dithionite, 160 g lye |
| In the air | C₁₆H₁₀N₂O₂Na₂ + ½ O₂ + H₂O → C₁₆H₁₀N₂O₂ + 2 NaOH                 | 16 g of oxygen              |

So at least two-thirds of a kilogram of dithionite goes into every kilogram of indigo. In the reduced form the central double bond has become single and the two carbonyls have become C–O⁻; the molecule twists out of flat, and the long run of alternating single and double bonds that absorbs orange light is broken. That is why the vat is yellow and the cloth turns blue. The plant was a poor source: its leaves hold 0.2 to 0.8 per cent of indican, and two indicans give one indigo, so by our arithmetic a tonne of leaves at 0.5 per cent yields at most 2.2 kilograms of dye.

## Seventeen years at Ludwigshafen

Baeyer's routes worked in a flask and failed in a factory. The nitrobenzaldehyde route needed about four tonnes of toluene for every tonne of indigo, when the whole coal-tar industry yielded only five or six thousand tonnes a year. In 1890 **Karl Heumann** in Zurich showed that indigo formed when phenylglycine, or better its carboxylic acid, was fused with caustic potash, and the acid could be made, through anthranilic acid, from naphthalene, which tar supplied without limit. Oxidizing the naphthalene cheaply was the last obstacle. In 1891, the company's story goes, a laboratory assistant broke a mercury thermometer into a flask, and the chemist Eugen Sapper found that the mercury catalyzed the oxidation.

A circular from a rival, the Elberfeld dye works of **Bayer**, told customers that the new product was plant indigo repackaged. It launched at 16 marks a kilogram against 20 for refined natural indigo, and by 1904 competition with Hoechst had driven it to 7.

## The stain of indigo

Before the synthesis, Bengal grew four-fifths of the world's indigo. India's indigo fields shrank from about 700,000 hectares in 1897 to 121,000 in 1914. The First World War cut off German exports, and by 1916 they were back to 300,000. In Champaran, in the Tirhut country of north Bihar that _The Graphic_ still called Bengal in 1881, tenants were bound to plant three parts in twenty of their land with indigo for the planters, the _tinkathia_ system. When synthetic dye made indigo unprofitable, planters let tenants off for higher rents, Wikipedia's account runs, then forced the crop back when the war made it pay. In 1917 a tenant farmer, Rajkumar Shukla, persuaded **[Mahatma Gandhi](kloom:e/mahatma-gandhi)** to come and see. Ordered out of the district, Gandhi refused and pleaded guilty; the case was withdrawn, and a committee on which he sat recommended ending the system. The _tinkathia_ system, Gandhi wrote in his autobiography, "had been in existence for about a century". The **[Champaran Satyagraha](kloom:e/champaran-satyagraha)** was his first campaign of civil disobedience in India.

The dye works were already making medicines. At Bayer in Elberfeld, on 10 August 1897, a month after BASF's launch, a chemist wrote up a pure form of an old remedy: aspirin, the next frame.
