In 1907, in a home laboratory in Yonkers, New York, the Belgian chemist **[Leo Baekeland](kloom:e/leo-baekeland)** heated two ordinary chemicals, phenol and formaldehyde, in a sealed vessel under heat and pressure. Out came a hard, amber resin that would neither melt nor dissolve once it had set. He applied for a patent that July and announced it to the American Chemical Society in February 1909. **[Bakelite](kloom:e/bakelite)**, the first plastic made entirely from synthetic chemicals, was molded into insulators, radios and telephones, and when Baekeland died in 1944 the world made about 175,000 tons of it a year. Nobody could yet say what it was.

## The aggregate school

Chemists had measured enormous molecular weights for rubber, starch and cellulose, and most of them did not believe the numbers. The leading view was that such materials were _aggregates_: clusters of small molecules held together by weak, ill-defined forces, as soap molecules gather in water. Some argued that no molecule could be larger than the crystal's unit cell that X-rays revealed. Natural rubber, in the view of Carl Harries and Rudolf Pummerer, was small rings of isoprene stuck together by "partial valences" at their double bonds.

In 1920 **[Hermann Staudinger](kloom:e/hermann-staudinger)**, professor of organic chemistry in Zurich, published "Über Polymerisation", arguing the opposite. Rubber, cellulose and the like were single, very long molecules, their small units joined end to end by the same covalent bonds as any other organic compound. By 1922 he called them _Makromoleküle_. He tested it on rubber. If its clusters were held by partial valences at the double bonds, adding hydrogen to those bonds should break them up; he hydrogenated rubber and got something much like rubber. Later he showed by the viscosity of solutions that the molecules' size survived chemical change.

The resistance lasted nearly twenty years. **[Heinrich Wieland](kloom:e/heinrich-otto-wieland)**, who won the 1927 Nobel Prize, wrote to him: "Dear colleague, drop the idea of large molecules; organic molecules with a molecular weight higher than 5000 do not exist." Colleagues called the field _Schmierenchemie_, grease chemistry. Staudinger was used to standing apart: in the war he had publicly opposed his old friend Fritz Haber's poison gas. The X-ray work of Herman Mark and others in the 1930s, and the syntheses below, settled it, and Staudinger received the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1953.

## Counting the links

The idea of a **[polymer](kloom:e/polymer)** makes one simple calculation possible. Divide a chain's molar mass by its repeating unit's, and you have the number of units, the _degree of polymerisation_. Polythene is ethylene, C₂H₄, strung end to end, each unit weighing 2 × 12.01 + 4 × 1.008 = 28.05 grams a mole. For a chain of 100,000 grams a mole, a round number chosen for illustration:

By our arithmetic, that one molecule holds some 3,560 links, twenty times the limit Wieland set. The plate draws a stretch of its zigzag, each carbon bond at its fixed angle, and the whole chain as it really lies, a loose random coil.

## Built to order

At **[DuPont](kloom:e/dupont)**, **[Wallace Carothers](kloom:e/wallace-carothers)** came at the question from the other side. Hired in 1928 to do pure research, he set out to build long molecules from small ones by well-understood reactions, joining molecules with a reactive group at each end. Each link split off a molecule of water, and his first polyesters stuck at molecular weights of about 4,000, held short, he concluded, by the water the reaction made. Removing it in a molecular still, his colleague Julian Hill made polyesters of about 12,000 in 1930 and drew them out into fibers. On 28 February 1935 Gerard Berchet, in Carothers's group, made a polyamide from adipic acid and hexamethylenediamine, each with six carbons: **[nylon 66](kloom:e/nylon-66)**.

n HOOC(CH₂)₄COOH + n H₂N(CH₂)₆NH₂ → [–OC(CH₂)₄CO–NH(CH₂)₆NH–]ₙ + 2n H₂O

Every amide bond releases one water, so the reaction runs only as water is driven off. Carothers did not see nylon sold: he killed himself in April 1937, a year and a half before DuPont announced it. Polythene came from an accident at ICI in Northwich in 1933, when Eric Fawcett and Reginald Gibson squeezed ethylene to several hundred atmospheres and found a white wax, made by traces of oxygen. It was reproducible from 1935, and in the war it insulated radar cables.

## Rubber in the cold

Rubber's spring is its long chains uncoiling when stretched and coiling back, tied together at a few points so they cannot flow away. Cool a rubber and its chains move ever more sluggishly, until below its glass transition, about −70 °C for tire rubber, it is hard and glassy. In 1986 the physicist Richard Feynman showed that stiffening at the temperature of ice, with O-ring rubber from a model of the Challenger shuttle's booster joint, squeezed in a clamp and dropped in a glass of iced water.
