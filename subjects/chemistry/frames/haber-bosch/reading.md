In 1898 the chemist **[William Crookes](kloom:e/william-crookes)** used his presidential address to the British Association, at Bristol, to warn that the world would run short of wheat. The wheat-eating peoples were multiplying, the good land was nearly all taken, and the only way to grow more on it was nitrogen fertilizer, which came from dwindling stores: the seabird guano of Peru's islands, already dug out, and the saltpeter, sodium nitrate, of Chile's Atacama desert, whose exports he put at about 1,200,000 tons a year. Yet the air is four-fifths nitrogen. "It is through the laboratory", he said, "that starvation may ultimately be turned into plenty." Germany, with poor soil and no nitrate of its own, felt it most: its imports of Chilean saltpeter rose from 350,000 tonnes in 1900 to 900,000 in 1912.

## A reaction that runs both ways

Nitrogen gas is two atoms held by a triple bond, and it will hardly react with anything. Made to combine with hydrogen, it gives **[ammonia](kloom:e/ammonia)**, but the reaction also runs backwards:

N₂ + 3 H₂ ⇌ 2 NH₃, giving out 92 kJ for each mole of nitrogen

Where it settles is a tug of war that **[Le Chatelier's principle](kloom:e/le-chateliers-principle)** describes: a mixture at equilibrium shifts to undo whatever is done to it. Four molecules of gas become two, so squeezing the gas pushes it towards ammonia. The reaction gives out heat, so heating it pushes it back. Cold and crushed is best, but in the cold nothing happens at all: the molecules need heat to react, even on a catalyst. In 1905 **[Fritz Haber](kloom:e/fritz-haber)**, at Karlsruhe, measured the equilibrium near 1,000 °C at ordinary pressure and found about one part of ammonia in 20,000. He thought it hopeless and let it rest for three years.

Then he went to the limit of his compressor, 200 atmospheres. Haber published his measurements as an equation for the equilibrium, and it can be worked for any temperature and pressure. By our arithmetic from his equation, treating the gases as ideal, a mixture of three parts hydrogen to one of nitrogen settles at:

At 300 °C the yield would be nearly two-thirds, if any catalyst worked there; none did. Haber's osmium and uranium worked at 500–600 °C, where the yield at 200 atmospheres is between 8 and 18 per cent. The trade-off is the whole design: a temperature hot enough for the catalyst, a pressure high enough to pay for the heat, and a loop that takes the ammonia out, cools it to a liquid and sends the unused gas round again. Haber's apparatus, built with his assistant Robert Le Rossignol, did all three, and passed the heat of the outgoing gas to the incoming.

## Drop by drop

In July 1909 men from **[BASF](kloom:e/basf)** came to Karlsruhe to see it. The tabletop machine made liquid ammonia, drop by drop, at about 250 milliliters every two hours. Wikipedia's history of the process quotes Vaclav Smil's figure of 80 grams an hour, and by our arithmetic the two agree: liquid ammonia near 0 °C weighs 0.64 grams a milliliter, so 125 milliliters is 80 grams. BASF bought the process and gave it to **[Carl Bosch](kloom:e/carl-bosch)**, a chemist who had begun as a metallurgist, to make it a factory.

Two things nearly stopped him. The first was the catalyst: osmium was so rare that BASF had bought up the world's supply. **[Alwin Mittasch](kloom:e/alwin-mittasch)** found that iron, with small additions such as aluminum and potassium oxides, worked as well and lasted; Bosch counted the tests that got there at 20,000, and Wikipedia's article on Mittasch counts 10,000. The second was the steel. Bosch's first reactor tubes burst after 80 hours. Under the microscope he saw why: hydrogen had soaked into the hot steel, pulled out its carbon and left it brittle. His answer was to split the wall's two jobs. A thin liner of soft steel kept the gas in; hydrogen that leaked through it escaped through grooves and many small holes drilled in a thick outer jacket, which held the pressure and stayed sound. The plate draws it. The works at Oppau, on the Rhine, began making ammonia on 9 September 1913, and in 1914 made 8,700 tonnes. A single pass over the catalyst turned only about a sixth of the gas into ammonia, Bosch said, which is why it went round and round.

## Bread from air

The **[Haber process](kloom:e/haber-process)** made Germany's nitric acid for explosives through the First World War, and Haber himself turned to poison gas, the story of the next frame. He received the Nobel Prize in Chemistry for 1918, Bosch shared the prize of 1931, and Haber ended his Nobel lecture on the farmer who, with the chemist's help, "changes stones into bread".

Today, as of 2026, the process still makes almost all of the world's synthetic ammonia. The International Energy Agency reckoned in 2021 that making ammonia takes about 2% of the world's final energy use, and that about 70% of it becomes fertilizer. How many people it feeds is an estimate, and the estimates differ: Smil put it at about 40% of the world's people in 2000, and Jan Willem Erisman and his colleagues at 48% in 2008. Crookes's plenty came through the laboratory, as he said it would, from coal and natural gas and a reactor with holes in it.
