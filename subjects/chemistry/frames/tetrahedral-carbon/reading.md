In September 1874 a Dutch student of twenty-two, **[Jacobus Henricus van 't Hoff](kloom:e/jacobus-henricus-van-t-hoff)**, published at his own expense a short pamphlet with a long title: _A proposal for the extension of the structural formulas now used in chemistry into space_. Two months later, in Paris, **[Joseph Achille Le Bel](kloom:e/joseph-achille-le-bel)** published the same idea in the _Bulletin_ of the Chemical Society, reached by a different road, from the symmetry of the whole molecule, and independently. Their idea was that a molecule has a shape in three dimensions, and that the four bonds of a carbon atom point to the corners of a **[tetrahedron](kloom:e/tetrahedron)**, with the carbon at its centre.

## Too many isomers

Chemists of the 1860s drew carbon compounds as flat diagrams, four bonds at right angles, and van 't Hoff began with what was wrong with them. If the diagram were the molecule, methane with two of its hydrogens replaced, CH₂R₂, could be built two ways, with the two R's next to each other or across from each other, and chemists should find two compounds. They only ever found one. Put the four bonds on a tetrahedron and every corner is next to every other, so there is only one.

The same counting says something new about a carbon holding four different groups, which van 't Hoff called an **[asymmetric carbon atom](kloom:e/asymmetric-carbon)**. By our arithmetic, four different groups can be put on four corners in 4 × 3 × 2 × 1 = 24 ways. A molecule is the same molecule however it is turned, and a regular tetrahedron can be turned 12 ways onto itself, each leaving it where it was: the twelve turns form what mathematicians call a group. So the 24 placements make 24 ÷ 12 = 2 molecules, and the two are mirror images that cannot be laid one on the other, like a pair of hands.

They were not quite the first to think in three dimensions. Wikipedia's article on stereochemistry says that Kekulé made tetrahedral models in 1862 without publishing them, and that the Italian chemist Emanuele Paternò drew a three-dimensional molecule in 1869.

## The shape of light

Mirror twins explained an old puzzle. Since the work of Biot and Pasteur, chemists had known that many substances from living things, sugars, tartaric acid, lactic acid, twist the plane of polarised light, and that some come in a right-handed and a left-handed form, as **[Louis Pasteur](kloom:e/louis-pasteur)** had shown by sorting crystals, the story of the frame on chirality. Van 't Hoff went through the list of every optically active compound whose structure was known, and found an asymmetric carbon atom in each. He found one exception, a propyl alcohol, and a colleague told him privately that its small rotation came from an impurity. He also used the rule forwards: a compound that twists light must contain such a carbon, which settles which of two formulas is right, and CHBrClI, bromochloroiodomethane, should come in two active forms.

The tetrahedra also explained why fumaric acid and maleic acid, with the same formula, are two different compounds. Joined by a double bond, two carbons are two tetrahedra sharing an edge, which cannot turn about it, and the groups on them can lie on the same side or on opposite sides.

## How big the angle is

The pamphlet names no angle; the geometry fixes one. Put the carbon at the centre of a cube, at (0, 0, 0), and its four neighbours at four corners, no two of them joined by an edge of the cube: (1, 1, 1), (1, −1, −1), (−1, 1, −1) and (−1, −1, 1). Take any two bonds. Their dot product is 1 × 1 + 1 × (−1) + 1 × (−1) = −1, and each is √3 long, so the cosine of the angle between them is −1/3, and the angle is 109.47°. It is the same between every pair. The plate draws the tetrahedron in its cube. Methane's four bonds meet at it.

## Pegasus

In 1877, when van 't Hoff was teaching at the Veterinary School in Utrecht, a German version of his work appeared with a foreword by the chemist Johannes Wislicenus, and drew a furious review from **[Hermann Kolbe](kloom:e/hermann-kolbe)**, who edited the _Journal für praktische Chemie_. The Dutchman, he wrote, had found it easier "to mount Pegasus (evidently borrowed from the Veterinary School)" than to do exact chemistry, and had flown up Parnassus to see the atoms arranged in space. By about 1880, with Wislicenus and Viktor Meyer behind it, the tetrahedral carbon had won.

Van 't Hoff sold his idea with his hands as well as his pen. He made small cardboard tetrahedra himself and used them to persuade other chemists; a few survive, in Museum Boerhaave in Leiden and the Deutsches Museum in Munich, and the historian Trienke van der Spek calls them the first material models of a three-dimensional molecule. He and Le Bel shared the Royal Society's Davy Medal in 1893. In 1901 van 't Hoff received the first **[Nobel Prize in Chemistry](kloom:e/nobel-prize-in-chemistry)**, not for the tetrahedron but for his laws of chemical dynamics and of osmotic pressure in solutions.

The tetrahedron gave chemistry shapes, but not energies: why a reaction goes, and how far. The next frame is the American who answered that, Josiah Willard Gibbs.
