Uranium, element 92, was the end of the table, and the first claims to go past it were wrong. In 1934 **[Enrico Fermi](kloom:e/enrico-fermi)**'s group in Rome fired neutrons into uranium and concluded, cautiously, that they had made element 93, and his Nobel Prize of 1938 cited "new radioactive elements"; what they had mostly seen, it turned out a month later, was uranium splitting in two. The first **[transuranium element](kloom:e/transuranium-element)** came out of an experiment on that splitting.

## Element 93

In 1939 **[Edwin McMillan](kloom:e/edwin-mcmillan)**, a physicist at Berkeley, measured how far the fragments of fission fly by stacking cigarette papers on a thin layer of uranium oxide and exposing it to neutrons from the cyclotron. The fragments flew into the stack. Two activities stayed behind in the uranium: the 23-minute uranium-239, made when a nucleus simply swallows a neutron, and something new with a half-life of about two days. It should have been element 93. Emilio Segrè, who had found element 43 two years before and expected 93 to behave like rhenium, tested it, found it acting like a rare earth, and concluded that it was one of the many fission products.

McMillan went back to it in the spring of 1940. "In spite of what the Nobel Committee may think," he said in his Nobel lecture, "I am not a chemist." **Philip Abelson**, visiting from Washington, supplied the key: the new substance had two oxidation states, like uranium. Reduced, it came down with rare-earth fluorides; oxidized with bromate, it stayed in solution. It grew from the 23-minute uranium as that decayed, and it had nothing of rhenium in it. Their letter of June 1940 guessed that there might be "a second 'rare earth' group of similar elements starting with uranium". McMillan named it **[neptunium](kloom:e/neptunium)**, for the planet beyond Uranus.

## Element 94

McMillan left in November 1940 for war work on radar and let a young instructor in chemistry, **[Glenn Seaborg](kloom:e/glenn-t-seaborg)**, carry on with a graduate student, **Arthur Wahl**, and Joseph Kennedy. In December they bombarded uranium oxide with deuterons in the new 60-inch cyclotron, and alpha particles grew in the neptunium they separated. On the night of 23 February 1941 they found the trick that proved a new element, an isotope of mass 238: persulfate, with silver as a catalyst, oxidized the alpha-emitter to a state that no longer precipitated as a fluoride, which bromate could not do. Element 94 was harder to oxidize than 93. In the spring of 1942, needing symbols for a report, they called it **[plutonium](kloom:e/plutonium)**, after Pluto.

The isotope that mattered was the one neptunium-239 decays into. Its chain, with today's half-lives:

| Step       | Reaction           | Half-life of the product | As measured in 1940–41 |
| ---------- | ------------------ | -----------------------: | ---------------------: |
| capture    | ²³⁸U + n → ²³⁹U    |             23.5 minutes |             23 minutes |
| beta decay | ²³⁹U → ²³⁹Np + e⁻  |                2.36 days |               2.3 days |
| beta decay | ²³⁹Np → ²³⁹Pu + e⁻ |             24,110 years |     about 30,000 years |

Each beta decay turns a neutron into a proton, so the mass stays at 239 and the charge climbs 92, 93, 94. By our arithmetic, ten days after a bombardment 0.5^(10 ÷ 2.36), about 5%, of the neptunium is left, and nearly all the rest is plutonium. In May 1941 Kennedy, Seaborg, Segrè and Wahl took half a microgram of plutonium-239 made this way and found that slow neutrons split it more readily than uranium-235. The letters were held back until 1946.

## Micrograms

A bomb could be made of it, so plutonium had to be made by the kilogram in reactors and separated by chemistry, from a chemistry learned on invisible traces. At Chicago's **Metallurgical Laboratory**, Burris Cunningham and Louis Werner worked at the microgram scale, in capillary tubes, and on 10 September 1942 weighed the first plutonium compound: 2.77 micrograms of oxide.

That is a scale-up of about two thousand million, by our arithmetic, in three years. The process that did it at the **[Hanford Site](kloom:e/hanford-site)** used the two oxidation states again. Stanley Thompson found in December 1942 that bismuth phosphate carried 98% of plutonium out of solution when it was in the +4 state and left it behind in the +6, so plutonium could be precipitated with the carrier, oxidized, and left in solution while the carrier took the fission products down. The first plutonium reached Los Alamos in February 1945, and was in the core tested at Trinity in July. What it was for is told in physics' frame on the Manhattan Project, and the test and the danger of criticality in the Feynman subject.

## A new row

Elements 95 and 96 would not come. Seaborg's group assumed that they would oxidize like plutonium, and their separations failed until, in the summer of 1944, he decided that the elements from actinium on were a second series like the rare earths, filling an inner shell, with the +3 state growing more stable along the row. Treated as rare earths, curium and americium were identified within months, and were named after the Curies and the Americas as their rare-earth partners were named after Gadolin and Europe. The table gained a row beneath it, which the plate draws. Seaborg told later that colleagues warned him the idea would ruin his reputation.

McMillan and Seaborg shared the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1951. Berkeley went on to element 101, then fought Dubna over 102 to 105. Element 106, made in 1974, was named **[seaborgium](kloom:e/seaborgium)** in 1994; IUPAC then ruled that no element could be named for a living person, and gave way in 1997. Seaborg called it a greater honor than the Nobel Prize.

The superheavy elements beyond were made atom by atom: the last frame of this trail.
