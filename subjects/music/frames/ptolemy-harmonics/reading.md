Around AD 150, in **[Alexandria](kloom:e/alexandria)**, **[Claudius Ptolemy](kloom:e/ptolemy)** wrote a book in three parts on the mathematics of musical scales, the _Harmonics_. He is far better known for the **[Almagest](kloom:e/almagest)**, his astronomy, and the two books share a habit of mind: a model built from numbers is worth only what it predicts, and it has to be checked against something outside the head. For the planets that was the sky. For music it was the ear, working with an instrument. The _Harmonics_ opens with that argument, and in Andrew Barker's translation it is plain about why the ear needs help: "When we set out to use our ears to assess harmonic relations, there is needed to help them, just as there is for the eyes, some rational criterion working through appropriate instruments, as the ruler is needed to deal with straightness, and the compasses for the circle and the measurement of its parts."

## Two schools, both scolded

The instrument was the _kanōn_, the **[monochord](kloom:e/monochord)**: a string over a ruler with a bridge that slides along it. Ptolemy used it to fault both camps of Greek harmonic theory. The followers of **[Pythagoras](kloom:e/pythagoras)** trusted reason over hearing, and their rule that a concord must be a multiple, such as 2:1, or a ratio of the form (_n_+1):_n_ forced them to deny that an octave and a fourth together, 8:3, is a concord, although every ear hears it as one. The followers of **[Aristoxenus](kloom:e/aristoxenus)** trusted hearing and measured intervals by adding tones, so that a fourth was two and a half tones and an octave six. Ptolemy answered that six whole tones of 9:8, set out on the kanon, overshoot the octave, and that the ear can hear it.

Whether the instrument could really decide that is still argued. David Creese's history of the monochord (2010) takes the demonstration at Ptolemy's word. Stefan Hagel replied in 2012 that a kanon can only lay out what has already been computed: on a string of about 90 centimeters, the difference between a pure fifth and a tempered one moves the bridge by half a millimeter to a millimeter, finer than a hand and an ear could set.

Ptolemy did more than any writer before him to make the instrument good enough. Hagel lists his improvements: a single ruler of 120 parts, so that different scales could be compared in absolute terms; kanones with many strings, so that a whole scale could be played as a melody rather than one interval at a time; and a device he called the _helikon_, which used similar triangles to set the main ratios and a sliding common bridge to change key.

## Dividing the fourth

Greek scales were built from the **[tetrachord](kloom:e/tetrachord)**, four notes spanning a fourth, 4:3, whose two outer notes were fixed and two inner notes moved. Where the inner notes stood decided the scale's character, its _genus_. Ptolemy listed the divisions of his predecessors, Archytas, Eratosthenes and Didymus among them, and then gave his own, all but the old ditonic made only of ratios of the form (_n_+1):_n_. Here they are as John Chalmers tabulates them, rising from the lowest note:

| Ptolemy's genus  | Steps, rising        |
| ---------------- | -------------------- |
| Enharmonic       | 46:45 · 24:23 · 5:4  |
| Soft chromatic   | 28:27 · 15:14 · 6:5  |
| Tense chromatic  | 22:21 · 12:11 · 7:6  |
| Soft diatonic    | 21:20 · 10:9 · 8:7   |
| Tonic diatonic   | 28:27 · 8:7 · 9:8    |
| Ditonic diatonic | 256:243 · 9:8 · 9:8  |
| Tense diatonic   | 16:15 · 9:8 · 10:9   |
| Even diatonic    | 12:11 · 11:10 · 10:9 |

He did not pretend all were in use: the even diatonic, he wrote, sounded somewhat foreign and rustic. The one that mattered later is the **[tense diatonic](kloom:e/ptolemys-intense-diatonic-scale)**, also called the syntonic. Its two upper steps, 9:8 and 10:9, make together 90:72, a major third of exactly 5:4. The old ditonic division, the Pythagorean one, puts two tones of 9:8 there instead, a third of 81:64. The plate sets both on Ptolemy's ruler of 120 parts: the movable bridges stand at 112.5 and 100 for his scale and at 113.906 and 101.25 for the Pythagorean one. The two thirds differ by 81:80, by our arithmetic about a fifth of a semitone.

Sounded together, they are easy to tell apart. A third of 5:4 is still: its partials fall together. Built on a middle C of about 262 vibrations a second, an 81:64 third beats about sixteen times a second, by our arithmetic, a fast rattle in the sound. Ptolemy's tense diatonic is the major scale of **[just intonation](kloom:e/just-intonation)**, the tuning by small whole numbers that later theorists measured every compromise against.

## What the Middle Ages kept

**[Boethius](kloom:e/boethius)**, writing in Latin about 510, drew on the _Harmonics_ for his textbook on music, but the scale he handed to the medieval schools was the Pythagorean one, and medieval theory heard the third, 81:64 in that tuning, as a dissonance, unfit to end a piece on. Ptolemy's 5:4 came back when singers and keyboard players wanted thirds in their chords, in the sixteenth century.

The third book of the _Harmonics_ turns from instruments to the soul and the heavens, matching the parts of the soul and the motions of the planets to the musical system; Hagel describes its ecliptic as a vast curved monochord with the planets for its moving bridges. **[Johannes Kepler](kloom:e/johannes-kepler)** knew it well, and his [_Harmonices Mundi_](kloom:e/harmonice-mundi) of 1619, in which he found his third law of the planets, carries an appendix comparing his harmonies with Ptolemy's. How keyboard players got pure thirds back is the next step on the trail, meantone.
