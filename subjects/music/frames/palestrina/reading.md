Six voices sing the Credo of the **[_Missa Papae Marcelli_](kloom:e/missa-papae-marcelli)**, the _Pope Marcellus Mass_, but not all at once and not one after another. A group of them declaims a phrase of the creed together, in the same rhythm, so that each syllable is heard; a cadence rounds it off; a different group takes the next phrase, and so on to the end. Only on the longest syllables do the lines unfold into ornament. This is the mass that, by a story told for three centuries, saved church music from the **[Council of Trent](kloom:e/council-of-trent)**. Its composer was **[Giovanni Pierluigi da Palestrina](kloom:e/giovanni-pierluigi-da-palestrina)**, born in the town of Palestrina east of Rome in 1525 or 1526, and its first printing was in his second book of masses in 1567.

## A choirmaster's living

Palestrina spent most of his working life in Rome's great churches. Pope **[Julius III](kloom:e/pope-julius-iii)**, who had been bishop of Palestrina, made him choirmaster of the Cappella Giulia at **[St Peter's Basilica](kloom:e/st-peters-basilica)** in 1551, and in January 1555 put him among the singers of the papal chapel, the **[Sistine Chapel Choir](kloom:e/sistine-chapel-choir)**, which sang the pope's services under Michelangelo's ceiling. The singers objected: he was admitted without their entrance examination, and he was married, with four children, where the chapel's rule wanted clerics. Julius died that March, Pope **[Marcellus II](kloom:e/pope-marcellus-ii)** reigned for three weeks, and in July 1555 Paul IV dismissed Palestrina and two other married singers with small pensions.

## What the council said

The council that met at Trent, in its last sessions of 1562 and 1563, was the Catholic Church's answer to the Reformation, the **[Counter-Reformation](kloom:e/counter-reformation)** in council. Some bishops there complained that elaborate polyphony, its voices entering one after another on the same words, buried the text, and a preliminary canon drafted for the session of September 1562 stressed that the words must be understood. The decree the council passed on September 17 said far less. In J. Waterworth's translation of 1848, bishops "shall also banish from churches all those kinds of music, in which, whether by the organ, or in the singing, there is mixed up any thing lascivious or impure." Craig Monson showed in 2002 that the delegates "strived officially to say as little as possible about music"; the draft was never approved, and it was afterward, in their own dioceses, that reformers such as **[Charles Borromeo](kloom:e/charles-borromeo)**, cardinal and archbishop of Milan, pressed for words that could be heard.

The mass carries the name of Marcellus, and it was most likely composed by 1562, when it was copied into a manuscript at **[Santa Maria Maggiore](kloom:e/santa-maria-maggiore)**, where he was then choirmaster. The printed book of 1567, the _Missarum liber secundus_, was dedicated to **[Philip II of Spain](kloom:e/philip-ii-of-spain)**. Most of the mass is for six voices, seven in the last Agnus Dei. The Kyrie is imitative in the older way; the Gloria and Credo are the declaimed blocks that make the words plain.

## How the legend grew

In 1607 the composer **[Agostino Agazzari](kloom:e/agostino-agazzari)** wrote that music "would have come very near to being banished from the Holy Church by a sovereign pontiff," had Palestrina not shown with this mass that the fault lay with composers, not with music. In 1828 **[Giuseppe Baini](kloom:e/giuseppe-baini)**'s life of Palestrina gave the scene: the papal singers gathered before the cardinals at Cardinal Vitellozzi's house on April 28, 1565, three masses sung, the third astonishing everyone. A papal chapel diary records such a meeting, but not what was sung. The 1911 _Encyclopaedia Britannica_ still told Baini's story, while warning that many of its details were "legends"; the _Catholic Encyclopedia_ said the same year that Franz Xaver Haberl's work in the archives had "conclusively demolished these fictions." Hans Pfitzner's opera _Palestrina_ (1917) staged the legend anyway. Historians now find no evidence that the council meant to ban polyphony, or that this mass changed its mind. What is not in doubt is that Palestrina, like his contemporary Vincenzo Ruffo, chose to write in a simpler style that church officials wanted, one that let the words through.

He left about 105 masses and more than 300 motets. The papal choir kept singing him: about 1924 Victor issued the Sistine Chapel Choir under Monsignor Antonio Rella in his motet _Exultate Deo_ (1046-B), described as a male choir, unaccompanied.

## Learning his rules

His music became a model. In Vienna in 1725 **[Johann Joseph Fux](kloom:e/johann-joseph-fux)**, who had served three emperors as composer, published _Gradus ad Parnassum_, a course in **[counterpoint](kloom:e/counterpoint)** written as a dialogue between Josephus, the pupil, and Aloysius, the master: by Aloysius, Fux says in his preface, he means "that brightest light of music, Palestrina," to whom he owes everything he knows of the art (in our translation of the Latin). Its first exercise is the plate's: under a plain tune in the Dorian mode, the _cantus firmus_, the pupil writes one note against each note, using only consonances. Begin and end on a perfect consonance, the master says, use more imperfect ones (thirds and sixths) than perfect ones, move from an imperfect to a perfect consonance by contrary motion, and before the last note make a major sixth. Leopold Mozart is said to have taught his son from it, and Haydn worked through its exercises. The style it taught was more than a century old by then; music had meanwhile found another way to make words heard, in Monteverdi's _Orfeo_.
