Pluck a string and you hear one note. **[Hermann von Helmholtz](kloom:e/hermann-von-helmholtz)** set out to show that you are hearing several at once. Trained in medicine, he had stated the conservation of energy in 1847, and from 1858 he was professor of physiology at Heidelberg. In 1863, after working on it, he said, since 1854, he published _Die Lehre von den Tonempfindungen als physiologische Grundlage für die Theorie der Musik_: in English, **[_On the Sensations of Tone as a Physiological Basis for the Theory of Music_](kloom:e/sensations-of-tone)**. Its question was the oldest in this trail, why some intervals sound sweet and others harsh, and its answer came from the ear rather than from numbers.

## A sphere held to the ear

To hear the parts of a sound, Helmholtz built what are now called **[Helmholtz resonators](kloom:e/helmholtz-resonance)**: "hollow spheres of glass or metal, or tubes, with two openings," as [Alexander Ellis](kloom:e/alexander-john-ellis)'s translation describes them. One opening had sharp edges and faced the sound. The other was a small funnel that went into the ear, its end coated with sealing wax and pressed in while the wax was still soft so that it fitted "air-tight." The air in the sphere springs in and out through its neck at one pitch only. Stop the other ear, play a chord or speak, and the room goes dull; but when that one pitch is present, "it brays into the ear most powerfully." He heard his resonators answer in the whistling of the wind, the rattling of carriage wheels and the splashing of water. A graded set, one sphere for each note, like the metal set of about 1870 in the Hunterian Museum in Glasgow, let anyone, "even if he has no ear for music," pick out the faint tones inside a sound.

![Eight polished metal spheres of graded sizes on a white shelf, from one as big as a melon to one the size of a plum, each with a narrow nipple rising from its top](resonators.jpg)

What the spheres found was a ladder. A string vibrates as a whole, in halves, in thirds and so on, and each mode sounds at a whole-number multiple of the lowest, as the plate draws them. Helmholtz leaned on **[Joseph Fourier](kloom:e/joseph-fourier)**, who had proved, as he put it, that any "regular periodic form of vibration" can be built from simple vibrations at once, twice, three times the base frequency; Georg Ohm had proposed in 1843 that the ear makes that analysis itself, and Helmholtz tested and championed the idea, now **[Ohm's acoustic law](kloom:e/ohms-acoustic-law)**. Timbre is how loud each rung is. Helmholtz proved it by building vowels. Eight tuning forks, a low B♭ and its first seven partials, were kept vibrating by electromagnets, each in front of its own resonance chamber whose lid opened from a key. With the octave b♭ strong and the low B♭, f′ and d″ feebler, the apparatus said "a very fine O"; with the low B♭ strong and all the rest weak, a clear U.

## Rough and smooth

When two tones a little apart sound together they beat, swelling and fading as their waves fall in and out of step. Slow beats are a pulse. Faster, they become a rattle: Helmholtz found the semitone b′–c″, beating 33 times a second, "very jarring." Faster still, they blur back into smoothness.

That gave him his theory. Two notes are each a ladder of partials. Where rungs of one ladder land exactly on rungs of the other, nothing beats; where rungs land close but not together, they beat in the rough range, and the pair sounds dissonant. At the octave every partial of the upper note is a partial of the lower. At the fifth, 3:2, many coincide. At a whole tone, 9:8, almost every pair is near and none is together.

Helmholtz drew this. For a violin tone held at c′ and a second tone sliding up an octave and more, he summed the roughness of every pair of partials, with a rule he admitted was "somewhat arbitrary": none for no beats, most for about 33 a second, less beyond. His figure is a mountain range in which "only a few narrow valleys remain," deepest at the octave and the fifth, then the fourth, the major sixth and the major third, with the minor third and minor sixth only saddles. The chart is our own computation from his rule, with six partials and a softer peak than his, so its thirds are only shoulders.

## The ear as a piano

Inside the ear, Helmholtz proposed, sits the same apparatus. Raise the dampers of a piano and sing into it, and only the strings matching the partials of your voice sound back. Suppose, he wrote, "we were able to connect every string of a piano with a nervous fibre": that is how the **[cochlea](kloom:e/cochlea)** hears, its tiny structures each tuned to one pitch. In the twentieth century **[Georg von Békésy](kloom:e/georg-von-bekesy)**, watching the cochlea's membrane in specimens, saw not separate tuned strings but a wave running along it, peaking at a place set by the pitch; it won him the Nobel Prize in 1961, and the fine mechanics are still argued.

The book reached English through the phonetician Alexander Ellis, whose translation of 1875 was followed by a second edition in 1885 with long additions of his own. One of them, a new unit, is the subject of the next frame.
