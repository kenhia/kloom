On the evening of 2 November 1920, on the roof of a nine-story factory building at East Pittsburgh, a Westinghouse transmitter of 100 watts read out the returns of the Harding–Cox presidential election as the _Pittsburgh Post_ passed them along. The station signed itself 8ZZ, and within days **[KDKA](kloom:e/kdka-am)**. A local paper found the returns "exceptionally clear and distinct," and added: "Between announcements of the returns radiophone music was transmitted, which added much to the entertainment." Perhaps a thousand people heard it. At least three other stations read out returns that night, and the claim to have been first came later, but KDKA went on, from December every evening, and music became most of what the radio was for.

## Records on the air

The music was records, at first. In the garage behind his house in Wilkinsburg, the Westinghouse engineer **[Frank Conrad](kloom:e/frank-conrad)** had been playing phonograph records over his amateur station, 8XK, since 17 October 1919. When he ran through his own, a Brunswick record shop lent him new ones in return for being named on the air. In September 1920 the Joseph Horne department store advertised receiving sets "$10 upwards" and boasted of an "air concert" its set had picked up. Westinghouse's vice-president H. P. Davis saw that a station on the air every night would sell the company's radios.

There had been music on the air before. **[Lee de Forest](kloom:e/lee-de-forest)** broadcast the tenor Enrico Caruso from the stage of the Metropolitan Opera on 13 January 1910, but the tests showed the idea was not yet practical. In 1916 his station in the Bronx played Columbia records every night, naming the label with each. On 15 June 1920 **[Nellie Melba](kloom:e/nellie-melba)** sang into a microphone at the works of **[Guglielmo Marconi](kloom:e/guglielmo-marconi)**'s company in Chelmsford, England; listeners heard her trill and two arias, some of them as little more than scratches.

## A voice on a wave

Radio carries sound on the waves that **[Heinrich Hertz](kloom:e/heinrich-hertz)** first made in his laboratory in the 1880s. A station sends out a carrier, a wave too fast to hear, and in **[amplitude modulation](kloom:e/amplitude-modulation)** its strength is made to swell and shrink with the music, as the plate draws. The carrier on election night was 545 kilohertz (a wavelength of 550 meters); by our arithmetic, it rose and fell about 1,240 times in each cycle of the A a violin tunes to. A receiver throws away the carrier and keeps the outline. The cheapest, a **[crystal set](kloom:e/crystal-radio)** with a coil, a crystal and headphones, needed no battery, since the station's own signal drove the phones.

## Fessenden's Christmas Eve

Was there a broadcast of music fourteen years earlier? **[Reginald Fessenden](kloom:e/reginald-fessenden)**, who had developed this way of sending sound, wrote to a former colleague in January 1932 that on Christmas Eve 1906 his station at Brant Rock, Massachusetts, had played a phonograph record of Handel's "Largo"; that he played "O Holy Night" on the violin and sang; and that he read from Luke, "Glory to God in the highest." In 2006 the radio journalist James O'Neal searched for any trace of it from the time and found none: no newspaper story, no ship's log, nothing in a listener's log kept at Sayville, New York, and Fessenden himself had dated the broadcasting of speech and music to 1907 in 1925. The radio scientist Jack Belrose answered that there was "no doubt that it was made," and in 2008 the IEEE dedicated a plaque at Brant Rock to it. A demonstration for engineers on 21 December 1906 is documented, and Donna Halper and Christopher Sterling argue that, since it included a phonograph record, it was itself a broadcast of entertainment.

## The BBC and the records' slump

In Britain the Post Office chose to license a single broadcaster, the British Broadcasting Company, formed on 18 October 1922 and paid for by a license fee on receiving sets. **[John Reith](kloom:e/john-reith-1st-baron-reith)**, its general manager and then the first director-general of the **[BBC](kloom:e/bbc)** when it became a public corporation in 1927, meant it to carry, in his own phrase, "all that is best in every department of human knowledge, endeavour and achievement." (The motto "inform, educate, entertain" is credited to him, but there is no record from the time that he said it.) In 1927 the BBC took over the Promenade Concerts, **[the Proms](kloom:e/bbc-proms)**, and broadcast them.

Radio was free once the set was bought, and a live broadcast, through microphones and an amplified receiver, sounded clearer than any acoustic record. Victor, one of the largest record companies, sold 54.9 million records in 1921 and 3.1 million in 1932. How much of the fall was radio and how much the Depression is hard to separate. In 1929 Victor was sold to the **[Radio Corporation of America](kloom:e/rca-corporation)**. By April 1930 about 40 percent of American households had a radio.

Songwriters wanted their share. **[ASCAP](kloom:e/american-society-of-composers-authors-and-publishers)**, which licensed its members' songs for performance, raised its fees from radio so far through the 1930s (by 448 percent between 1931 and 1939 in one account, and 900 percent in another) that the broadcasters set up their own society, **[BMI](kloom:e/broadcast-music-inc)**, and for ten months of 1941, from 1 January to 29 October, played no ASCAP music at all on the networks. Stephen Foster's "Jeanie with the Light Brown Hair," out of copyright, was played so often that _Time_ said she was "widely reported to have turned grey."

The records fought back by borrowing the radio's own electronics: electrical recording.
