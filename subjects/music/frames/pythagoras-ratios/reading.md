Pluck a string, then stop it exactly halfway and pluck again: the second note is the first, an octave higher. Stop it two-thirds of the way along and you hear a fifth; three-quarters, a fourth. The octave, the fifth and the fourth are the ratios 2:1, 3:2 and 4:3. Greek tradition gave the discovery to **[Pythagoras](kloom:e/pythagoras)**, who grew up on the island of Samos and, at about forty, around 530 BC, moved to Croton in southern Italy, where he gathered followers. He wrote nothing, and no contemporary set down his teaching in any detail. In the historian Carl Huffman's words, the stories of how he found the ratios "are clearly false, since none of the techniques for the discovery ascribed to him would, in fact, work."

## What Pythagoras knew

The ratios reach us through his followers. The earliest writer on them whose words survive is **[Philolaus](kloom:e/philolaus)** of Croton (about 470 to 385 BC), said to be the first Pythagorean to publish a book. In a surviving fragment he calls the octave a _harmonia_, a fitting together, of a fourth and a fifth, and divides the fifth into three whole tones of 9:8 and a remainder of 256:243. He wanted the scale as a picture of the cosmos; nothing shows he tested it on an instrument.

How much was Pythagoras's own? Xenocrates, of the early Academy in Athens, said in the fourth century BC that Pythagoras "discovered that the intervals in music, too, do not arise in separation from number." Huffman reads that narrowly: Pythagoras knew the concords were whole-number ratios, perhaps from instrument makers, and used them to show how things are like numbers. The historian Leonid Zhmud reads it as a program of research. A riddle the Pythagoreans recited asked "What is the oracle at Delphi?" and answered "The tetraktys, which is the harmony in which the Sirens sing": the numbers 1, 2, 3 and 4, from which all three concords are built.

## The smithy

The story is first told by **[Nicomachus](kloom:e/nicomachus)** of Gerasa in his _Manual of Harmonics_, around AD 100, six centuries after Pythagoras. **[Iamblichus](kloom:e/iamblichus)** copied it into his life of Pythagoras, and Boethius passed it to the Latin Middle Ages. In Thomas Taylor's English of Iamblichus (1818): "as he was walking near a brazier's shop, he heard from a certain divine casualty the hammers beating out a piece of iron on an anvil, and producing sounds that accorded with each other, one combination only excepted." The hammers weighed 12, 9, 8 and 6 pounds. At home he hung four equal strings from a peg, tied the same weights to them, and heard the same concords: 12 against 6 an octave, 12 against 8 a fifth, 12 against 9 a fourth, and 9 against 8, the one discord, a whole tone.

![A woodcut in four panels: the biblical Jubal among smiths swinging hammers at an anvil; Pythagoras striking a row of numbered bells and glasses filled to different levels; Pythagoras at a board of strings with weights hanging from their ends; and Pythagoras and Philolaus blowing pipes of different lengths](gaffurio.jpg)

The numbers are right for lengths of string, and wrong for everything else. A hammer's pitch is not set by its weight, and a string's goes with the square root of the weight stretching it. Strings weighted 12 and 6 would sound, by our arithmetic, not an octave but a tritone, the interval halfway to it. The lutenist **[Vincenzo Galilei](kloom:e/vincenzo-galilei)**, Galileo's father, hung weights on strings and found that an octave needs four times the weight, not twice; he said in print in 1589 that the legend's weights were wrong. Marin Mersenne explained the error in 1636. Franchino Gaffurio's _Theorica musice_ of 1492 still shows Pythagoras at his weighted strings.

## The monochord

The instrument that does work is the **[monochord](kloom:e/monochord)**, the Greek _kanōn_: one string over a sounding board, a rule beneath it and a movable bridge, as in the plate. Set the bridge at 6 of 12 marks and each half sounds the octave; at 8, the longer part sounds the fifth. Walter Burkert dated the monochord after the time of **[Aristotle](kloom:e/aristotle)**, who seems not to have known it, two centuries after Pythagoras. Zhmud thinks Pythagoras may have used one.

## The music of the spheres

In the [_Timaeus_](kloom:e/timaeus-dialogue), written about 360 BC, **[Plato](kloom:e/plato)** has the maker of the world divide the world's soul by the numbers 1, 2, 3, 4, 9, 8 and 27 and fill the gaps with fourths, fifths and tones of 9:8, leaving Philolaus's remainder, 256:243. The myth closing his [_Republic_](kloom:e/republic-plato) puts a Siren on each of eight circles of the heavens, "hymning a single tone or note," and "the eight together form one harmony." Aristotle reported the Pythagorean answer to why we never hear this **[music of the spheres](kloom:e/musica-universalis)**: we have heard it since birth, as "coppersmiths, who are so accustomed to the noise of the smithy that it makes no difference to them." He thought it untrue. Johannes Kepler would hunt for it in the planets' speeds in 1619.

## Twelve fifths

Climb twelve pure fifths from C, through every note of the keyboard, and you come out at a B-sharp seven octaves up. But (3:2)¹² is not 2⁷: the fifths overshoot by 531,441:524,288, the **[Pythagorean comma](kloom:e/pythagorean-comma)**, about a quarter of a semitone (23.5 cents, a unit invented in 1885). Sounded with a middle C of about 262 vibrations a second, that B-sharp, brought down to the same octave, beats against it about three and a half times a second, by our arithmetic. No ladder of pure fifths closes the circle; where to hide the comma is the story of the Tuning trail.
