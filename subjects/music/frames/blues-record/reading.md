On or about 10 August 1920, in the Okeh studio on West 45th Street in New York, **[Mamie Smith](kloom:e/mamie-smith)** sang **"[Crazy Blues](kloom:e/crazy-blues)"** into the recording horn, backed by a band of Black musicians that its composer, **[Perry Bradford](kloom:e/perry-bradford)**, had named the Jazz Hounds. The band in the photograph on the sheet music is a cornet, a trombone, a clarinet and a violin with a piano, and Smith, in the record historian Allan Sutton's words, "shouted her way" over them: "I can't sleep at night," "I can't eat a bite," and near the end a threat to "get myself a gun and shoot myself a cop." **[Okeh Records](kloom:e/okeh-records)** issued it that autumn as Okeh 4169 (in October, by the trade papers; in November, says the librarian Ed Komara). It was the first hit **[blues](kloom:e/blues)** record by a Black singer.

## A song from the Delta

The blues grew in the South among the first generations of Black Americans born after the Thirteenth Amendment ended slavery in December 1865. The composer and bandleader **[W. C. Handy](kloom:e/w-c-handy)** said he first heard it as a composer would one night at Tutwiler, Mississippi, in the years after he moved to the Delta in 1903. In his telling, in his autobiography of 1941, a ragged man waiting for the same delayed train played a guitar, pressing a knife on its strings, and sang one line three times, "Goin' where the Southern cross' the Dog," to "the weirdest music I had ever heard." He sold "The Memphis Blues" (1912) outright for $100, and kept the rights to **"[St. Louis Blues](kloom:e/saint-louis-blues-song)"**, finished in September 1914. For its words he took the folk singers' three-line verse, he wrote, but instead of saying one thing three times he chose "making a statement, repeating the statement in the second line, and then telling in the third line why the statement was made."

## Twelve bars

That verse, AAB, fits the **[twelve-bar blues](kloom:e/twelve-bar-blues)**, a frame Handy described as three chords, "tonic, subdominant, dominant seventh," a form already in use "from Missouri to the Gulf." Each line takes four bars: the voice sings for about two and a half, and the band answers in the gap.

| Bars | Line | Chords, a common form | Handy's verse, 1914                   |
| ---- | ---- | --------------------- | ------------------------------------- |
| 1–4  | A    | I · I · I · I7        | I hate to see de eve'nin sun go down  |
| 5–8  | A    | IV · IV · I · I       | Hate to see de eve'nin sun go down    |
| 9–12 | B    | V7 · V7 · I · I       | 'Cause my baby, he done lef' dis town |

Players vary the chords, above all in the last line.

Over these major chords the singer bears down on the third and seventh notes of the scale, "slurring between major and minor," Handy wrote. He tried to catch the slur in print with flat thirds and sevenths, "now called 'blue notes'." A piano can play only one or the other; a voice, or a string under a knife, bends between them.

"Crazy Blues" is only partly built this way. Bradford wrote it for the stage, from a tune he had already sold to other publishers as "Harlem Blues", and Komara counts its six choruses as sixteen bars, twelve, sixteen, twelve, twelve and sixteen. Sutton calls it a "blues-inflected pop song."

## A market kept apart

Victor tested her in January 1920 and passed. Okeh's recording director, Fred Hager, who had received warnings from whites against recording Black musicians, agreed to a session in February, with a band that Sutton, from photographs, shows was Okeh's white studio orchestra (Komara thought it uncertain). How well the August record sold is not known. Bradford's autobiography of 1965 is the source of the figures usually repeated, 75,000 copies in its first month and a million within seven months; Sutton calls them "highly doubtful," since Okeh's sales records are lost, and Komara writes "reportedly" 75,000 in two months. By January 1921 Okeh had cut its records to 75 cents, but kept Smith's at a dollar.

Okeh advertised her in Black newspapers as "Our Race Artist," and the trade made a category of it: the **[race record](kloom:e/race-record)**, issued in a series numbered apart from the white catalog. Okeh's ran from 1921, Columbia followed that year and **[Paramount Records](kloom:e/paramount-records)**, a Wisconsin furniture company's label, in 1922. In 1921 Harry Pace, Handy's former partner, had launched **[Black Swan Records](kloom:e/black-swan-records)** in Harlem, the first widely distributed label owned and run by African Americans; it was bankrupt by the end of 1923, and Paramount bought it in 1924.

## Who was paid

**[Bessie Smith](kloom:e/bessie-smith)**'s first record for Columbia, "Downhearted Blues" of February 1923, is said to have sold 780,000 copies in six months. **[Ma Rainey](kloom:e/ma-rainey)** made more than a hundred sides for Paramount from December 1923. Then came men singing to their own guitars: **[Blind Lemon Jefferson](kloom:e/blind-lemon-jefferson)**, taken from Dallas to Chicago to record at the turn of 1926, and **[Charley Patton](kloom:e/charley-patton)**, sent north from Mississippi by a Jackson store owner to record "Pony Blues" for Paramount at a studio in Richmond, Indiana, in June 1929. These were studio sessions in the North, though Okeh's portable outfit recorded in Atlanta and New Orleans from 1923.

The money ran one way. Handy wrote that "Crazy Blues" earned Bradford, as its composer, $53,000 in phonograph royalties. Singers were usually paid a flat fee for each side and no royalty at all. The University of Wisconsin's history project Wisconsin 101, drawing on the historian Stephen Calt, finds that nine of every ten of Paramount's artists never received one, and that the label often had them sign away the copyrights in their songs. Mamie Smith died in New York in 1946, poor enough to be buried without a stone. In Chicago, the same Okeh race series was recording the trumpeter who would make the solo the heart of jazz.
