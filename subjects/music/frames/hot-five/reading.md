On 28 June 1928, in [Okeh](kloom:e/okeh-records)'s studio in [Chicago](kloom:e/chicago), **[Louis Armstrong](kloom:e/louis-armstrong)** began by playing alone: a fanfare of four even notes, a syncopated push, a run up in triplets, then a long fall in double time across two octaves, ending on a hushed, shaking note. Only then did the band come in, on one held chord. The tune was **"[West End Blues](kloom:e/west-end-blues)"**, a twelve-bar blues by Armstrong's old teacher **[King Oliver](kloom:e/king-oliver)**, who had recorded it himself seventeen days before. Armstrong's version, issued as Okeh 8597, is often heard as the moment **[jazz](kloom:e/jazz)** became an art of the soloist.

## North from New Orleans

Jazz grew in **[New Orleans](kloom:e/new-orleans)** around 1900, and it went north with the **[Great Migration](kloom:e/great-migration-african-american)**, about 1.6 million Black Southerners who moved to Northern cities between 1910 and 1940. Chicago's Black population rose from 44,103 in 1910 to 109,594 in 1920, an increase of 148 percent, by the count of the city's commission on the riot of 1919. Oliver moved there in 1918, and in 1922 he sent for Armstrong to play second cornet in his Creole Jazz Band at the Lincoln Gardens on the South Side.

The first jazz on record was not theirs. On 26 February 1917 the **[Original Dixieland Jass Band](kloom:e/original-dixieland-jass-band)**, five white musicians from New Orleans, recorded "[Livery Stable Blues](kloom:e/livery-stable-blues)" for Victor; the record is often said to have sold a million, though Victor's own cards show some 250,983 copies made. Oliver's band reached a studio only in 1923, at **[Gennett Records](kloom:e/gennett-records)** in Richmond, Indiana, where Armstrong's horn was so loud that he was put about fifteen feet from the recording horn. Gennett paid some artists a flat $15 to $50 a session, Black artists less, and promised most a penny a record sold. On 5 October 1923, the day of their second session there, more than 6,000 members of the Ku Klux Klan paraded through Richmond.

## The Hot Five

The pianist in Oliver's band was **[Lil Hardin](kloom:e/lil-hardin-armstrong)**, from Memphis, trained at Fisk University and a fluent sight-reader. She married Armstrong in 1924, urged him out of Oliver's shadow, and in 1925 brought him back from New York to a job at the Dreamland at $75 a week, billed as "The World's Greatest Jazz Cornetist." On 12 November 1925 he made his first records under his own name for Okeh, as **[Louis Armstrong and His Hot Five](kloom:e/louis-armstrong-and-his-hot-five)**: Lil Hardin Armstrong on piano, Kid Ory, Johnny Dodds and Johnny St. Cyr. Louis and Lil each brought a tune (hers was "My Heart"; she would later write "Struttin' with Some Barbecue" for the band) and sold both to the producer E. A. Fearn for a flat $50, with no royalties; Okeh paid flat fees for the sessions too, and Armstrong was first paid royalties on these sides in the 1950s, when Columbia reissued them.

The Hot Five was a studio band. Its members played together in other groups, but as the Hot Five they appeared in public only at two events Okeh organized. On "Heebie Jeebies" (February 1926) Armstrong sang a chorus in nonsense syllables. In his telling, the lyric sheet fell to the floor and he kept going; his **[scat singing](kloom:e/scat-singing)** was not the first on record (Gene Greene scatted in 1911), but it was the one others copied, and he said the record sold forty thousand in a few weeks.

## Five choruses, heard in order

By 1928 the band was new around him, with **[Earl Hines](kloom:e/earl-hines)** on piano. The music is in E-flat; the bars are a transcription's.

| Bars  | What is heard                                                                                              |
| ----- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1–6   | Trumpet alone, the cadenza; the band's chord, held                                                         |
| 7–18  | Trumpet plays Oliver's melody, bending it, over held clarinet and trombone and the rhythm section's chords |
| 19–30 | Fred Robinson's trombone, sliding into its notes; the drummer's clicking cymbal comes in                   |
| 31–42 | Jimmy Strong's clarinet, low and plain, answered phrase by phrase by Armstrong's scat                      |
| 43–54 | Hines alone: runs over two octaves, then octaves struck like a trumpet's                                   |
| 55–62 | Trumpet holds a high B-flat for four bars, then tumbles down from it                                       |
| 63–68 | Hines's falling chords, one last trumpet phrase, the band's chord, a cymbal's click                        |

The plate draws the same order, part by part. The old New Orleans way had three horns improvising together; here each takes the center in turn, and the trumpet frames the whole.

Lil Hardin Armstrong later said the cadenza grew out of the cornet exercise books she and Louis had practiced together. The critic Gunther Schuller, in _Early Jazz_ (1968), heard in it notice that jazz could stand beside any music. Okeh put the record in its race series, at 75 cents, and the advertisement says so; that year it also began issuing Armstrong in its popular series for white buyers. The next section goes back to Vienna, to Arnold Schoenberg's twelve tones.
