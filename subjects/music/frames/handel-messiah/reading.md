On the morning of Tuesday 13 April 1742, _Faulkner's Journal_ printed a request from the stewards of a charitable society: "the Favour of the ladies not to come with hoops this day to the Musick Hall in Fishamble Street. The Gentlemen are desired to come without their swords." Room saved meant more tickets, and more money for charity. At noon **[George Frideric Handel](kloom:e/george-frideric-handel)** gave the first performance of **[_Messiah_](kloom:e/messiah-handel)**, and the paper reported "above 700 People in the Room".

## Opera's collapse and the English oratorio

Handel was born in Halle in 1685, settled in **[London](kloom:e/london)** in 1712 and became a British subject in 1727. He made his name there with Italian opera, more than forty of them, but by the early 1730s the public was tiring of it: English ballad operas mocked it, takings fell, and a rival company competed for the patrons who paid for it. Handel spent large sums of his own keeping his going, and gave his last Italian opera in 1741.

What he turned to was the English **[oratorio](kloom:e/oratorio)**: a large work for soloists, chorus and orchestra on a sacred story, sung in a theater or a hall without costumes or staging. _Messiah_ was his sixth. Its words were compiled by **[Charles Jennens](kloom:e/charles-jennens)**, a wealthy landowner who in July 1741 wrote to a friend that he hoped Handel would "lay out his whole Genius & Skill upon it ... The Subject is Messiah". Jennens took them all from the **[King James Version](kloom:e/king-james-version)** of the Bible and from the Psalms of the Book of Common Prayer. There are no characters and no plot: Part I gives the prophecies and the birth of Christ, Part II his suffering, death and resurrection, Part III the promise of the resurrection of the dead. Handel began the music on 22 August and finished it on 14 September, twenty-four days.

## Dublin, for charity

He took it to **[Dublin](kloom:e/dublin)**, where he had arrived in November 1741 at the invitation of the Lord Lieutenant, and where a new music hall had been built that year in Fishamble Street for a society that freed prisoners held for debt. The singers were sixteen men and sixteen boys from the choirs of St Patrick's and Christ Church cathedrals, with two women soloists, the soprano Christina Maria Avoglio and the actress **[Susannah Cibber](kloom:e/susannah-maria-cibber)**, a contralto, for whom Handel transposed two numbers down.

The takings came to about £400, about £127 to each of three charities: the society for relieving prisoners, the Charitable Infirmary and Mercer's Hospital. Handel and the singers took nothing. The society later reported releasing 142 debtors in 1742; accounts that credit them all to this one performance miss that it was the society's figure for the year.

## The shape of the Hallelujah

Part II ends with the chorus everyone knows, in D major, with trumpets and, for the first time in the work, timpani. The voices begin with "Hallelujah" alone, all together in chords, on a short figure that turns on a single step. "For the Lord God omnipotent reigneth" comes first in unison, all four parts on one line, and then in imitation, with the Hallelujahs breaking in. "The kingdom of this world" is sung plainly, chord by chord, like a hymn; "and he shall reign for ever and ever" starts as a fugue on a leaping theme. At "King of Kings, and Lord of Lords" the sopranos hold one note while the others call "for ever and ever" and "Hallelujah" beneath them, each time higher in the sopranos and trumpets, until a full silence and a last, slow Hallelujah. Victor's Oratorio Chorus recorded it under Josef Pasternack in 1918.

Audiences stand for it because **[George II](kloom:e/george-ii-of-great-britain)** is said to have risen at the London premiere in March 1743; there is no good evidence the king was there, and the first mention of the audience standing is in a letter of 1756.

## The Foundling Hospital and the crowds

London was cool to _Messiah_ at first, some thinking a theater no place for Scripture. Charity made it. The **[Foundling Hospital](kloom:e/foundling-hospital)**, founded by Thomas Coram in 1739 for abandoned infants, had Handel give a benefit concert in 1749, and from 1750 he conducted _Messiah_ in its chapel every year. By the account of **[Charles Burney](kloom:e/charles-burney)**, Handel's eleven performances to 1759 raised £6,935 for the hospital, and those by his successors to 1777 brought the total to £10,299. Handel left the hospital a fair copy of the score. Its performance of 1754 is the first whose forces are known: thirty-eight players, by our count of the list, and a chorus of nineteen, six of them boys from the Chapel Royal.

After his death the work grew. In 1784, a quarter-century after his death and, as his birth was then reckoned, a century after it, a Commemoration in **[Westminster Abbey](kloom:e/westminster-abbey)** put 525 singers and players under one conductor, and Burney recorded receipts of £12,736 for its five concerts. In Vienna in 1789 **[Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart](kloom:e/wolfgang-amadeus-mozart)** reorchestrated _Messiah_ in German for Baron van Swieten, adding flutes, clarinets, trombones and horns. At **[the Crystal Palace](kloom:e/the-crystal-palace)** in 1857 the chorus numbered 2,000 and the orchestra 500, and on 29 June 1888 an agent of Thomas Edison recorded a chorus from Handel's _Israel in Egypt_ there on a wax cylinder, its note reading "A chorus of 4000 voices recorded with phonograph over 100 yards away." Until 2009 it was the oldest recording of music known to survive. The next section opens in Mozart's Vienna.
