In 476 Odoacer deposed the last emperor of the West, and from 493 Italy was ruled by the Ostrogothic king **[Theodoric the Great](kloom:e/theodoric-the-great)**, who kept the Roman senate. One of its senators wrote the book through which medieval Europe learned what the Greeks since **[Pythagoras](kloom:e/pythagoras)** had worked out about music. **[Boethius](kloom:e/boethius)**, born about the time of Odoacer's coup, read Greek, rare in the Latin West by then, and set out to put its mathematical arts into Latin: arithmetic, music, geometry and astronomy, the four ways he named the **[quadrivium](kloom:e/quadrivium)**. His _Arithmetic_ came first, a loose Latin version of the _Introduction to Arithmetic_ by **[Nicomachus](kloom:e/nicomachus)**, which carries the sieve of Eratosthenes into Latin as _cribrum_. The five books of _De institutione musica_, the _Fundamentals of Music_, followed, about 510, drawn from Nicomachus and **[Ptolemy](kloom:e/ptolemy)**.

In a letter that **[Cassiodorus](kloom:e/cassiodorus)** drafted for him, probably in 507, Theodoric asks Boethius to have a water clock and a sundial made for the king of the Burgundians, and reminds him, in Thomas Hodgkin's English of 1886: "You have translated Pythagoras the musician, Ptolemy the astronomer, Nicomachus the arithmetician, Euclid the geometer." In another, **[Clovis I](kloom:e/clovis-i)**, king of the Franks, has asked for a harper, and Theodoric turns to Boethius, "being such a lover of music yourself," to choose him.

## Three kinds of music

Music, Boethius says in his first chapter, "is so naturally joined to us that we could not be without it even if we wished" (our translation, as are those that follow, from Gottfried Friedlein's Latin edition of 1867). Then he sorts it into three kinds. _Musica mundana_, the music of the world, is the order of the heavens, the elements and the seasons: "How could so swift a machine of the heavens move in a silent, noiseless course?" It is the Pythagoreans' **[music of the spheres](kloom:e/musica-universalis)**, and we cannot hear it. _Musica humana_ is the attunement of body and soul, which "whoever goes down into himself understands." Only the third sounds: the music "set in certain instruments, such as the cithara or the pipes."

What we hear, he defines. A consonance is "a mixture of high and low sound falling sweetly and evenly on the ears"; a dissonance is "a harsh and unpleasant blow" of two sounds that refuse to mix, each striving to reach the ear whole. Stop a string at its middle and sound half against whole: the octave, as Book Five reports Ptolemy saying, "is almost a single note." Two notes a whole tone apart rub.

He ranks those who make music, too. The player takes his name from his instrument, the _citharoedus_ from the cithara, and serves; the poet makes songs "by a certain natural instinct"; only one who can judge modes, rhythms and songs by reason deserves the name. "He is a musician," Book One ends, "who has taken up the knowledge of singing after weighing it by reason, not through the servitude of work but by the command of speculation." Roman patricians looked down on paid performers, and Nicomachus had held the same view.

## Where the Greek came from

Since a study of 1898 most scholars have agreed that the first four books follow Nicomachus and the fifth Ptolemy's _Harmonics_. Nicomachus's surviving _Manual_ promises a longer _Introduction to Music_, now lost; Boethius credits him with ideas the _Manual_ lacks, so he seems to have had it, and it survives only in his Latin. Book Four is the hard case, and two scholars, Ubaldo Pizzani and Calvin Bower, read it differently:

| Book     | What it teaches                             | Source, after Bower (1978)                      | Pizzani (1965)                  |
| -------- | ------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------- |
| I–III    | three musics; consonance; ratios; the comma | Nicomachus's lost _Introduction_                | the same                        |
| IV.1–2   | intervals as ratios of number               | Euclid's _Section of the Canon_, via Nicomachus | Euclid, directly                |
| IV.3–4   | Greek and Latin note signs                  | a lost Greek source                             | Gaudentius, in Mutianus's Latin |
| IV.5–13  | dividing the monochord in three genera      | Nicomachus                                      | an unknown source               |
| IV.14–18 | the modes; testing consonances by ear       | Ptolemy, through Nicomachus                     | Ptolemy                         |
| V        | Ptolemy against the Pythagoreans            | Ptolemy's _Harmonics_, Book 1                   | the same                        |

Book One retells, after Nicomachus, how Pythagoras heard hammers of 12, 9, 8 and 6 weights ring in a smithy (the frame on Pythagoras tells it, and why it is false). Two of the manuscripts Friedlein collated add a figure there: the four numbers at the corners of a square, each side and diagonal named for the consonance between its ends. The left side, 12 to 6, is the octave; the diagonals, 12 to 8 and 9 to 6, the fifth; top and bottom, 12 to 9 and 8 to 6, the fourth; and the right side, 9 to 8, the tone. That figure is the plate. Book Four turns the numbers into a **[monochord](kloom:e/monochord)**, "the division of the rule, to which the whole intention tends."

## A thousand years of school

In 523 Boethius, then master of offices at Theodoric's court, spoke up for a senator accused of treason and was accused himself. In prison he wrote **[_The Consolation of Philosophy_](kloom:e/on-the-consolation-of-philosophy)**, and he was executed, in 524 by most accounts, though the _Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy_ says probably 526. His music book lasted. Cassiodorus digested Boethius's handbooks in his own _Institutions_, and about 140 medieval copies survive, among them the twelfth-century book from Christ Church, Canterbury, now in Cambridge, whose miniature shows Boethius with **[Plato](kloom:e/plato)**, Pythagoras and Nicomachus. In the universities, Paris and Oxford above all, it was a set text: Jean des Murs recast it in 1323 in a treatise widely studied in the later Middle Ages, and at the **[University of Oxford](kloom:e/university-of-oxford)**, Anthony Wood wrote in the seventeenth century, a bachelor of music was one admitted to lecture on any of Boethius's musical books; Bower wrote that it was still an Oxford textbook in the eighteenth century. It was printed at Venice in 1491–92.

What Boethius left the Middle Ages was a theory of sounding numbers, not a way to write down a tune; that would come with the neumes.
