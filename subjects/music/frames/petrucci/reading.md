On 25 May 1498 a printer from Fossombrone named **[Ottaviano Petrucci](kloom:e/ottaviano-petrucci)** won from the Signory of **[Venice](kloom:e/venice)** the sole right, for twenty years, to print "figured music", music in several measured parts, and tablature for organ and lute. Three years later his shop issued the **[Harmonice Musices Odhecaton A](kloom:e/harmonice-musices-odhecaton)**, the "hundred songs of harmonic music". It held 96. It was the first book of part-music ever printed from movable type. In his dedication, dated 15 May 1501, Petrucci wrote (in our translation) that printers brought out something new every day, yet left measured music lying neglected, music "without which we neither win the favor of God, nor celebrate weddings, nor feasts", because able men, "beaten by the difficulty", had so often given up.

## Why music was hard to print

Words are a line of letters. Music is a grid: five lines that must run unbroken across the page, note heads that must sit on exactly the right line or space, and syllables that must fall under the right notes. Half a century after **[Johannes Gutenberg](kloom:e/johannes-gutenberg)**'s **[printing press](kloom:e/printing-press)**, printers had found ways round the problem without solving it. The **[Mainz Psalter](kloom:e/mainz-psalter)** of 1457, printed by Gutenberg's backer **[Johann Fust](kloom:e/johann-fust)** and his former assistant Peter Schöffer, printed its texts and left its chant to be written in by hand. Service books printed chant from about 1473, some of it cut in woodblocks, and **[Ulrich Han](kloom:e/ulrich-han)**'s _Missale Romanum_, printed in Rome in 1476, set it in type, the first book in Italy with printed music. Chant needs a handful of note shapes. Polyphony, with its many note values and rests, needs dozens, and every voice had to be read by its own singer.

So Petrucci's claim, in his petition, to be its inventor was a stretch. The musicologist Stanley Boorman, whose catalog of Petrucci's work runs to 1,285 pages, points out that Venice had already seen "over fifteen years of successful liturgical music printing" by comparable methods. What was new was doing it for intricate part-music, cleanly, and in quantity.

## Three passes through the press

Each sheet went through the press three times, and each time the type had to land in the same place on the paper. Boorman's study of the books themselves, as Sean Kisch summarizes it, gives the order below; Wikipedia's article on the _Odhecaton_ puts the words before the notes, and an 1880 dictionary saw only two passes.

| Pass | What was printed                                    | What had to line up                                     |
| ---- | --------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1    | The staves, long unbroken lines of five             | Nothing yet: this pass sets the grid                    |
| 2    | Notes, rests, clefs and signs, each on a small body | Every note head on its line or space                    |
| 3    | Words, initials, composer's names and page numbers  | Each syllable under its note, the titles over the staff |

Setting the notes was the hard part. Each note had to stand at its pitch, so the compositor packed small spacing pieces above and below it, and the tails of the notes were cut to overhang their bodies so that flagged notes spaced evenly. The metal was, by Boorman's reading, the alloy hardened with "fused marcasite of antimony" that let the thin strokes of clefs, flats and ledger lines come out fine. The plate draws the three passes as layers of one stave, first apart and then registered. In the third edition, the book's third leaf is six staves printed with nothing on them, the first pass alone. By 1503 Petrucci had cut the passes to two, printing the staves and the words together.

Who cut the type is disputed. Wikipedia's article credits the punchcutters **[Francesco Griffo](kloom:e/francesco-griffo)** and Jacomo Ungaro, both then in Venice; Kisch notes that nothing shows Petrucci buying type, and that he is assumed to have cut his own.

## A hundred songs, and the market

The music came from the North. Petrucci dedicated the book to a Venetian nobleman, Girolamo Donato, and a letter beside his, from the humanist Bartolomeo Budrio, credits the "hundred songs", cleaned up "by his work and diligence", to **Petrus Castellanus**, a Dominican friar who directed the music at Santi Giovanni e Paolo. The songs were French chansons for three and four voices by Ockeghem, **[Josquin des Prez](kloom:e/josquin-des-prez)**, Busnois, Agricola, Obrecht and others, many of them anonymous, and the volume opens with an _Ave Maria_ by Marbrianus de Orto. Most were printed without their words: the two upper parts on the left page and the two lower on the right, so that four singers or players could read from one opening. A record of 1969 by the New York Pro Musica, _Petrucci, First Printer of Music_, opens with songs from it, among them _La alfonsina_ and the nonsense-syllable song _Latura tu_.

Petrucci printed new books and new editions every few months, 61 known in all, a third edition of the _Odhecaton_ dated 25 May 1504 among them; the page here is from it. About 1511, with Venice at war, he left the Venetian business to two partners and went home to Fossombrone, where he went on printing under a privilege from Pope Leo X.

## One pass

In Paris in 1528 **[Pierre Attaingnant](kloom:e/pierre-attaingnant)** published a book of chansons in which each piece of type carried a note together with its own short stretch of staff, so that staves, notes and words went through the press once. The staff lines came out wavy where the pieces met, and the music came out cheaply, in a third of the presswork. Others credit the method to John Rastell in London about 1520, or to printers in Vienna or Augsburg, but a French court case of the eighteenth century still named Attaingnant as its inventor, and single impression ruled music printing for some two hundred years. Petrucci's other great gift to the market was a composer: in 1502 he gave a whole book of masses to one man, Josquin.
