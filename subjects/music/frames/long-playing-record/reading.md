At a press showing at the Waldorf-Astoria in New York in June 1948, Edward Wallerstein, the president of **[Columbia Records](kloom:e/columbia-records)**, stood between two stacks of records. One, of ordinary 78s, stood about eight feet high; the other, about fifteen inches, held the same music on a new kind of disc. He played one of the 78s to its end, about four minutes, where it stopped, as he remembered it, "right in the middle of a movement." Then he played the same music on the new record on a small Philco player and went straight past the break. The new record was the **[LP](kloom:e/lp-record)**, the long-playing record, and its name was Columbia's trademark.

## Music in four-minute pieces

A twelve-inch 78 held under five minutes a side and a ten-inch about three and a half, so longer music came cut into pieces. George Gershwin's _Rhapsody in Blue_, recorded in 1924, filled both sides of one disc in shortened form, and a symphony's movements were broken into sides of four minutes and twenty seconds or less. Odeon of Germany had sold Tchaikovsky's _Nutcracker Suite_ in 1909 on four double-sided discs in one package, and such a set of discs took its name from the photograph album. The listener got up every few minutes to turn or change a record, and heard the music stop each time.

## A finer groove

Columbia began work on a longer record in 1939, the year it came under the radio network **[CBS](kloom:e/cbs)**, stopped for the war and took it up again in 1945. The LP turned at 33⅓ revolutions a minute, the speed of radio transcription discs, and packed its groove much closer. A report of October 1948 gave 224 to 300 grooves to the inch, against about 90 on a 78, with a stylus tip of one thousandth of an inch, a third of the old one, tracking at a fifth of an ounce. That fine groove was pressed in vinyl, **[polyvinyl chloride](kloom:e/polyvinyl-chloride)**, instead of the abrasive shellac compound of the 78, and its quiet surface let the music be cut softer. The engineers' own paper of 1949 put the reason plainly: once an amplifier stood between groove and loudspeaker, the groove no longer had to drive the sound, so it could be made finer.

Wallerstein had timed the classical repertory and asked for seventeen minutes a side, enough to put about 90 percent of it on one record; by the launch the team had about twenty-two. The first twelve-inch LP, Columbia ML 4001, was Mendelssohn's Violin Concerto with Nathan Milstein and the New York Philharmonic under Bruno Walter, recorded at Carnegie Hall in 1945. It is music written not to stop. The violin enters almost at once; at the end of the first movement a single bassoon holds its B through the final chord and steps up a semitone to C, and the slow movement has begun. On 78s a side could end there; on the LP the bassoon simply goes on. Many of the first LPs were copied from sixteen-inch lacquer discs that Columbia had cut beside its 78s since 1939, engineers fading one disc into the next by hand; about 40 percent, Wallerstein recalled, came from tape.

## Who made it

The LP is usually credited to **[Peter Goldmark](kloom:e/peter-carl-goldmark)**, who directed research at CBS and led the team. Wallerstein, in an account published after his death, wrote that Goldmark "didn't actually do any of the work," and gave the credit to William Bachman, Bill Savory, Jim Hunter, Vin Liebler and Ike Rodman. Goldmark's memoir of 1973 says the opposite: that Wallerstein discouraged the project from the start. CBS had spent $250,000 on it.

## The war of the speeds

In February 1949 **[RCA Victor](kloom:e/rca-corporation)** answered with the 45, a seven-inch disc with a large center hole, for a changer that stacked them. Wallerstein called it "fine for short pops numbers but no good at all for classics." Wallerstein put RCA's losses from June 1948 to January 1950, when it began to make LPs too, at $4.5 million. The industry divided the work: the LP for albums, the 45 for singles. By 1958 the LP made up 58 percent of American record sales by value, and the 78 one percent.

## Two channels in one groove

On 14 December 1931 the [EMI](kloom:e/emi) engineer **[Alan Blumlein](kloom:e/alan-blumlein)** applied for a British patent on "binaural" sound, which said that two channels "may be cut in tracks in one groove at 45° to the recording surface on opposite sides of the normal." Each wall of the V-shaped groove carries one channel; sound common to both moves the stylus sideways, and the difference between them moves it up and down. Blumlein died in 1942 testing airborne radar. The Westrex company's version of his groove became the standard, and the first **[stereophonic](kloom:e/stereophonic-sound)** LPs went on sale in March 1958.

The long side let performers plan an album around one theme, the concept album. It gave way to the cassette and the compact disc, and came back: in 2025 the United States bought 46.8 million vinyl records, worth $1.04 billion, the nineteenth year of growth in a row.
