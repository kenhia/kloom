By the early 1930s a guitarist in a dance band was mostly felt, not heard. As swing bands grew, an acoustic guitar playing rhythm was, in the critic Alan Kurtz's words, "buried alive by ten brass and five saxes." In **[Los Angeles](kloom:e/los-angeles)**, **[George Beauchamp](kloom:e/george-beauchamp)**, who had played Hawaiian lap steel in vaudeville, set out to make the strings themselves electrical. In 1931 he, Paul Barth and the engineer **[Adolph Rickenbacker](kloom:e/adolph-rickenbacker)** formed the Ro-Pat-In Corporation, and in the late summer of 1932 they began making a lap steel cast in aluminum, a round body on a long neck nicknamed the "Frying Pan." It was the first **[electric guitar](kloom:e/electric-guitar)** sold commercially, and Ro-Pat-In became **[Rickenbacker](kloom:e/rickenbacker)**.

## A magnet, a coil and a string

Beauchamp applied for a patent on 2 June 1934, and it was granted on 10 August 1937, by which time others were making electric guitars too. The patent explains the **[pickup](kloom:e/pickup-music-technology)**. The strings run through two horseshoe magnets set end to end, north pole facing north and south facing south, and below the strings sits a coil of enameled wire with six pins standing in it, one under each string, that "concentrate the magnetic force." A steel string standing still leaves the field steady. Plucked, it moves through the field, the flux through the coil changes with every swing, and a current is induced in the coil, "proportional to the characteristics of the movement of the strings." This is **[Faraday's law of induction](kloom:e/faradays-law-of-induction)**: the voltage around a circuit equals the rate at which the magnetic flux through it changes. The patent draws the field as lines of force, crowded between the magnets and the pins. Since the voltage follows the rate of change, it rises and falls with the string's speed rather than its position, as the plate shows. The plate draws the ideal case; the physicists Nicholas Horton and Thomas Moore showed in 2009 that a real pickup adds distortion of its own.

| Link           | What it does                                               |
| -------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------- |
| Steel string   | Vibrates, carrying its own magnetism through the field     |
| Pickup         | Turns the changing flux into a small voltage               |
| Volume control | A variable resistance on the body, under the player's hand |
| Jack and cord  | A two-conductor cable to the amplifier                     |
| Amplifier      | In the patent, a one-stage audio amplifier                 |
| Loudspeaker    | Turns the amplified current back into sound                |

Because the body did nothing but hold the strings, Beauchamp could cast it from metal and let the strings ring: he wrote that they could have "long periods of vibration with no dampening action." So a plucked note rings on, and the player's steel bar, slid along the strings as the patent describes, bends it into the glide of Hawaiian music.

## The guitar as a horn

In 1936 Gibson brought out the ES-150, a hollow archtop with a bar pickup, named for its price of $150 with an amplifier. In August 1939 the producer John Hammond brought a twenty-three-year-old guitarist from Oklahoma City, **[Charlie Christian](kloom:e/charlie-christian)**, who played an ES-150, to Los Angeles to meet **[Benny Goodman](kloom:e/benny-goodman)**. By one account Goodman called "Rose Room" on the bandstand that night, sure the newcomer did not know it, and Christian played chorus after chorus. He went from $2.50 a night to $150 a week, a Black musician in a white bandleader's group. That December he wrote in _Down Beat_: "Guitar players have long needed a champion, someone to explain to the world that a guitarist is something more than just a robot plunking on a gadget to keep the rhythm going." He played single notes, not chords, picking each with a downstroke, as the guitarist Eddie Durham had told him to, to "get a punch like a saxophone." "Solo Flight," recorded with Goodman's full orchestra of twelve horns on 4 March 1941, makes the amplified guitar the soloist from start to finish; to Kurtz it "projected gloriously" or, put another way, "stood out like a sore thumb." Christian died of tuberculosis on 2 March 1942, aged 25. The record came out at the end of 1943.

## The solid body

A hollow guitar with a pickup howls when the amplifier's sound shakes its top. To stop that, and to make notes sustain, **[Les Paul](kloom:e/les-paul)** bolted a neck and his own pickups to a four-by-four of pine, with the halves of an Epiphone body glued on for looks: the "Log." Guitar Player dates it to 1939, Wikipedia to 1940, the National Inventors Hall of Fame to the early 1940s, and the accounts differ even on whose neck it wore. Gibson turned it down in 1941. **[Leo Fender](kloom:e/leo-fender)**, who ran a radio repair shop in Fullerton, California, did not wait: his company, **[Fender](kloom:e/fender-company)**, sold the single-pickup Esquire in 1950 at $139.95 and the two-pickup Broadcaster that autumn, renamed the **[Telecaster](kloom:e/fender-telecaster)** in 1951 after Gretsch objected that it made a Broadkaster drum. It was a slab of ash with a maple neck screwed on, built to be made in quantity and easily mended. Gibson answered with the **[Gibson Les Paul](kloom:e/gibson-les-paul)** in 1952, and Fender with the contoured, three-pickup **[Stratocaster](kloom:e/fender-stratocaster)** in 1954.

Players then turned the faults into sounds. In 1964 Dave Davies of the Kinks slashed the speaker cone of his little Elpico amplifier with a razor blade, and its torn, buzzing tone carried "You Really Got Me." That October the Beatles' "I Feel Fine" began with feedback: Paul McCartney plucked an A on his bass, John Lennon's guitar, leaning against the bass amplifier, picked it up, and a single buzzing note swelled out of the speaker ("Nnnnnnwahhhhh!" in McCartney's memory) before the riff began in D. That feedback was the loop of string, pickup, amplifier and speaker closing on itself. Rock and roll would make the electric guitar its voice.
