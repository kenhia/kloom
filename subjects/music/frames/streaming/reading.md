After **[Napster](kloom:e/napster)** closed in July 2001, **[Apple](kloom:e/apple-inc)** built a legal road for music as files, in two pieces. On 23 October 2001 it presented the **[iPod](kloom:e/ipod)**, a $399 player with a five-gigabyte hard drive that "Puts 1,000 Songs in Your Pocket". On 28 April 2003 **[Steve Jobs](kloom:e/steve-jobs)** opened the **[iTunes Music Store](kloom:e/itunes-store)**: more than 200,000 songs from BMG, EMI, Sony, Universal and Warner, coded at 128 kilobits a second, for 99 cents each and no subscription. "Consumers don't want to be treated like criminals," he said, "and artists don't want their valuable work stolen." It sold more than a million songs in its first five days.

A song bought was still a song owned. In Stockholm in April 2006 **[Daniel Ek](kloom:e/daniel-ek)**, who had briefly run the file-sharing program µTorrent, founded **[Spotify](kloom:e/spotify)** with **[Martin Lorentzon](kloom:e/martin-lorentzon)**. Ek had decided, as he later told it, that "you can never legislate away from piracy"; the answer was a service better than piracy that also paid. Spotify opened in several European countries on 7 October 2008 and in the United States in July 2011. Nobody bought a song from it: listeners paid, or heard advertisements, for the right to play any of millions. By the end of September 2026 Spotify had 788 million monthly listeners, 306 million of them paying.

The **[Recording Industry Association of America](kloom:e/recording-industry-association-of-america)** counts what American record companies take in, and its database adjusts the series for inflation (billions of 2025 dollars, as the chart and table give them; the totals are our sums, without licensing for film and advertising, $0.4 billion in 2025). The peak was 1999, almost all of it [compact discs](kloom:e/compact-disc). Downloads never made up for the discs, and the industry bottomed out in 2014 and 2015 at well under half its peak in real terms. In 2015 streaming became the largest format for the first time. ## How a stream pays

A stream has no price. Spotify, like the other services, puts about two-thirds of what it earns from music into a pool and divides it by _streamshare_: an act with 1 percent of all the streams gets about 1 percent of the pool, paid to its record company and publisher, who pay the artist under their contracts. The plate draws the split. In 2025 Spotify paid out more than $11 billion; an act with one stream in every million earned, on average, more than $11,000 from it.

Divide payouts by plays and a "rate" appears. Duetti, which buys independent artists' catalogs, put what such artists' recordings earned on Spotify in 2024 at $3 per 1,000 streams, on Apple Music at $6.20 and on Amazon at $8.80. Spotify called the comparison "ridiculous and unfounded": its listeners stream more, so the same pool is cut finer. Since April 2024 a track has needed 1,000 streams in a year to earn recording royalties at all, which Spotify said moved about $40 million a year from tiny payments to larger ones.

A committee of the House of Commons traced a British subscription in 2021:

| Where £9.99 a month goes                        | Share            | Amount        |
| ----------------------------------------------- | ---------------- | ------------- |
| Value-added tax                                 | 20 percent       | £1.67         |
| The streaming service                           | about 30 percent | £2.50         |

The amounts are our arithmetic from its shares. Independent labels told the committee the services keep nearer 35 percent. A survey it cited found that in 2019, 82 percent of professional musicians made less than £200 from streaming.

## Songs for skippers

A listener who can skip for nothing changes how songs begin. Hubert Léveillé Gauvin at Ohio State timed the top-ten American singles from 1986 to 2015: the instrumental introduction, more than 20 seconds long in the mid-1980s, had shrunk to about five, and voices and titles arrived sooner. A hit of 1986 might play for twenty seconds before anyone sang; a hit of 2015 was singing almost at once. He found, though, that an artist's most popular songs of 2015 were no quicker to begin than their less popular ones, so the skip button is not the whole story.

The pool keeps growing, and so does what is poured into it. Its newest arrivals were made by no one singing at all, by machines that learned music from recordings.
