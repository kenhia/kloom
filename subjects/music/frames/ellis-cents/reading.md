On March 25, 1885, at the **[Society of Arts](kloom:e/royal-society-of-arts)** in London, **[Alexander John Ellis](kloom:e/alexander-john-ellis)** read a paper called "On the Musical Scales of Various Nations." He was seventy, a phonetician and Fellow of the Royal Society, and the translator of [Helmholtz](kloom:e/hermann-von-helmholtz)'s [_On the Sensations of Tone_](kloom:e/sensations-of-tone). The reading, he wrote, "was entirely occupied by illustrations," because it mattered more "that the audience should actually hear the scales than be merely spoken to about them." He played them on a two-stringed "dichord" that Broadwood's had built for him, with a separate fingerboard marked for each scale, and on five English concertinas, one of them tuned to Javanese scales. The printed paper ran to forty-three pages of the Society's _Journal_. It needed a new unit, and Ellis supplied one: the **[cent](kloom:e/cent-music)**.

## A hundred to a semitone

Ratios, he said, "convey no conception whatever to the musician." So: "Suppose a piano made of such a gigantic size that we could interpose 99 smaller finger keys between any two at present existing, and that we could tune these at exactly equal intervals, called cents, so that 100 cents would form an equal semitone." There are then 1,200 to the octave. He had first defined the unit in 1880, in a paper on the history of musical pitch, and used it throughout the second edition of his Helmholtz in 1885.

A cent is a logarithm. Multiplying ratios adds intervals, so the measure must turn multiplication into addition: the cents in a ratio _r_ are 1200 × log₂ _r_. Ellis said it plainly in his Helmholtz: "Cents are in fact a system of logs in which cent log 2 = 1200." For readers without tables he gave a rule that needs only arithmetic: multiply 3,477 by the difference of the ratio's two numbers and divide by their sum. For the major third, 4:5, that is 3,477 ÷ 9, or 386 cents. "No ear," he added, "has yet succeeded in hearing the interval of 1 cent between two notes played in succession," though 5 "may be easily heard by good ears."

In cents, every argument in this trail becomes a subtraction: the equal third is 14 cents wide of pure, the Pythagorean 22. The plate lays one octave on such a line, where equal steps look equal and the ratios 1.1, 1.2 and so on crowd toward the top.

Ellis did not invent the idea of a logarithmic step. In his Helmholtz he credited **[Joseph Sauveur](kloom:e/joseph-sauveur)**, who in 1701 had divided the octave into 301 parts, from a thousand times the logarithm of 2, and had given a rule much like his own; Sauveur, who named the science of acoustics, had, his colleague Fontenelle wrote, "neither a voice nor hearing."

## What he measured

With the unit came the measurements. Ellis's ears were **[Alfred James Hipkins](kloom:e/alfred-james-hipkins)** of John Broadwood and Sons, piano makers, whose "delicate ear" for small intervals, Ellis wrote, made the paper possible. They matched each instrument's notes against a long series of tuning forks whose pitches Ellis had fixed from a standard set: an Indian sitar given to Ellis by Raja Ram Pal Singh, who played it for them, a Japanese koto, Chinese bells, a wooden harmonicon sent from Singapore, and the instruments of two **[gamelan](kloom:e/gamelan)** ensembles from **[Java](kloom:e/java)** that had played at the Aquarium in London in 1882.

The gamelan's scales were the surprise. In the scale called **[sléndro](kloom:e/slendro)** he measured five steps in an octave, of 228, 256, 244, 232 and 240 cents, near enough to five equal steps of 240 that he supposed that to be the ideal, its fourth flat and its fifth sharp by European measure. **[Pélog](kloom:e/pelog)**, the other gamelan's tuning, drew five-note scales from a fund of seven notes, and in it he found steps of "very nearly 100, 150, and 300 cents."

His conclusion is the sentence the paper is known for: "the Musical Scale is not one, not 'natural,' nor even founded necessarily on the laws of the constitution of musical sound, so beautifully worked out by Helmholtz, but very diverse, very artificial, and very capricious." He was not free of the ears he criticized; he found the gamelan's singing "dreadfully, and apparently intentionally, unmusical" and left it out. But he asked for the work to be finished by "physicists who have some notion of music, rather than of musicians whose ears are trained to particular systems."

## What it began

Ellis's measuring, and the cent, became tools of comparative musicology, the study of the world's musics side by side, which grew in Berlin around its archive of cylinder recordings and was renamed **[ethnomusicology](kloom:e/ethnomusicology)** in the 1950s. Outside it too, cents are still the standard way to state the size of an interval.

This is the last frame of the trail. Its anchor, Pythagoras and the ratios, began with a string and whole numbers; Ellis ended by measuring the world's scales and finding that whole numbers did not rule them.
