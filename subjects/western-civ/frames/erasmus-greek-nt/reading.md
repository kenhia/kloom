For over a thousand years, the New Testament lived in Latin. The Latin _Vulgate_, made by **Jerome** in the fourth century, was the text of the Church. But when **Desiderius Erasmus**, a Dutch humanist priest, began collating the Greek manuscripts in the great libraries and monasteries of Europe, he found that Jerome's Latin did not always match the original. On 1 March 1516, his printer in **Basel**, **Johann Froben**, published the first printed New Testament in Greek, side by side with a corrected Latin version and notes explaining every choice. It was called the _Novum Instrumentum omne_ — "the complete new instrument" — and it changed the course of the Reformation before Martin Luther even posted his theses.

## The recovery of Greek

Erasmus had learned his Greek during the Renaissance recovery of classical texts, studying under the best masters and reading the Church Fathers in their original language. He spent a decade comparing the Greek manuscripts he could reach. For the main body of his text he relied on eight Greek manuscripts in Basel, including one for the Gospels, another for Acts and the Epistles, and a third for Revelation. The work was rushed — Erasmus complained it was "precipitated rather than published" — but it was whole, critical, and grounded in real sources.

The New Testament in Greek had not been in wide circulation since before the Middle Ages. Arabic and Hebrew texts stayed alive in their traditions, but Western Christendom had forgotten the Greek itself. Erasmus's printed edition brought it back, making the original language available to any scholar with the price of a book.

## The Reformation's foundation

Scholars could now see what Jesus and his apostles had actually written. **Martin Luther**, in Wittenberg, used Erasmus's Greek text for his German translation of the New Testament, published in 1522. Across Europe, printers raced to make their own versions from the Greek: **William Tyndale** in English, versions in French, Spanish and Hungarian all drew on Erasmus's work. Over the next century the Greek text came to be called the _Textus Receptus_, the "received text," and it became the basis for the King James Bible in 1611.

The five revised editions Erasmus himself published, each corrected against newly discovered manuscripts, shaped Bible translation for three centuries. His method — comparing manuscripts to recover the original — became the foundation of biblical scholarship. The Latin Vulgate, which had stood unchallenged in the Church for over a thousand years, now had a rival not just in faith but in philology.

The printing press had made books cheap and common. Erasmus's work showed that the printed word could also be critical and exact, reaching back through centuries to the source. His Greek New Testament sat on the shelves of scholars, printers and reformers, and what they read there would reshape Christianity.
