On Christmas morning 2021 an Ariane 5 rocket lifted off from Kourou, in French
Guiana, carrying the largest telescope ever sent into space. On 11 July 2022
the **[James Webb Space Telescope](kloom:e/james-webb-space-telescope)** showed the world its first deep image: a
patch of sky the size of a grain of sand held at arm's length, crowded with
thousands of galaxies.

## Looking back in time

Light takes time to travel. We see the Sun as it was eight minutes ago, the
nearest stars as they were years ago, and distant galaxies as they were
billions of years ago. Looking far away means looking back.

The universe is also expanding, and the light of the most distant galaxies
has been stretched on its long journey, out of the visible range and into
the **[infrared](kloom:e/infrared)**. To see the first galaxies, a telescope has to see infrared
light — and to do that, it has to be very cold, or its own warmth will
drown out the faint signals it is looking for.

## An origami observatory

Webb's primary mirror is 6.5 metres across, made of 18 hexagonal segments of
gold-coated beryllium. It was too big to fit inside any rocket, so the
telescope launched folded. Over the following weeks, a million and a half
kilometres from [Earth](kloom:e/earth), it unfolded itself: a sunshield the size of a tennis
court, five layers of thin film, spread out to keep the telescope in
permanent shade; the mirror wings swung into place; the segments were
adjusted, one by one, until they worked together as a single mirror.

It sits near the second Lagrange point, L2, a spot beyond the [Moon](kloom:e/moon)'s orbit
where it can keep the Sun, Earth and Moon all on one side, behind its
shield. There, the cold side of the observatory stays at a few tens of
degrees above absolute zero.

## The first deep field

The first image, **[Webb's First Deep Field](kloom:e/webbs-first-deep-field)**, is centred on a galaxy cluster
called SMACS 0723, seen as it was about 4.6 billion years ago. The cluster's
huge mass bends and magnifies the light of galaxies far behind it, acting as
a natural lens. Among them are galaxies whose light set out some 13 billion
years ago, when the universe was young.

In the same summer, Webb sent back images of dying stars, of a nursery of
new stars in the Carina Nebula, and a spectrum showing water in the
atmosphere of a planet orbiting another star. Its instruments are the
descendants of every lens, mirror and equation in this timeline, from
[Eratosthenes](kloom:e/eratosthenes)' shadow to [Maxwell](kloom:e/james-clerk-maxwell)'s waves. After thousands of years of
looking up, we have built a way to look back to the beginning.
