On 2 August 762, by the account of the geographer al-Ya'qubi, work began on a new capital beside the Tigris. Its builder was **[al-Mansur](kloom:e/al-mansur)**, second caliph of the **[Abbasid Caliphate](kloom:e/abbasid-caliphate)**, the dynasty that in 750 had overthrown the Umayyad caliphs of Damascus and moved the center of the Muslim world east, to Iraq. **[Islam](kloom:e/islam)** was then a century and a half old. **[Muhammad](kloom:e/muhammad)** had died in 632, and the armies of the caliphs who followed him had carried Arab rule over the old Persian empire, Byzantium's southern provinces and, from 711, most of Iberia. The new city was **[Baghdad](kloom:e/baghdad)**, officially the City of Peace, and it was round.

The plate draws it as the orientalist Guy Le Strange reconstructed it in 1900 from the Arabic geographers: a ditch filled with water, two walls, a ring of houses, and an inner wall around a great open center holding the caliph's palace and the mosque. Four gates, named for the roads they faced (Kufa, Basra, Khurasan and Syria), led to the center down straight, arcaded streets. It was finished in 766. Nothing of it survives, and the sources disagree widely about its size.

## How a book was translated

The best witness to how Greek learning was put into Arabic is the translator **[Hunayn ibn Ishaq](kloom:e/hunayn-ibn-ishaq)** (808–873), a Christian of the Church of the East from al-Hira. In 855 or 856, at the age of 48, he wrote a letter listing the works of **[Galen](kloom:e/galen)** he knew of, 129 in its modern editor's numbering, with who had translated each, into which language and for whom. The last mattered, he explained, because "the translation differs according to the abilities of the translator of the book and of the one for whom it was translated" (in our English of Gotthelf Bergsträsser's German, 1925). A few of his entries:

| Galen's work     | Into Syriac                                                       | Into Arabic                                    |
| ---------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------- |
| _On Sects_       | Hunayn at about 20, for a physician of Gondeshapur; revised at 40 | Hunayn, for Muhammad ibn Musa                  |
| _On the Pulse_   | Hunayn, for the physician Salmawaih                               | Hunayn, for Muhammad ibn Musa                  |
| _On Bones_       | Hunayn, for the physician Yuhanna ibn Masawaih                    | Hunayn, for Muhammad ibn Musa                  |
| _On the Muscles_ | Hunayn, for Yuhanna ibn Masawaih                                  | Hunayn's nephew Hubaysh, for Muhammad ibn Musa |

The route ran from Greek through Syriac, the scholarly language of the region's Christians, into Arabic. The patrons were Christian court physicians, who read Syriac, and Muslim scholars such as Muhammad ibn Musa, one of the three **[Banu Musa brothers](kloom:e/banu-musa-brothers)**, who wanted Arabic. When several Greek manuscripts of _On Sects_ had come into his hands, Hunayn wrote, "I collated these with one another, so that a single correct manuscript came out of them, and then collated this manuscript with the Syriac and corrected it; so I am accustomed to proceed with everything I translate." For Galen's _On Demonstration_ he traveled through "Mesopotamia and Syria, Palestine and Egypt" as far as Alexandria, and found about half of it, in Damascus.

Galen was one author among many. Al-Hajjaj ibn Matar put Euclid's _Elements_ into Arabic twice, for a vizier of Harun al-Rashid and again for the caliph **[al-Ma'mun](kloom:e/al-mamun)** (813–833), and translated Ptolemy's _Almagest_ around 829. Hunayn's circle went on to nearly the whole of Aristotle.

Was Hunayn paid the weight of his books in gold? The biographer Ibn Abi Usaybi'a, writing nearly four centuries later, reports that al-Ma'mun did. The same book quotes Hunayn as taking a book's weight in silver dirhams from whoever he translated it for, and says its author saw those books: a large hand, widely spaced, on paper as thick as three or four modern leaves. It adds that the Banu Musa paid a team of translators about 500 dinars a month. The gold is a story; the record is of private patrons.

## A house, or a storeroom?

The traditional account puts all this in the **[House of Wisdom](kloom:e/house-of-wisdom)**, an academy founded by al-Ma'mun with Hunayn at its head. The Yale Arabist Dimitri Gutas reads the evidence differently: the name meant a storehouse of books, a palace library of the kind the Persian kings had kept, and none of the translations mentions it. Yet there was a library with a keeper, for Ibn Abi Usaybi'a names "the administrator of Bayt al-Hikmah" among the envoys al-Ma'mun sent to Byzantium for manuscripts. The library is likely; the academy is in doubt.

## Paper

The books were written on paper. It is said to have come with Chinese prisoners taken at Talas in 751, but the historian Jonathan Bloom points to paper documents of 722–723 found near Samarkand. By the reign of Harun al-Rashid (786–809), he writes, Baghdad's officials kept their records on it, and the fourteenth-century historian Ibn Khaldun credits a vizier with starting its manufacture there; later accounts date a mill to 794–795. Paper drank the ink, so a document could not be scraped and rewritten as parchment could. Baghdad's Stationers' Market had more than a hundred paper- and booksellers' shops.

## Making new things

**[Al-Kindi](kloom:e/al-kindi)**, the first Arabic philosopher, wrote, in Peter Adamson's translation, that "we must not be ashamed to admire the truth or to acquire it, from wherever it comes." **[Al-Khwarizmi](kloom:e/al-khwarizmi)** wrote the algebra; al-Razi ran hospitals and classified substances; **[Ibn al-Haytham](kloom:e/ibn-al-haytham)**, in Cairo, doubted Ptolemy and rebuilt optics; Ibn Sina, **[Avicenna](kloom:e/avicenna)** in Latin, wrote the _Canon of Medicine_ that universities taught for centuries. Each appears in another of these subjects.

A second center grew in the west. In 756 an Umayyad prince who had escaped the Abbasids made himself emir at Córdoba, in **[al-Andalus](kloom:e/al-andalus)**, and in 929 his descendant took the title of caliph. Under al-Hakam II (961–976) the royal library there was put at 400,000 to 500,000 volumes. It was through Spain, at Toledo, that much of this learning reached Latin in the twelfth century. The next time Latin Europe came east, it came armed.
