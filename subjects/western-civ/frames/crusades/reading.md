On 27 November 1095, on the last day of a church council at Clermont, in central France, **[Pope Urban II](kloom:e/pope-urban-ii)** preached to a crowd. The Byzantine emperor had asked the West for soldiers against the Seljuk Turks, who had taken most of Asia Minor from him. Urban asked for more than soldiers. The wars that followed, against the Muslim rulers of the eastern Mediterranean and later against others, are the **[Crusades](kloom:e/crusades)**.

No text of the sermon survives. Five chroniclers wrote versions of it, and they disagree. **[Fulcher of Chartres](kloom:e/fulcher-of-chartres)**, who was there, has Urban speak of aid to the eastern Christians and of peace at home: "Let those who have been accustomed unjustly to wage private warfare against the faithful now go against the infidels," and "rent their lands and collect money for their expenses." Robert the Monk, writing about 1107, makes **[Jerusalem](kloom:e/jerusalem)** "the navel of the world" and has the crowd cry "It is the will of God!" Baldric of Dol and Guibert of Nogent, writing after Jerusalem had fallen, also put the Holy City first, perhaps with hindsight. The canons of the **[Council of Clermont](kloom:e/council-of-clermont)** say what was offered: "whoever for devotion alone, not to obtain honor or money, goes to Jerusalem to liberate the Church of God can substitute the journey for all penance," in Robert Somerville's translation. That was the crusade **[indulgence](kloom:e/indulgence)**, the first plenary indulgence on record, of the kind Martin Luther attacked in 1517.

## Taking the cross

A crusader made a vow and sewed a cloth cross to his clothes, as the first crusaders did on their right shoulders. By 1145, when Pope Eugenius III called the second crusade, the privileges that came with the vow were written down:

| Privilege       | In Eugenius's bull of 1145 (Henderson's translation, 1910)                                                   |
| --------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Forgiveness     | "that remission of sins which our aforesaid predecessor pope Urban did institute"                            |
| Family and land | wives, sons, goods and possessions "under the protection of our selves" and of the bishops                   |
| Lawsuits        | none over property held when they took the cross, until "most certain news" of "their return or their death" |
| Debts           | they "shall not pay interest for the time past"                                                              |
| Mortgages       | they may "freely pledge … their lands or other possessions to churches"                                      |

The last two mattered most, because going was ruinously expensive: a knight's journey could cost four years' income. Robert of Normandy pawned his duchy to his brother, William II of England, and Godfrey of Bouillon sold or mortgaged his lands to the church. Even the siege of Jerusalem ran on cash: according to the _Gesta Francorum_, Count Raymond of Toulouse paid a denarius to anyone who carried three stones to fill the ditch before his siege tower. The plate draws where the two towers stood, and one at the wall in section.

The **[Fourth Crusade](kloom:e/fourth-crusade)** shows the arithmetic at scale. In 1201 its envoys contracted with Venice for a fleet, at rates the chronicler Geoffrey of Villehardouin records:

| For                                                    | Rate    | Sum (by our arithmetic) |
| ------------------------------------------------------ | ------- | ----------------------- |
| 4,500 horses                                           | 4 marks | 18,000 marks            |
| 33,500 men (4,500 knights, 9,000 squires, 20,000 foot) | 2 marks | 67,000 marks            |
| The fleet, with nine months' food                      |         | 85,000 marks            |

Too few came, and the army was still 34,000 marks short. The Venetians accepted payment in service instead: the crusaders captured the Christian city of Zara for them in 1202.

## Mainz and Jerusalem

The first to die were Jews. In May 1096 a band under a Rhineland count, Emicho, killed Jews at Speyer and Worms. At Mainz on 27 May the archbishop, who had sheltered the community in his palace, fled. Many Jews killed their own children and themselves rather than be baptized. "On the third of Siwan, on a Tuesday eleven hundred souls were killed and slaughtered," wrote the Hebrew chronicler Solomon bar Samson, in Jacob Marcus's translation. These are the **[Rhineland massacres](kloom:e/rhineland-massacres)**.

The **[First Crusade](kloom:e/first-crusade)** reached Jerusalem on 7 June 1099 and took it on 15 July. The killing that followed is described by its own side: the _Gesta_ has its men wade "in blood up to their ankles", and Raymond of Aguilers, a chaplain in the army, has them ride in it "up to their knees and bridle reins". The Damascus chronicler Ibn al-Qalanisi wrote that "the Jews assembled in the synagogue, and the Franks burned it over their heads." The number of dead is disputed: the twelfth-century jurist Ibn al-Arabi gave 3,000, and Ibn al-Athir, writing a century later, 70,000, which historians think far too many. A letter from the Cairo Geniza shows the Jews of Ascalon ransoming captives.

## Two centuries

Four **[crusader states](kloom:e/crusader-states)** were founded, and the military orders, the **[Knights Hospitaller](kloom:e/knights-hospitaller)** and the **[Knights Templar](kloom:e/knights-templar)**, garrisoned them; the Hospital's own story, of a pilgrims' hospital that became an order, is told in _Keeping Watch_. In 1187 **[Saladin](kloom:e/saladin)**, sultan of Egypt and Syria, destroyed their army at Hattin and took Jerusalem by negotiated surrender: ten dinars for a man, five for a woman, two for a child, and slavery for many who could not pay. In April 1204 the Fourth Crusade sacked Christian **[Constantinople](kloom:e/constantinople)**, which Pope Innocent III had forbidden it to attack. In May 1291 the Mamluk sultan al-Ashraf Khalil took Acre, the last great crusader city.

Why did they go? Muslim chroniclers saw avarice, and many writers from the seventeenth century on saw land and plunder. The charters in which crusaders sold and mortgaged their land show instead ruinous costs, whole families going together and the language of penance; Jonathan Riley-Smith, the leading crusade historian of his generation, called crusading, in the title of a 1980 essay, "an act of love". Others, from sixteenth-century Lutherans who saw Urban's quarrel with the emperor Henry IV behind it to the German historian Carl Erdmann, have read it as politics: a quarrel of pope and emperor or, for Erdmann, an ideology that had grown up in the Latin West. The sources support each in part. The next frame stays in Latin Europe, where the money and faith went into building.
