On 29 August 1831, in a basement laboratory at the [Royal Institution](kloom:e/royal-institution) in
[London](kloom:e/london), **[Michael Faraday](kloom:e/michael-faraday)** closed a circuit and watched a needle twitch. The
twitch was brief, and it meant that magnetism could make electricity. Every
generator and transformer on the grid today works by the effect he saw.

## The bookbinder's apprentice

Faraday was born in 1791 in Newington Butts, Surrey, and had only the most
basic schooling. At fourteen he was apprenticed to a bookbinder, and he read
the books he bound. In 1812 he attended lectures by the chemist **Humphry
Davy**, sent Davy a bound volume of notes on them, and the next year was taken
on as his assistant. His mathematics, by most accounts, never went beyond
simple algebra. He made up for it with experiment.

In 1820 the Danish physicist **[Hans Christian Ørsted](kloom:e/hans-christian-orsted)** showed that an electric
current deflects a compass needle: electricity makes magnetism. In 1821
Faraday built devices in which a wire carrying a current turned continuously
around a magnet, an early form of electric motor. The obvious question was
the reverse one. If a current makes magnetism, could magnetism make a
current?

## A ring of soft iron

Faraday had a ring welded from soft round bar iron, seven-eighths of an inch
thick and six inches across, and wound two separate coils of copper wire on
opposite sides of it, insulated with twine and calico. The Royal Institution
reckons the winding took about ten working days. One coil, _A_, went to a
battery; the other, _B_, to a galvanometer, a needle that swings when current
flows. The plate shows the arrangement.

When he connected the battery, the needle jumped, then settled back. When he
disconnected it, the needle jumped the other way. A current in one coil
produced a current in the other, although no wire joined them, but only while
the first current was _changing_. The iron carried the magnetism from one
side of the ring to the other. The Royal Institution, which still has the
ring, calls it the first transformer.

Through the autumn he chased the effect. When a bar magnet "was suddenly
thrust in" to a coil, the needle "immediately" deflected; when he pulled it
out, it swung back. Late in October, working with a large magnet belonging
to the Royal Society, he spun a copper disc between magnetic poles and drew
off, in his words, "a _permanent current of electricity_". The disc
was very inefficient, and never a practical power source, but it was the
first generator, and it pointed the way to the dynamos that followed.

## The law and the lines

Faraday read his results to the Royal Society on 24 November 1831, as the
first of his _Experimental Researches in Electricity_, printed in the
_Philosophical Transactions_ in 1832. In modern terms, what he found is
[_Faraday's law of induction_](kloom:e/faradays-law-of-induction): the voltage driven around a closed circuit
equals the rate at which the magnetic flux through it changes. **Joseph
Henry** in America found the same effect independently and published in 1832;
Faraday published first. In 1834 **Emil Lenz** gave the rule for which way
the induced current flows.

Faraday explained it all without equations, with "lines of force" that
filled the space around magnets and were cut by moving wires. Most
contemporaries rejected so unmathematical an account. The physicist who took it seriously is the next frame, "Let
there be LIGHT."
