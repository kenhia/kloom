On 25 April 1953 the journal _Nature_ printed a one-page letter from two
researchers in Cambridge, **[James Watson](kloom:e/james-watson)** and **[Francis Crick](kloom:e/francis-crick)**. It proposed
a structure for deoxyribose nucleic acid, **[DNA](kloom:e/dna)**, and it ended with one of
the most famous understatements in science:

> It has not escaped our notice that the specific pairing we have postulated
> immediately suggests a possible copying mechanism for the genetic material.

## The question

By the 1950s it was clear that inheritance was carried by molecules, and
increasingly clear that DNA was the one that mattered. It was also known that
DNA was a long chain built from four kinds of units, the bases **adenine**,
**thymine**, **guanine** and **cytosine**, and that in any organism the
amount of A matched the amount of T, and G matched C. What nobody knew was
how the molecule was arranged, or how it could store information and copy
itself.

## Photo 51

At King's College London, **[Rosalind Franklin](kloom:e/rosalind-franklin)**, an expert in X-ray
crystallography, and her student **Raymond Gosling** were photographing DNA
fibres with X-rays. In May 1952 they took an exceptionally clear image of the
hydrated form of DNA, later known as **[Photo 51](kloom:e/photo-51)**. Its distinctive X-shaped
pattern is the signature of a helix, and its spacings give the helix's
dimensions: a full turn every 3.4 nanometres.

Early in 1953 Franklin's colleague **Maurice Wilkins** showed the photograph
to Watson, without her knowledge. Watson and Crick, who had been building
models, also saw a report containing her measurements. Within weeks they had
a model that fitted: two strands winding around each other in a **double
helix**, with the bases paired inside like the rungs of a twisted ladder —
A always with T, G always with C.

Their letter appeared alongside two papers from King's, one by Wilkins and
his colleagues and one by Franklin and Gosling, that supplied the
experimental evidence. Watson, Crick and Wilkins shared the Nobel Prize in 1962. Franklin had died of cancer in 1958, aged 37; the prize is not awarded
posthumously. Her part in the discovery is now widely recognised.

## A code

The pairing was the key. Because A fits only with T and G only with C, each
strand carries the information needed to rebuild the other. Unzip the helix,
and each half can serve as a template for a new, identical partner. That is
how a cell copies its genes before it divides.

Within a few years researchers showed that the order of the bases spells out
instructions — three bases to each amino acid — for building proteins. Life
turned out to be written in a four-letter alphabet. Half a century after the
_Nature_ letter, the Human Genome Project read out the roughly three billion
letters of a human being.
