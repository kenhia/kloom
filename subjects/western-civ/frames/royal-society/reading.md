On 28 November 1660, after a lecture at Gresham College in [London](kloom:e/london) by the
young astronomer **[Christopher Wren](kloom:e/christopher-wren)**, twelve men agreed to found a "Colledge
for the Promoting of Physico-Mathematicall Experimentall Learning". In 1662
Charles II gave them a royal charter, and they became the **[Royal Society](kloom:e/royal-society)**.
Among the early fellows was **[Robert Boyle](kloom:e/robert-boyle)**; by 1662 the Society had a
Curator of Experiments, **[Robert Hooke](kloom:e/robert-hooke)**, to stage demonstrations at its
meetings, and a secretary to handle its letters.

## Take nobody's word for it

The Society's motto is _Nullius in verba_. It is cut down from a line of
Horace's _Epistles_, "not bound to swear by the words of a master", and
the Society today glosses it as "take nobody's word for it": the fellows'
determination "to withstand the domination of authority and to verify all
statements by an appeal to facts determined by experiment". Sources differ on
when it was adopted. The Society's own history puts it in the first charter
of 1662; Wikipedia's account of the Society has the arms and motto granted
with the second charter, of 23 April 1663.

A rule like that has a price. If no one's word is enough, every claim has to
be shown: done in front of witnesses, or written out so fully that someone
somewhere else can try it for themselves.

## Monday, 6 March 1665

The secretary was **[Henry Oldenburg](kloom:e/henry-oldenburg)**, born in Bremen around 1619, who
kept up a correspondence with natural philosophers across Europe. He turned
that post into print. The first number of the _[Philosophical Transactions](kloom:e/philosophical-transactions-of-the-royal-society)_
is dated "Munday, March 6. 1664/5"; England then began its year on 25 March.
It ran to sixteen pages and sold for a shilling. It was Oldenburg's private
venture, as editor and publisher, and the journal lost money for most of its
life.

Its introduction says why it exists: so that those engaged in such studies
"may be invited and encouraged to search, try, and find out new things,
impart their knowledge to one another". The first number held news of
improved lenses from Rome, Adrien Auzout's prediction of a comet's path, a
note of Boyle's on cold, a "very odd Monstrous Calf", whaling off Bermuda,
and Major Holmes's report that pendulum watches invented by **[Christiaan
Huygens](kloom:e/christiaan-huygens)** had found the longitude on a voyage from Guinea.

Was it the first scientific journal? The _Journal des sçavans_ came out two
months earlier. But it was state-sponsored, mostly book reviews, and covered
every kind of learning; the _Transactions_ kept to natural knowledge, and has
been published ever since.

## Who, and when

Oldenburg had written to Boyle in November 1664 that "we must be very
careful as well of regist'ring the person and time of any new matter, as the
matter itselfe". The first number shows what he meant. A short note reports
that Hooke, with a twelve-foot telescope, had seen a spot in a belt of
Jupiter "on the ninth of May, 1664, about 9 of the clock at night", and that
within two hours it had moved half the planet's width. The observation now
had a name and a date in print, which settled who saw it first, and anyone
with a good telescope could look for themselves.

That is the pattern the journal set: a report that is printed, dated and
signed, so that its claim of priority is on record and others can repeat it.
Wikipedia credits the _Transactions_ with all four functions of a journal
from the start: registration, certification, dissemination and archiving.
Certification is the overstatement. The Society's Council provided on 1 March
1665 that the tract be revised by its members, and Oldenburg sent papers to
people who could judge them. But the Society took the journal over, choosing
papers by a committee, only in 1752, and refereeing became systematic only in
the 1830s.

Within a few years the young [Isaac Newton](kloom:e/isaac-newton)'s first paper, on light and
colors, went out in its pages in February 1672. Huygens's watches, for their
part, did not keep time well enough at sea. That problem waited for
Harrison's marine chronometer. A century on, the same habit of report and
trial met iron and coal in Watt's steam engine.
