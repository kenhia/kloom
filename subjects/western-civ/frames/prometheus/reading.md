Every story of civilisation has to begin somewhere, and the Greeks began theirs
with a theft.

In Hesiod's _Theogony_ and _Works and Days_, composed around 700 BC, the Titan
**Prometheus** twice outwits Zeus on humanity's behalf. First, at a feast at
Mecone, he divides a sacrificed ox so that the gods are tricked into choosing
the bones wrapped in glistening fat, leaving the meat for mortals. Zeus, in
his anger, withholds fire from humankind. So Prometheus steals it back,
hiding the ember inside a hollow stalk of giant fennel, whose pith smoulders
slowly without burning the stem.

The punishment is famous. Zeus has him chained to a rock, where an eagle eats
his liver every day and the liver grows back every night — until, in Hesiod's
telling, Heracles sets him free. Humanity is punished too: Zeus sends
Pandora, and with her the troubles of the world.

## Fire as the first technology

Later writers widened the gift. In the tragedy _Prometheus Bound_, attributed
to Aeschylus, the chained Titan lists what he has given people beyond fire:
numbers, letters, the reading of the stars, the taming of animals, ships,
medicine. Fire is the root of all of it — the first technology that let us
change the world around us instead of only enduring it.

The myth is right about the order of things, if not the details. Charred
bones and microscopic traces of ash deep inside **Wonderwerk Cave**, in South
Africa, suggest that early humans were keeping fire around a million years
ago, well beyond the reach of any wildfire. Making fire at will is another
step: at a site in eastern England, burnt soil, fire-cracked handaxes and
fragments of iron pyrite — used with flint to strike sparks — point to fire
being lit on purpose some 400,000 years ago.

Fire cooked food, which made it easier to digest; it kept predators away; it
lit the night and gathered people around a hearth where stories could be
told. It also made the later crafts possible: firing pottery, smelting metal,
baking the clay tablets on which, one day, writing would be kept.

## Why begin with a myth

This timeline is mostly history, with dates and sources. It starts with a
myth on purpose. The Greeks explained the human condition as the result of a
gift given at great cost, and a debt — to curiosity, to cleverness, to the
willingness to take what the gods kept for themselves. Much of what follows
is that story told again with real names: people who asked, measured, copied,
built and argued, and who passed the fire on.
