In 1550 fourteen theologians and lawyers met in Valladolid to answer a question that the king, the emperor **Charles V**, had put to them: whether Spain might lawfully make war on the peoples of the Americas, "wars that they call conquests", when those peoples had committed no fault but their unbelief. Charles had ordered new conquests suspended until they answered. Two men argued it. **[Bartolomé de las Casas](kloom:e/bartolome-de-las-casas)**, a Dominican friar and bishop of Chiapas, said the wars were tyrannical and unjust. **[Juan Ginés de Sepúlveda](kloom:e/juan-gines-de-sepulveda)**, a humanist and the emperor's chronicler who had never crossed the Atlantic, said they were just. The hearing, the **[Valladolid debate](kloom:e/valladolid-debate)**, is often called the first in which a European power asked whether its own conquests were right. By then most of the people they were fought over were dead.

## Fifty-eight years of conquest

**[Christopher Columbus](kloom:e/christopher-columbus)** landed in the Bahamas in October 1492. His journal, which survives in Las Casas's abstract, says of the **[Taíno](kloom:e/taino)** people who met him, in Clements Markham's translation of 1893: "They should be good servants and intelligent … I believe that they would easily be made Christians." Two days later: "with fifty men they can all be subjugated and made to do what is required of them." On Hispaniola the settlers did. Under the **[encomienda](kloom:e/encomienda)**, the crown granted a Spaniard the labor of a group of Indigenous people, in return for protecting them and teaching them the faith; in practice it was forced labor in the gold mines, close to enslavement. **[Hernán Cortés](kloom:e/hernan-cortes)** landed in Mexico in 1519 and, with thousands of Tlaxcalan allies, took Tenochtitlan, the capital of the **[Aztec Empire](kloom:e/aztec-empire)**, in August 1521, after smallpox had swept the city and killed many of its leaders. In 1532 Francisco Pizarro and 168 men seized the Inca emperor Atahualpa at Cajamarca, and executed him the next year. The **[Spanish Empire](kloom:e/spanish-empire)** now ran from Mexico to Peru.

## The Great Dying

Nobody counted the Americas' people before 1492, and estimates have run from 8 to over 100 million:

| Estimate of the population of the Americas in 1492 | Millions   |
| -------------------------------------------------- | ---------- |
| Alfred Kroeber, 1939                               | 8.4        |
| Angel Rosenblat, 1954                              | 13.4       |
| Henry Dobyns, 1966                                 | 90.0–112.6 |
| William Denevan, 1992                              | 53.9       |
| Koch and others, 2019 (interquartile range)        | 44.8–78.2  |

The latest, a review of 119 regional estimates at University College London, puts the population at 60.5 million and the loss at 90 percent within a century. Disease did most of the killing: smallpox, measles, influenza and typhus, new to the Americas. But the historian Andrés Reséndez points out that no epidemic is recorded in the Caribbean before 1519, while forced labor, famine and war had already emptied the islands. Las Casas wrote that over three million people died on Hispaniola between 1494 and 1508. An ancient DNA study of 2020 suggests the islands never held more than tens of thousands; the dying is not in doubt, only its scale.

## The Requirement

From 1513 a conquering captain was to read aloud a text called the _Requerimiento_, the **[Spanish Requirement](kloom:e/spanish-requirement-of-1513)**, before making war. It told its hearers that a pope had given their lands to the kings of Castile, and that they must submit. If they did not, in Arthur Helps's translation of 1855: "we shall take you and your wives and your children, and shall make slaves of them … and we protest that the deaths and losses which shall accrue from this are your fault." It was read in Spanish to people who could not understand it, or not read at all before an attack.

A few Spaniards objected. On a Sunday in December 1511 the Dominican **[Antonio de Montesinos](kloom:e/antonio-de-montesinos)** preached to the settlers of Santo Domingo, as Las Casas tells it, in our translation: "Are these not men? Have they not rational souls? Are you not bound to love them as yourselves?" The settlers demanded he be punished; the king answered with the Laws of Burgos of 1512, which regulated the encomienda rather than ending it.

## How the case was heard

Las Casas printed an account of the debate in Seville in 1552, the book in the picture, and it says how it ran (in our translation):

| Stage             | What happened                                                                        |
| ----------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| First session     | Sepúlveda spoke and "said all that he wished"                                        |
| Five days         | Las Casas read aloud the whole of his _Apologia_, five days running                  |
| The summary       | It was so long that the judges asked Domingo de Soto, one of them, to summarize both |
| Copies            | One copy of the summary for each of the fourteen judges, to vote on later            |
| Twelve and twelve | Sepúlveda answered twelve objections drawn from it; Las Casas made twelve replies    |

Sepúlveda's case rested on **[Aristotle](kloom:e/aristotle)**'s _Politics_: "Those men therefore who are as much inferior to others as the body is to the soul … are slaves by nature," in William Ellis's version. The Indigenous peoples, he said, were such men, and their human sacrifice justified war. Las Casas answered that they had reason like anyone, and that faith could not be forced.

The hearing ran into 1551, and the judges gave no joint verdict. Each side claimed victory, and the conquests went on. Las Casas had already won the **[New Laws](kloom:e/new-laws)** of 1542, which forbade enslaving Indigenous people and ended the inheritance of encomiendas; settlers in Peru rebelled and killed the viceroy who enforced them, and in 1545 the crown restored the inheritance.

## The other trade

Las Casas confessed a stain on his record. In 1516, to spare the Indigenous people, he proposed that settlers bring in African slaves. He wrote of himself in his _History of the Indies_, in our translation, that he "did not see the injustice with which the Portuguese take them and make them slaves," and that once he did, "he would not have given it for all there is in the world," since "the same reason holds for them as for the Indians." The crown licensed 4,000 Africans for the islands, a license sold to Genoese merchants for 25,000 ducats. For three and a half centuries **[slavery](kloom:e/slavery)** in the Americas was fed by the **[Atlantic slave trade](kloom:e/atlantic-slave-trade)**, which carried about 12.5 million people out of Africa, as the frame on abolition tells.
