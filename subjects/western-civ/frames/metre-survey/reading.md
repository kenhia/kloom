In the 1790s France set out to replace a jumble of local units with one that
belonged to no king and no town, and it chose to take that unit from the
[Earth](kloom:e/earth). The method was [Eratosthenes](kloom:e/eratosthenes)' method: measure a stretch of a meridian
on the ground, find the angle it spans in the sky, and scale up.

## A unit from the planet

Before the [Revolution](kloom:e/french-revolution), units in France could vary from place to place, a
nuisance for trade and a standing invitation to cheat. In 1791 the **French
Academy of Sciences** proposed that the new unit, the **[meter](kloom:e/metre)**, should be one
ten-millionth of the distance from the North Pole to the Equator, measured
along the meridian through [Paris](kloom:e/paris). Nobody could walk a quarter of the globe,
but the part of that meridian running through France could be surveyed, and
the rest could be calculated.

Two astronomers took the job, starting in 1792. **[Jean-Baptiste Delambre](kloom:e/jean-baptiste-delambre)**
measured the northern section, from **Dunkirk** south to **Rodez**; **[Pierre
Méchain](kloom:e/pierre-mechain)** measured the southern section, from Rodez to **Barcelona**. Their
tool was _triangulation_: measure one baseline on the ground very carefully,
then measure only angles from hilltop to church tower to hilltop, so that a
chain of triangles carries the length across hundreds of kilometers. The
latitudes of the two ends, taken from the stars, gave the angle the arc
spans at the Earth's center. They worked through the Revolution and the
Terror, and finished in 1798.

## A small error, kept quiet

In 1799 the result was made into a bar of platinum, the **Mètre des
Archives**, deposited in the National Archives in Paris and declared by law
to be the standard. It was slightly wrong. The calculation assumed a
flattening of the Earth of 1/334, and it did not allow for a local deflection
of the vertical at the southern end of the arc. Measured against the
original definition, the meter came out about 0.2 millimeters short.

Méchain knew something was off. His astronomical checks on latitude in
Barcelona did not agree, and he kept the discrepancy very small in what he
reported, making his measurements look more consistent than they were. The
doubt stayed with him; he went back to Spain to return to the work,
caught yellow fever and died at Castellón de la Plana in 1804. The historian
**Ken Alder** tells the story in _The Measure of All Things_ (2002).

## From the Earth to a bar to light

The error barely mattered, because once the bar existed the bar was the
meter, not the Earth. In 1875 the **[Metre Convention](kloom:e/metre-convention)** made the meter an
international unit, and in 1889 a new prototype of 90 per cent platinum and
10 per cent iridium, with an X-shaped cross-section, replaced the old one,
with calibrated copies sent to the signatory nations. In 1960 the meter was
redefined as 1,650,763.73 wavelengths of a line of krypton-86, and in 1983
as the distance light travels in a vacuum in 1/299,792,458 of a second.

The meter had begun as a fraction of the planet and ended as a fixed
multiple of a constant of nature. The last frame on this trail follows the
[kilogram](kloom:e/kilogram), the unit that held out longest, down the same road.
