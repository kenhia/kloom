In the winter of 1901–02, French excavators working for **[Jacques de Morgan](kloom:e/jacques-de-morgan)** on the acropolis of **[Susa](kloom:e/susa)**, in southwestern Iran, dug up three large pieces of black basalt. Fitted together they made a stone 2.25 meters tall, rounded at the top, carved at its head with two figures and below them with about 4,130 lines of **[cuneiform](kloom:e/cuneiform)**. The Dominican scholar **[Jean-Vincent Scheil](kloom:e/jean-vincent-scheil)** published its text the next year and gave it the name it still has: the **[Code of Hammurabi](kloom:e/code-of-hammurabi)**.

**[Hammurabi](kloom:e/hammurabi)** was the sixth king of a dynasty of **[Babylon](kloom:e/babylon)**, and on the most widely used dating he reigned from about 1792 to 1750 BC. He began as one ruler among several in **[Mesopotamia](kloom:e/mesopotamia)** and ended, by war, alliance and betrayal, as master of the plain between the Tigris and the Euphrates. The stele was set up late in his reign, around 1753 BC, perhaps in **[Sippar](kloom:e/sippar)**, the city where he lived in his last years. About six centuries later it was carried to Susa as plunder, Scheil thought by the Elamite king **[Shutruk-Nakhunte](kloom:e/shutruk-nakhunte)**, and seven columns near its foot were polished away, perhaps for an inscription of the conqueror's own that was never cut. It is now in the **[Louvre](kloom:e/louvre)**.

## If a man

At the top of the stele the king stands before the seated sun god **[Shamash](kloom:e/shamash)**, the god of justice, who holds out a rod and a ring. Below is a prologue of about 300 lines, in which the gods choose Hammurabi to rule "so that the strong should not harm the weak", in L. W. King's 1910 translation. Then come the laws, about 280 of them, and then an epilogue of 500 lines.

Almost every law has the same shape. A case is set out ("if"), and a ruling follows ("then"). Nothing is commanded in general; there is no "thou shalt not". In King's words:

| Law | If                                                                                | Then                                          |
| --- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------- |
| 1   | any one ensnare another, putting a ban upon him, but he can not prove it          | he that ensnared him shall be put to death    |
| 196 | a man put out the eye of another man                                              | his eye shall be put out                      |
| 199 | he put out the eye of a man's slave                                               | he shall pay one-half of its value            |
| 215 | a physician make a large incision with an operating knife and cure it             | he shall receive ten shekels in money         |
| 218 | a physician make a large incision with the operating knife, and kill him          | his hands shall be cut off                    |
| 229 | a builder build a house … and the house which he built fall in and kill its owner | that builder shall be put to death            |
| 230 | it kill the son of the owner                                                      | the son of that builder shall be put to death |

The laws run in families. Law 218 is law 215 with one thing changed, the outcome of the operation. Law 230 is law 229 with one thing added. The historian **Marc Van De Mieroop** has called these two moves "opposition" and "pointillism", and they are how Mesopotamian scholars built every kind of list, from omens to the names of gods. They also explain the oddities: a law on threshing with goats, an implausible job for an unruly animal, comes where it does because the laws before it had worked through the other beasts that threshed.

## Not equal before the law

The best-known principle in the Code is retaliation in kind, the _lex talionis_ or **[eye for an eye](kloom:e/eye-for-an-eye)** of laws 196 and 200. But it applies only between equals. The Code knows three ranks of person: the _awīlum_, something like a gentleman; the _muškēnum_, a commoner, whom King translated as a "freed man"; and the slave. Blind a gentleman and you lose your eye. Blind a commoner and you pay a fine. Blind a slave and you pay his owner half the slave's price. Punishment could pass to children, as law 230 shows, and law 210 orders the death of an assailant's daughter.

Some principles are more familiar. The first two laws punish an accuser who cannot prove his charge. Written evidence counts for a great deal, especially in contracts, and intention matters. And the epilogue makes a promise. "That the strong might not injure the weak, in order to protect the widows and orphans," Hammurabi says, he set up "these my precious words, written upon my memorial stone". Any wronged man who had a case at law was to come and stand before the stele and its words: "the inscription will explain his case to him."

## A code, or something else?

Scheil's word "code" has stuck, but Assyriologists have doubted it since the 1950s. Thousands of court records survive from Old Babylonian Mesopotamia, and not one cites the Code, while many cite royal decrees of another kind, and some rulings go against it. The Code also leaves out whole areas of life, among them the work of shepherds, on which Babylonia's economy rested. One view, gaining ground, is that it is a work of scholarship about how a just ruling is formed, rather than legislation a judge applied. Scribes certainly treated it as a classic. They copied it for more than a thousand years, and over fifty manuscripts are known.

It was not the first such collection. The **[Code of Ur-Nammu](kloom:e/code-of-ur-nammu)**, written in Sumerian some three centuries earlier, already had a prologue and rulings in "if" and "then", though it settled most injuries with fines of silver rather than retaliation. Nor was it the last. Its likeness to the **[Covenant Code](kloom:e/covenant-code)** of the Book of Exodus, down to the rules for an ox that gores a man, has been argued over since 1902. Most scholars now put that likeness down to a shared legal tradition rather than borrowing. The next frame turns to the Hebrew Bible, where law would be given not by a king but by God.
