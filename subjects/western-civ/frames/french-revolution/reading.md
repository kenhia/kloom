By 1788 the French crown spent half its revenue servicing debts, many of them run up helping the Americans fight Britain. The nobles and the courts refused every new tax, saying only the **[Estates-General](kloom:e/estates-general-of-1789)** could grant one, and it had last met in 1614. **[Louis XVI](kloom:e/louis-xvi)** summoned it, and on 5 May 1789 about 1,200 deputies gathered at Versailles: the clergy, the nobility and the commoners of the Third Estate, which spoke for some 98 percent of the people and had been given twice as many seats. Whether the estates would vote as three houses or by head decided everything, and the king would not say. On 17 June the Third Estate declared itself the **National Assembly**. Three days later, locked out of its hall, it met in a tennis court and swore "never to separate" until France had a constitution. On 14 July, in the **[storming of the Bastille](kloom:e/storming-of-the-bastille)**, a Paris crowd hunting gunpowder took a royal fortress holding seven prisoners; 98 of the attackers died. The **[French Revolution](kloom:e/french-revolution)** had begun.

## Seventeen articles

On 26 August 1789 the Assembly adopted the **[Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen](kloom:e/declaration-of-the-rights-of-man-and-of-the-citizen)**. It made the rights of the governed, not the king's will, the reason for government. In the English translation of France's Constitutional Council, which still applies it as law:

| Article | What it says                                                                                                                                                        |
| ------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1       | Men are born and remain free and equal in rights. Social distinctions may be based only on considerations of the common good.                                       |
| 3       | The principle of any Sovereignty lies primarily in the Nation. No corporate body, no individual may exercise any authority that does not expressly emanate from it. |
| 6       | The Law is the expression of the general will. All citizens have the right to take part, personally or through their representatives, in its making.                |
| 16      | Any society in which no provision is made for guaranteeing rights or for the separation of powers, has no Constitution.                                             |

It left people out. The Assembly soon divided the nation into "active" citizens, men of 25 who paid a tax worth three days' wages, and "passive" ones: about 4.3 million of some 29 million French people could vote. Women were passive by definition. In September 1791 the playwright **[Olympe de Gouges](kloom:e/olympe-de-gouges)** answered with a _Declaration of the Rights of Woman and of the Female Citizen_, article by article. Its tenth, in Lynn Hunt's translation, says that "woman has the right to mount the scaffold, so she should have the right equally to mount the rostrum." The colonies' planters persuaded the Assembly that the rights of man did not reach their slaves. Only after the enslaved of Saint-Domingue rose in 1791 did the Convention abolish slavery, on 4 February 1794; it was restored in 1802.

## The Terror

The monarchy fell in August 1792 and a republic was declared in September. The king was tried by the Convention and guillotined on 21 January 1793. With foreign armies at the borders and civil war in the west, the Convention began the **[Reign of Terror](kloom:e/reign-of-terror)**. It set up a **Revolutionary Tribunal** in March and the **Committee of Public Safety** in April, and on 17 September passed the **[Law of Suspects](kloom:e/law-of-suspects)**, which ordered the arrest of anyone who "by their conduct, associations, comments, or writings" had shown themselves "enemies of liberty". Families of émigré nobles were suspect by kinship alone. The Tribunal sat with judges and a jury, and there was no appeal. On 10 June 1794 the Law of 22 Prairial, pressed by **[Maximilien Robespierre](kloom:e/maximilien-robespierre)**'s allies, stripped it down:

| The Tribunal    | Before June 1794              | Under the law of 22 Prairial                               |
| --------------- | ----------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------- |
| Jury            | twelve jurors                 | nine, of whom seven sufficed                               |
| Witnesses       | heard                         | dropped wherever "material or moral" proof seemed to exist |
| Defense counsel | allowed                       | given to "calumniated patriots", not to "conspirators"     |
| Penalty         | as the law set for each crime | death, for every offense it judged                         |

The historian Donald Greer, counting the records in 1935, found 16,594 death sentences carried out in the Terror, 2,639 of them in Paris; perhaps 10,000 to 20,000 more died in prison or were killed without trial. The war in the Vendée is counted apart: over 4,000 people were drowned in the Loire at Nantes, and estimates of the region's dead run from about 117,000 to 250,000, all of them disputed. Olympe de Gouges was guillotined in November 1793, and the chemist **[Antoine Lavoisier](kloom:e/antoine-lavoisier)**, as a former tax farmer, in May 1794. Robespierre fell on 9 Thermidor, 27 July, and went to the guillotine the next day.

## Remaking the world by reason

The Revolution redrew France as 83 departments and set out to measure the world anew: the meter was to be a ten-millionth of the distance from pole to equator, and the decimal measures were made law in 1795. A new **[French Republican calendar](kloom:e/french-republican-calendar)**, adopted in October 1793, counted its Year I from the founding of the Republic, 22 September 1792. It had twelve months of thirty days, each cut into three weeks of ten days, with five or six days left over at the year's end, and the day itself was divided into ten hours of a hundred minutes, as the plate draws. Decimal time was dropped in 1795; the calendar lasted until 1806.

## What was it?

On 9 November 1799, the 18th of Brumaire in the new calendar, General **[Napoleon](kloom:e/napoleon)** Bonaparte seized power, and most historians end the Revolution there. The argument over what it was began at once. In 1790 **[Edmund Burke](kloom:e/edmund-burke)** mourned in his _Reflections_ that "the age of chivalry is gone. That of sophisters, economists, and calculators has succeeded", and mocked the departments as squares that needed only "an accurate land-surveyor". **[Thomas Paine](kloom:e/thomas-paine)** answered in _Rights of Man_ that Burke "pities the plumage, but forgets the dying bird." In 1856 **[Alexis de Tocqueville](kloom:e/alexis-de-tocqueville)** found the old regime's centralized state living on in the new one, and saw that "the most critical moment for bad governments is the one which witnesses their first steps toward reform." Marxist historians from Georges Lefebvre to Albert Soboul read 1789 as the victory of the bourgeoisie; Alfred Cobban and then François Furet, from 1955 and 1978, read it as a revolution of politics and ideas, whose violence was there from the start.

The trail _Revolutions_ follows the idea before and after: England in 1688, Haiti in 1791, and Europe in 1848.
