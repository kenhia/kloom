On 8 April 1341, on the Capitoline Hill in Rome, two Roman senators set a crown of laurel on the head of a thirty-six-year-old poet from Tuscany, Francesco Petrarca, known in English as **[Petrarch](kloom:e/petrarch)**. By the count Wikipedia's article takes from the historian Roberto Weiss, he was only the second poet crowned since antiquity, after Albertino Mussato at Padua. Four years later, in the chapter library of the cathedral at Verona, he found a manuscript of **[Cicero](kloom:e/cicero)**'s letters to his friend Atticus, sixteen books of them, in a collection no one then knew existed. The copyists could not make out the old hand, so Petrarch, ill at the time, copied the whole volume himself. Then he wrote to its author. "I have read thy letters through to the end most eagerly," his letter to Cicero begins, in Mario Cosenza's translation of 1910, and it scolds the dead man for the quarrels and ambitions the letters reveal. It is dated "in the land of the living", Verona, 16 June 1345, "in the thirteen hundred and forty-fifth year from the birth of that God whom thou never knewest."

![The frontispiece Simone Martini painted around 1336 for Petrarch's own manuscript of Virgil: the poet sits writing under a tree, gazing upward, while a soldier, a vine-dresser and a shepherd with his ewes stand for the subjects of his poems, and two painted scrolls carry Latin verses](petrarch-virgil.jpg)

Petrarch wrote such letters to Livy, Seneca, Virgil and Homer too. In the one to Livy he says he reads him "to forget these regions, these times, and these customs", and lives in his pages with the Scipios and Catos "and not with the thievish company of today among whom I was born under an evil star." Historians credit him with the idea of a "dark" age between Rome's fall and his own, an idea most modern scholars find misleading. The movement that followed him is **[Renaissance humanism](kloom:e/renaissance-humanism)**.

## How a humanist worked

Its school subjects were the _studia humanitatis_: grammar, rhetoric, poetry, history and moral philosophy, read in Latin and, where a teacher could be found, in Greek. [Florence](kloom:e/florence)'s chancellor, **[Coluccio Salutati](kloom:e/coluccio-salutati)**, invited the Byzantine scholar **[Manuel Chrysoloras](kloom:e/manuel-chrysoloras)** to teach Greek there, and he arrived in the winter of 1397. His pupil Leonardo Bruni wrote that no one had studied Greek in northern Italy for seven hundred years. Chrysoloras's grammar, the _Erotemata_, was printed in 1484 and later studied by Erasmus.

The texts themselves had to be found. Medieval monasteries held them, often in a single damaged copy, and the work went in steps:

| Step   | What was done                                                                                           |
| ------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Find   | Poggio at St Gall, 1416: Quintilian complete, part of Valerius Flaccus, Asconius on Cicero's speeches   |
| Copy   | Petrarch copies Cicero's letters at Verona himself, 1345; Poggio copies his finds "with my own hand"    |
| Spread | Niccoli's copy of the Lucretius Poggio found becomes the model for more than fifty others               |
| Emend  | Valla corrects corrupt passages of Livy and attacks his rivals' corrections, in his _Emendationes_      |
| Test   | Valla asks whether a famous document could have been written when it claims, and answers from its Latin |

**[Poggio Bracciolini](kloom:e/poggio-bracciolini)**, a scribe of the papal court left idle at the Council of Constance, wrote to a friend in December 1416 that the Quintilian at St Gall had lain "in a most loathsome and dreary dungeon at the very foundations of one of the towers". In January 1417, in a German monastery he never named, probably Fulda, he found **[Lucretius](kloom:e/lucretius)**'s [_De rerum natura_](kloom:e/de-rerum-natura), the long Latin poem that sets out Epicurus's world of atoms and void. His manuscript is lost. The Florentine collector **[Niccolò de' Niccoli](kloom:e/niccolo-de-niccoli)** copied it, and that copy is now in the Laurentian Library. The plate draws the poem's family tree as editors now reconstruct it. Niccoli died owning some 800 manuscripts, and Cosimo of the **[House of Medici](kloom:e/house-of-medici)** took them over for his debts and made them the core of the library he built at San Marco.

The humanists copied in a new hand, their _littera antica_, revived from the Caroline minuscule of Charlemagne's scribes, which they took for ancient Roman writing. **[Humanist minuscule](kloom:e/humanist-minuscule)** became the model for the roman type of printed books.

## Valla and the Donation

The **[Donation of Constantine](kloom:e/donation-of-constantine)** claimed to be a decree in which the emperor, cured of leprosy by Pope Sylvester, gave the pope "the city of Rome, and all the provinces, places and cities of Italy and the western regions". Popes had cited it since 1054. In 1440 **[Lorenzo Valla](kloom:e/lorenzo-valla)**, secretary to King Alfonso of Aragon, then at war with Pope Eugenius IV, tested it word by word. In Christopher Coleman's translation of 1922:

| The Donation says                                                    | Valla answers                                                                                                         |
| -------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| "together with all our satraps"                                      | "Whoever heard of satraps being mentioned in the councils of the Romans?"                                             |
| "all the standards and banners"                                      | "But what 'banners' [banna] may signify, I do not discover."                                                          |
| the Senate's "shoes with felt socks", "distinguished by white linen" | "as though socks were of linen!" Martial's socks were goat hair                                                       |
| Constantinople is one of the four patriarchal sees                   | it "was not yet a patriarchate, nor a see, nor a Christian city, nor named Constantinople, nor founded, nor planned!" |

The first two are Latin of the wrong age. Coleman notes that Roman officials were called satraps only in the middle of the eighth century, and that _banda_, for flags, is an eighth-century word, which is where the forgery is now dated. Valla was not alone: Nicholas of Cusa also declared the Donation a forgery, and Reginald Pecock in England reached the same verdict on his own. But Valla showed how to prove it. Ulrich von Hutten printed the treatise in 1517, and Protestant readers took it up.

## A break, or a turn?

In 1860 the Swiss historian **[Jacob Burckhardt](kloom:e/jacob-burckhardt)** made the Renaissance a waking. In the Middle Ages, he wrote, in S. G. C. Middlemore's translation, human consciousness "lay dreaming or half awake beneath a common veil", and "in Italy this veil first melted into air." In 1927 Charles Homer Haskins answered with _The Renaissance of the Twelfth Century_: "the continuity of history rejects such sharp and violent contrasts between successive periods". The finds bear him out in part. The oldest manuscript of Lucretius was copied in a Carolingian scriptorium, and the books Poggio rescued had been kept for centuries by monks. When Stephen Greenblatt's _The Swerve_ (2011) made Poggio's Lucretius the spark of the modern world, the historian John Monfasani called its Burckhardtian reading "questionable". The next frame turns from texts to pictures: painting from Giotto to Michelangelo.
