In 1781 **[Richard Arkwright](kloom:e/richard-arkwright)** opened a cotton mill on Miller Street in **[Manchester](kloom:e/manchester)** with a steam engine, at first only to pump water back over its waterwheel; within a few years engines from Boulton and Watt were driving mills directly, and a market town became the first city of the factory. The census counted 70,409 people there in 1801 and 303,382 in 1851, more than four times as many by our arithmetic (the boundary changed along the way). By 1853 Manchester had 108 cotton mills, and the warehouses and exchanges that sold their yarn earned it the name **[Cottonopolis](kloom:e/cottonopolis)**. In 1842 the German **[Friedrich Engels](kloom:e/friedrich-engels)** was sent there to work in a mill in which his family had an interest. What he saw went into a book, **[_The Condition of the Working Class in England_](kloom:e/the-condition-of-the-working-class-in-england)**, published in Leipzig in 1845. In Paris in 1844 he began a lifelong partnership with **[Karl Marx](kloom:e/karl-marx)**; their _Communist Manifesto_ followed in 1848.

## How a mill worked

An early nineteenth-century mill was a tall, narrow box, 25 to 68 meters long and up to eight stories high, with its engine house inside. A low-pressure beam engine, of 48 horsepower on average in 1835, turned a vertical shaft that ran up the building, and on each floor bevel gears drove a horizontal _line shaft_ along the ceiling, from which belts or gears turned the machines.

The spinning machine of Manchester was the **[spinning mule](kloom:e/spinning-mule)**, Samuel Crompton's of 1779, a cross of Arkwright's **[water frame](kloom:e/water-frame)** and the spinning jenny. Its spindles rode on a carriage that ran out about 1.5 meters from the frame, drawing out and twisting the yarn, and ran back to wind it on, about four times a minute. Each pair of mules, with 250 to 350 spindles each in the 1830s, was worked by a _minder_ with two boys, the _piecers_, who joined broken threads as the carriage moved. In 1833 the largest mill, McConnel and Company's in Ancoats, employed 1,545 people; in 1841 the average Lancashire mill employed 193.

The day was set by the mill, not the worker. "The operative must be in the mill at half-past five in the morning; if he comes a couple of minutes too late, he is fined," Engels wrote, and he copied out a set of mill rules, here in Florence Kelley's translation:

| Rule                                                        | Fine                       |
| ----------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------- |
| Absent after the doors close, ten minutes after work begins | 3d. per loom               |
| A weaver absent while the machinery is in motion            | 3d. for each hour and loom |
| Leaving the room without the overlooker's permission        | 3d.                        |
| Failing to bring scissors                                   | 1d. a day                  |
| Speaking to another, singing or whistling                   | 6d.                        |

Parliament limited the day by stages. The **[Factory Acts](kloom:e/factory-acts)** of 1802 and 1819 had gone largely unenforced. The act of 1833 barred children under nine from textile mills (silk excepted), held those aged nine to thirteen to eight hours a day and those under eighteen to twelve, with no night work, required two hours of school a day for the youngest, and created four paid inspectors to enforce it. The Ten Hours Act of 1847 held women and young people to 58 hours a week from May 1848, which was meant in practice to hold the men who worked beside them too; it took another act, in 1850, to close its loopholes.

## The city

Engels described **[Little Ireland](kloom:e/little-ireland)**, by the river Medlock: "two groups of about two hundred cottages, built chiefly back to back, in which live about four thousand human beings, most of them Irish", with "one usually inaccessible privy" for every 120 people. The cholera that reached Britain in 1831 alarmed the town's sanitary police about Little Ireland, he wrote, but left it "in almost the same state"; in 1848 and 1849 cholera killed some 52,000 people in England and Wales. In 1842 **[Edwin Chadwick](kloom:e/edwin-chadwick)**'s _Report on the Sanitary Condition of the Labouring Population_ printed the average ages at death by class:

![Bar chart of the average age at death by class in 1842: in Manchester, 38 years for gentry, 20 for tradesmen and 17 for laborers; in Rutland, 52, 41 and 38](age-at-death.svg)

| Class                        | Manchester | Rutland |

| Professional persons, gentry |         38 |      52 |
| Tradesmen                    |         20 |      41 |
| Mechanics and laborers       |         17 |      38 |

An average age at death is not a life expectancy. Most of the gap was children: the same report says that more than 57 percent of the children of Manchester's laboring classes died before the age of five. **[William Farr](kloom:e/william-farr)**, at the General Register Office, compared mortality as a rate among people of the same ages. Chadwick's report led to the Public Health Act 1848, which set up a General Board of Health with Chadwick on it, and local boards to build drains and supply water.

## Better off or worse?

Historians have argued since Engels whether the factory made workers better or worse off. R. M. Hartwell made the case in 1961 for "the rising standard of living in England, 1800–1850"; Charles Feinstein's estimates of 1998 found that the average working-class family's standard of living rose by less than 15 percent between the 1780s and the 1850s.

## Where the cotton came from

Manchester spun cotton that enslaved people grew. In 1860 Lancashire's mills imported 1,391 million pounds of raw cotton, 1,116 million of it from the United States, about 80 percent by our arithmetic, almost all from the slave states of the South. When the **[American Civil War](kloom:e/american-civil-war)** cut the supply, the industry, already overstocked, fell into the **[Lancashire Cotton Famine](kloom:e/lancashire-cotton-famine)** of 1861 to 1865, and the mill workers went from the most prosperous in Britain to the most impoverished. In January 1863 **[Abraham Lincoln](kloom:e/abraham-lincoln)** thanked the "workingmen of Manchester" for backing the Union, calling it "an instance of sublime Christian heroism which has not been surpassed in any age or in any country."
