In the spring of 334 BC **[Alexander the Great](kloom:e/alexander-the-great)**, king of Macedon, twenty-one years old and once the pupil of **[Aristotle](kloom:e/aristotle)**, crossed into Asia to make war on **[Darius III](kloom:e/darius-iii)**, king of the Persian **[Achaemenid Empire](kloom:e/achaemenid-empire)**. Eleven years later he died at **[Babylon](kloom:e/babylon)**, at thirty-two, ruling from Greece and Egypt to the Indus. He left no grown heir and no working government, and his generals spent forty years fighting over what he had taken. What lasted was not his empire but a language and a way of living in cities, which spread with his armies from the Nile to the Hindu Kush.

## The march

The main sources were written long after him: the fullest, **[Arrian](kloom:e/arrian)**'s _Anabasis_, in the second century AD, from the lost memoirs of two of his officers. From them the campaign runs:

| Year   | Where                | What happened                                                         |
| ------ | -------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 334 BC | The Granicus         | First victory, over the Persian governors of Asia Minor               |
| 333 BC | Issus                | Darius defeated; he fled, leaving his family behind                   |
| 332 BC | Tyre                 | Taken after a long siege; by Arrian's count 8,000 killed, 30,000 sold |
| 331 BC | Egypt                | Received as pharaoh; Alexandria founded on the coast                  |
| 331 BC | Gaugamela            | Darius defeated again; Babylon and Susa surrendered                   |
| 330 BC | Persepolis           | The palace of the Persian kings burned                                |
| 329–27 | Bactria and Sogdiana | Two years of war in Central Asia; marriage to Roxana                  |
| 326 BC | The Hydaspes         | Victory over the Indian king Porus; then the army refused to go on    |
| 325 BC | The Gedrosian desert | The march home along the coast; many died                             |
| 323 BC | Babylon              | Death after twelve days of fever, on 10 or 11 June                    |

![A battered floor mosaic of a cavalry battle: on the left a bareheaded young rider drives his lance through an enemy horseman, and on the right a bearded king in a chariot turns back toward him as his driver whips the horses away, under a forest of raised spears](alexander-mosaic.jpg)

A floor mosaic from the House of the Faun at Pompeii, laid about two centuries later and believed to copy a Greek painting of his own time, shows Alexander charging Darius, at Issus or at Gaugamela. The plate draws the route through those stations on a grid of longitude and latitude. By our arithmetic, the straight lines between them add up to about 14,500 kilometers; the roads, the detours and the years of fighting made the march far longer. At the river Hyphasis, the modern Beas, his officer Coenus spoke for the army, in E. J. Chinnock's translation of 1884: "Do not lead us now against our will; for thou wilt no longer find us the same men in regard to dangers." Alexander shut himself in his tent for three days. Then, Arrian says, the sacrifices for the crossing were unfavorable, and he turned back.

Others paid for the march. Thebes, which revolted in 335 BC, was razed before he left Greece, and Persepolis was given to his soldiers to loot for days before its palace burned. The army that conquered Asia was also an army of plunder. Nor were the conquerors of one mind about what they had won. At Opis, in 324 BC, his Macedonians mutinied again, angry at his Persian dress and his Persian officers; at the feast that reconciled them he prayed, Arrian says, "that harmony and community of rule might exist between the Macedonians and Persians."

## After Alexander

His generals, the **[Diadochi](kloom:e/diadochi)** or "successors", divided the empire at Babylon and then fought over it until the battle of Ipsus in 301 BC settled the map. Three dynasties came out of it. The **[Ptolemaic Kingdom](kloom:e/ptolemaic-kingdom)** in Egypt lasted until 30 BC, when its last queen, Cleopatra, died. The **[Seleucid Empire](kloom:e/seleucid-empire)**, at its height from Syria to Bactria, shrank to a rump in Syria that Pompey abolished in 64 BC. The **[Antigonid dynasty](kloom:e/antigonid-dynasty)** ruled Macedonia until Rome conquered it.

Historians call the three centuries after him **[Hellenistic](kloom:e/hellenistic-period)**, a modern word, from _Hellas_, for the lands that came under Greek influence. Greek became the common language of government and trade from Egypt to Afghanistan, in the form called **[Koine](kloom:e/koine-greek)**, "common", which is the Greek of the New Testament. In Egypt a decree of Ptolemy V, the Rosetta Stone, was cut in Greek beside two Egyptian scripts. At **[Ai-Khanoum](kloom:e/ai-khanoum)**, on the Oxus in northern Afghanistan, founded around 300 BC by the Seleucids and not, as was once thought, by Alexander, French archaeologists found a gymnasium, a theater for some six thousand, and a stone on which a man named Klearchos had set up maxims he copied at Delphi. The last five read, in our translation: "As a child, be well behaved. As a youth, self-controlled. In middle age, just. As an old man, of good counsel. At the end, without grief."

## The Library

Arrian tells, as a story he believes, that when Alexander laid out **[Alexandria](kloom:e/alexandria)** the line of its walls was marked with the soldiers' barley, for want of anything else. Under the Ptolemies it had a royal research institute, the Mouseion, and the **[Library of Alexandria](kloom:e/library-of-alexandria)**, whose chief librarians included Eratosthenes. How big it was, nobody knows, and the ancient numbers disagree:

| Source                               | Rolls                                       |
| ------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------- |
| _Letter of Aristeas_, 2nd century BC | more than 200,000, with 500,000 the aim     |
| Tzetzes, 12th century AD             | 490,000 in the palace, 42,800 outside it    |
| Seneca, quoting Livy                 | 40,000 burned in the Alexandrian war, 48 BC |
| Aulus Gellius, 2nd century AD        | 700,000, all burned                         |

The historian Roger Bagnall thinks none of them deserves credence. All the Greek that survives from authors living before the end of the second century BC is about 3.8 million words, a few hundred rolls; if, as one historian estimated, forty times as much once existed, that is 10,000 to 15,000 rolls. Nor is there one villain for its end. Julius Caesar's fire in 48 BC, Aurelian's sack of the palace quarter in AD 272 or 273, the destruction of the Serapeum in 391 and the Arab conquest of 642 have all been blamed, the last in a story first told five hundred years after it. Bagnall's suspect is neglect: papyrus decays in Alexandria's damp air, and nobody went on recopying.

Euclid wrote his _Elements_ there, and tradition puts the making of the Septuagint, the Greek translation of the Hebrew Bible, there too. The next frame stays at the Library, with Eratosthenes measuring the Earth.
