The best-known water number in the argument is per answer: a bottle of water for a short conversation with a chatbot. In 2025 two AI companies and the chief executive of a third published much smaller ones. The published figures for one response now run from about a quarter of a milliliter to more than half a liter, a range of about two thousandfold by our arithmetic. Most of that gap is not an error on either side. The estimates count different things, for different models, in different years. This frame describes them as of November 2026.

## The high estimates

In April 2023 Pengfei Li, Jianyi Yang, Mohammad Islam and Shaolei Ren, of the **[University of California, Riverside](kloom:e/university-of-california-riverside)** and the University of Texas at Arlington, posted the preprint "Making AI Less 'Thirsty'." Its first version said **[ChatGPT](kloom:e/chatgpt)** "needs to 'drink' a 500ml bottle of water for a simple conversation of roughly 20-50 questions and answers." By October 2023 the claim was narrower: **[GPT-3](kloom:e/gpt-3)**, the older model, consumes 500 milliliters for "roughly 10-50 responses, depending on when and where it is deployed." The final version, of March 2025, says "medium-length responses" and calls the estimate conservative; the paper appeared in _Communications of the ACM_ that year.

The method is an estimate built from published averages. It takes about 0.004 kilowatt-hours of server electricity per response, from the figure **[OpenAI](kloom:e/openai)**'s researchers gave for GPT-3 in 2020 (0.4 kilowatt-hours for 100 pages), and multiplies it by **[Microsoft](kloom:e/microsoft)**'s reported efficiency ratios for each of its data center locations and by the water the local grid's power plants consume per kilowatt-hour. For the American average the paper's table gives 16.9 milliliters a medium-length response, 2.2 of them on site and 14.7 at power plants. Training is counted separately (5.4 million liters for GPT-3), not spread over responses.

In September 2024 **[The Washington Post](kloom:e/the-washington-post)** worked with Ren on a sharper version: writing one 100-word email with ChatGPT on **[GPT-4](kloom:e/gpt-4)** "requires 519 milliliters of water, a little more than 1 bottle," and 0.14 kilowatt-hours of electricity, at an average American data center. That energy figure was the researchers' estimate; the article points to the paper for its method. By our arithmetic it is 35 times the paper's GPT-3 assumption, about 400 times what OpenAI's chief executive later claimed for an average ChatGPT query and nearly 600 times **[Google](kloom:e/google)**'s measured median for its **[Gemini](kloom:e/gemini-language-model)** apps, below. None of those is a measurement of GPT-4 in 2024.

## The low estimates

In August 2025 Google reported its own measurement of the median text prompt to Gemini in May 2025: 0.24 watt-hours and 0.26 milliliters of water, "five drops." The energy is measured on Google's machines, idle capacity included. The water is the energy, less the cooling overhead, times Google's fleet average of 1.15 liters per kilowatt-hour consumed on site. Power-plant water is outside its boundary, which takes in what is "under Google's operational control," and so is training. The paper, by Google's own staff, was posted as a preprint. In June 2025 **[Sam Altman](kloom:e/sam-altman)**, OpenAI's chief executive, wrote that the average ChatGPT query uses "about 0.34 watt-hours" and "about 0.000085 gallons of water," about 0.32 milliliters, with no method. In July 2025 **[Mistral AI](kloom:e/mistral-ai)** published a life-cycle analysis made with the consultancy Carbone 4 and ADEME, France's ecological transition agency, and reviewed by two other consultancies: 45 milliliters for a 400-token response. Mistral says its figures include upstream impacts such as making the servers.

Each side reads the gap its own way. Ren's group argues that leaving out the power plant's water leaves out the larger part. Google set its 0.26 milliliters against earlier estimates of 45 to 50, which count the power plant and, in Mistral's case, the factory. Adding power-plant water at the American data center average of 4.52 liters per kilowatt-hour would bring Google's figure to about 1.3 milliliters by our arithmetic: five times larger, still far below 16.9. The rest of the gap lies between the models, hardware and years compared, which no source has separated. Google chose the median because a few costly prompts skew the average upward; a total, though, is the average times the count.

## What a per-answer figure cannot say

A small number multiplies. At an invented round billion responses a day, 0.26 milliliters is 260,000 liters a day; 16.9 milliliters is 16.9 million, or about 6 billion liters a year, by our arithmetic. Supporters set the small figures against daily life, as Google's "five drops" and Altman's "one fifteenth of a teaspoon" do; the _Post_'s own graphic put a pound of beef at about 6,992 liters. Opponents answer with geography. "There is no 'national reservoir,'" Ren told Vermont legislators in February 2026: data centers draw mostly drinking water from municipal systems, so the total lands on particular towns, which _Small water systems, big campuses_ follows.

We found no independent measurement of the water behind a production chatbot's response. Google's paper lists four earlier studies that measured energy per prompt on hardware, all of open models and none of water. Until companies publish how many responses they serve and their power plants' water, per-answer figures stay estimates and claims. The trail returns to _Where a data center's water goes_.
