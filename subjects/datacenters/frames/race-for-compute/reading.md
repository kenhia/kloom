"The United States is in a race to achieve global dominance in artificial intelligence." So begins the **[AI Action Plan](kloom:e/ai-action-plan)** that President **[Donald Trump](kloom:e/donald-trump)**'s White House published on July 23, 2025. "Whoever has the largest AI ecosystem will set global AI standards and reap broad economic and military benefits." The plan's answer was to build: chip factories, power plants and data centers, faster, it said, than the permitting system allowed. In its own words, "Build, Baby, Build!" Of all the reasons given for the buildings in this subject, this is the broadest, and the hardest to check, because most of it is about the future.

## The case as its advocates make it

The argument has three links. First, the capability of AI models has grown with the computing used to train them, and that computing lives in data centers; the history of that curve is told in _AI compute and hardware_. Second, the United States and **[China](kloom:e/china)** are the two countries building it at scale. Third, the country that falls behind loses twice: in an economy where AI does more of the work, and in a military that uses it. Gregory Allen of the **[Center for Strategic and International Studies](kloom:e/center-for-strategic-and-international-studies)**, a Washington think tank, read the first American chip controls in 2022 as if the United States were telling China: "AI technology is the future. We and our allies are going there. You can't come."

From that follow the plan's proposals for data centers: new exclusions from review under the National Environmental Policy Act for "data center-related actions that normally do not have a significant effect on the environment," faster federal permits, and federal land for campuses and their power plants. America's permitting rules, it says, "make it almost impossible to build this infrastructure in the United States with the speed that is required."

## The parts that are measured

The rules themselves are a matter of record:

| Date          | Step                                                                                                                                                |
| ------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Feb. 2022     | China approves "East Data, West Computing": 8 national computing hubs and 10 data center clusters, shifting data centers toward its western regions |
| Oct. 2022     | US Commerce Department requires licenses to export advanced AI chips, and tools to make them, to China                                              |
| Jan. 2026     | Commerce reviews sales of Nvidia's H200 and similar chips to China case by case, instead of presuming denial                                        |
| Nov. 19, 2026 | Commerce requires licenses for data centers abroad that supply large amounts of computing to Chinese companies                                      |

In practice the rules have moved back and forth. Documents released in November 2026 by Rep. Brian Mast, who chairs the House Foreign Affairs Committee, show the **[Bureau of Industry and Security](kloom:e/bureau-of-industry-and-security)**, which writes the **[export control](kloom:e/export-control)** rules, had approved licenses for more than 879,000 AI chips to Chinese companies by May 2026. **[Nvidia](kloom:e/nvidia)** said that "despite receiving some licenses, we have since stopped shipping H200s to China," and CNBC reported that Beijing itself had limited large-scale imports.

How far ahead is the United States? The **[International Energy Agency](kloom:e/international-energy-agency)** estimated that in 2024 American data centers used 45% of the electricity data centers used worldwide, China's 25% and Europe's 15%. That is a modeled estimate, not a meter reading. The plate draws a second measure from **[Epoch AI](kloom:e/epoch-ai)**, a research group that tracks AI models: the training compute of the 484 notable models since 2010 for which it gives a figure, 285 from American organizations and 88 from Chinese ones. Its largest American entry, OpenAI's GPT-6 Astra of 2026, is estimated at nearly 40 times the largest Chinese one, ByteDance's Doubao-pro, by our arithmetic. Epoch says its "Likely" estimates, which both of those are, may be off by a factor of ten.

## What winning is worth

Nobody has measured the prize yet. In 2023 **[Goldman Sachs](kloom:e/goldman-sachs)**, the investment bank, and **[McKinsey & Company](kloom:e/mckinsey-and-company)**, the consultancy, published projections of their own; in 2024 the MIT economist **[Daron Acemoglu](kloom:e/daron-acemoglu)** published a model, later peer-reviewed, that works task by task through what AI can do. They are far apart:

| Who, when                     | What it is                 | Estimate                                                                             |
| ----------------------------- | -------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Goldman Sachs, Apr. 2023      | a bank's projection        | world GDP 7% higher; productivity growth up 1.5 points a year, over ten years        |
| McKinsey & Company, June 2023 | a consultancy's projection | $2.6–4.4 trillion a year; labor productivity growth up 0.1–0.6 points a year to 2040 |
| Acemoglu, 2024                | an economist's model       | total factor productivity up no more than 0.66% in all, over ten years               |

Acemoglu expects "even more modest" gains, below 0.53%, once harder tasks are counted. Note the units: Goldman's 1.5 points is added every year, Acemoglu's 0.66% is the total for the decade.

## The critics' reply

Critics dispute what the race argument is used for. The **[AI Now Institute](kloom:e/ai-now-institute)**, a research group that studies the power of technology companies, argued in 2025 that the US-China "arms race" framing shields AI companies: it "insulates them from regulatory friction by framing any calls for accountability as not just anti-innovation but harming national interests." The plan's own words reach local and state control. It recommends that federal AI funding not go to states with "burdensome AI regulations," though it also says Washington should not interfere with "prudent laws that are not unduly restrictive to innovation," and the exclusions from review it proposes are for data center projects.

The second reply is efficiency. In December 2024 the Chinese company **[DeepSeek](kloom:e/deepseek)** reported training its V3 model on 2,048 Nvidia H800 chips, a final run it priced at $5.576 million by assuming a rental of $2 a chip-hour, leaving out "prior research and ablation experiments." That is DeepSeek's claim, and it covers only the last run. When DeepSeek's chatbot became widely used in January 2025, Nvidia's shares fell by about a sixth in a day. If capable models need less computing, fewer buildings may be needed, and the race argument weakens; if cheaper AI is used more, more may be needed.

## What is not known

Whether capability keeps rising with compute, whether the controls slow China or speed its own chipmaking, and whether any of the projected productivity appears in the statistics are all still open. Which way efficiency cuts is the subject of _Efficiency and the rebound effect_; how Washington turned the race into policy for data centers is _The federal push for data centers_.
