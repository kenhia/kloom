On March 18, 2025, the Board of Supervisors of **[Loudoun County](kloom:e/loudoun-county-virginia)**, **[Virginia](kloom:e/virginia)**, voted 7 to 2 to stop approving **[data centers](kloom:e/data-center)** by right. For a quarter of a century a data center that met the written standards of the county's **[zoning](kloom:e/zoning)** ordinance, in a district that allowed it, had needed only a review of its site plan by county staff: no public hearing and no vote. In 2000 the county's zoning administrator had ruled that data centers counted as offices, and a 2014 amendment named them a use allowed by right in 5 of the county's 41 districts. Since the vote, a new data center in the Industrial Park, General Industry and Mineral Resources–Heavy Industry districts needs a **[special exception](kloom:e/special-use-permit)**: hearings before the Planning Commission and the Board, and a vote that can go against it. The plate draws the two paths.

## What the board changed

| February 6, 2024  | began a review of the rules for data centers and electrical substations                                      |           |
| March 18, 2025    | required a special exception (ZOAM-2024-0001); made data centers a conditional use in the comprehensive plan | 7–2       |
| March 18, 2025    | let some pending applications continue under the old rules                                                   | 5–4       |
| September 2025    | began phase 2: siting, noise, building heights, substations, on-site power                                   |           |
| September 1, 2026 | asked the county attorney whether those exceptions could be ended                                            | unanimous |
| November 17, 2026 | paused its own votes on data center and substation applications                                              | 7–1–1     |

The second vote of March 2025, the grandfathering resolution, let an application accepted before February 12, 2025, continue by right if it stood more than 500 feet from homes and grew by no more than 5% in floor area. **[Juli Briskman](kloom:e/juli-briskman)**, the Algonkian District's supervisor, moved to send those applications to hearings too; her motion failed 4 to 5. Patch counted 19 applications that would keep staff review only, and three near homes that would not. By September 2026 the supervisors' own count was 17 still in review, about 11 million square feet.

## Why, by each side's account

The supervisors who voted for the change spoke for residents. "We have 400,000 some bosses, and they've asked us to do something," said Matt Letourneau, a Republican. Residents' complaints had grown with the buildings and with the substations and lines that served them (_Ashburn's power lines_ follows those). The county had not rewritten its comprehensive plan between 2001 and 2019, or its zoning ordinance between 2003 and 2023, Supervisor Michael Turner wrote in a paper: two decades that "precisely aligned" with the industry's growth. "Homeowners have also spent money on their homes," said the board's chair, **[Phyllis Randall](kloom:e/phyllis-randall)**.

The case for the old rules, as its defenders make it: by-right standards are not an absence of rules. The board itself wrote and approved them, and a by-right project still had to meet them. Companies bought land on the strength of them, and a predictable process, its lawyers argue, is part of what drew the industry, whose taxes the county's own budget for fiscal 2027 projects at $1.3 billion, 40% of the total. Kristen Umstattd, one of the two votes against, was "concerned in general about taking away by-right opportunities for businesses that have invested." McGuireWoods, a law firm that advises developers, told clients the change meant "increasing development hurdles," though approval remained possible.

## Since the vote

The second phase was not finished by the autumn of 2026, and the board decided not to keep voting on projects meanwhile. On November 17, 2026, it voted 7–1–1 (Umstattd against, Caleb Kershner abstaining) to pause its final votes on data center and substation applications until the new standards are adopted, expected in July 2027, for no more than the twelve months Virginia law allows. The county counted 33 active applications that it could affect. Substations needed for the grid's reliability may still come before the board. The county attorney, Leo Rogers, had advised that Virginia law permits no moratorium on a land use, and that the board could not pause by-right applications, grandfathered ones included.

At the hearing, residents described generators, noise and lines near their homes. The Data Center Coalition's representative warned of uncertainty for businesses, property owners and workers; a land-use lawyer argued that it amounted to a moratorium, which Virginia law forbids; EdgeCore said its approved project in Sterling, of more than $1 billion, needed a substation the pause could delay. Kershner said the board could already defer or reject any single application, and warned of the cost of litigation; asked whether the threatened lawsuits were credible, Rogers said: "I do not." By a supervisor's office's count, Loudoun then had about 245 data center buildings built or under construction, some 58 million square feet, and 55 million more approved or in review.

The state's own rules moved too. Bills on data centers filled the **[General Assembly](kloom:e/virginia-general-assembly)**'s 2026 session: one that passed lets the State Corporation Commission decide whether to shift more energy costs onto data centers, others dealt with water, siting, diesel generators and taxes, and a proposed statewide moratorium was carried over to 2027.

What is not yet known is what the second phase's standards will require, how many special exceptions have been granted or refused since March 2025 (the county publishes each case, but this reading found no tally), whether the grandfathered projects will all be built, and whether the pause holds in court. One county's path, from a ruling that data centers were offices to a hearing for each, is the general question of _Zoning a data center_: who gets to decide.
