A data center buys its electricity from a grid that burns gas and coal and runs nuclear plants, wind farms and solar panels all at once. How much of that grid's carbon belongs to the data center? The rules most companies follow, the **[Greenhouse Gas Protocol](kloom:e/ghg-protocol-corporate-standard)**'s _Scope 2 Guidance_ of 2015, give two answers, and a company that buys power under contracts must report both. In 2025 they differed for **[Meta](kloom:e/meta-platforms)** by a factor of 722: 7.8 million tonnes of CO₂ by one method, 10,861 tonnes by the other, both printed in Meta's own report, the smaller because Meta matches a whole year's use with contracts for clean power. Neither number is measured at a chimney. Both multiply metered electricity by an emissions factor, and the methods differ in which factor they allow.

## Two ways to count

The _location-based_ method uses the average emissions of the grid where the power is used. The _market-based_ method uses the company's contracts. A **[renewable energy certificate](kloom:e/renewable-energy-certificate-united-states)** stands for one megawatt-hour generated from a renewable source, and it can be sold apart from the electricity itself ("unbundled"). A **[power purchase agreement](kloom:e/power-purchase-agreement)** is a long-term contract to buy a generator's output, or its certificates, often from a plant built because of the contract. Under the 2015 rules a company may match a year's use with certificates bought anywhere in the same market, at any hour of the year.

## What the companies report

Every figure in the table is the company's own, computed from its meters and contracts by methods it chose; none is an independent measurement. By that accounting, each company's total has risen. **[Google](kloom:e/google)**'s 2026 report puts its emissions 81% above its 2019 base, and says that reaching its 2030 net-zero "moonshot is getting harder". **[Microsoft](kloom:e/microsoft)** said its total rose 25% partly because it stopped using "non-additional, unbundled renewable energy certificates" in February 2025: in the year to June 2025 its market-based scope 2 went from 0.26 to 2.71 million tonnes, a change in what is counted as much as in what was emitted. **[Amazon](kloom:e/amazon-company)** reports market-based figures only and aims for net zero by 2040. Meta's report of November 19, 2026, says it is "re-evaluating" its milestones, "including the 2030 timeframe for our net zero emissions goal". In July the Climate Group said Meta had left its RE100 pledge, "no longer able to meet the technical criteria due to investments made in new gas power". Most of each total is scope 3, the chips and buildings bought, which _Embodied carbon and e-waste_ follows.

## The recount, and the replies

In September 2024 **[_The Guardian_](kloom:e/the-guardian)** set four companies' location-based scope 2 for 2020 to 2022, from their own reports, against their official figures, and estimated that the in-house data centers of Google, Microsoft, Meta and Apple emitted about 7.62 times what their official, market-based figures said. It is a recount, not a new measurement. Uptime Institute's Jay Dietrich told the paper the location-based figure is "the right metric". In July 2024 a group of Amazon's own employees, Amazon Employees for Climate Justice, wrote that 68% of the company's certificates were unbundled, from Amazon's own disclosure to CDP for 2023, and estimated that its American data centers drew 22% renewable power from their local grids. Amazon replied that the paper "has incorrect findings and assumptions".

The companies' case, as Meta and Google make it: location-based figures charge a company for a grid it does not run, while long-term contracts pay for new plants. Meta says it has contracted more than 30 gigawatts of clean power, favoring projects on the grids where its data centers run. Critics answer that a certificate from a distant wind farm does not change what the local plants burn at night. Both sides agree on the rise: Google's location-based scope 2 nearly tripled from 2019 to 2025.

## Hourly matching, and what is not known

In 2020 Google set a goal of carbon-free energy every hour on every grid by 2030. It reports about 65% for 2025. In October 2025 the Greenhouse Gas Protocol proposed requiring hourly matching and certificates from the grid where power is used. Nearly 1,100 responses came in. Support for hourly matching was lowest among companies and industry groups; many respondents warned it could shrink voluntary buying of clean power, and others answered that only hourly, local matching makes such buying add clean power where it is used. Amazon and Meta had argued since 2022, with other companies, for counting each purchase's effect on emissions instead. In September 2026 the Protocol's board proposed two market-based methods: an hourly one, and the 2015 rules until a date not yet set. A consultation is planned for 2027 and a final standard for late 2028.

What neither method gives is the emissions a data center actually causes, which depend on which plants run more because of it, hour by hour. That is modeled, not counted. What is measured is the [carbon dioxide](kloom:e/carbon-dioxide) in the air, for the whole world at once. The next frame asks whether data centers can change when they use power.
