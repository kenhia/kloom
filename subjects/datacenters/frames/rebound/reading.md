In 1865 the economist **[William Stanley Jevons](kloom:e/william-stanley-jevons)**, then 29, published [_The Coal Question_](kloom:e/the-coal-question), a warning that Britain's growth rested on coal that would grow dearer as the seams went deeper. It was "very commonly urged," he wrote, that new and more efficient ways of using coal would meet the failing supply. In his chapter "Of the Economy of Fuel" he answered: "It is wholly a confusion of ideas to suppose that the economical use of fuel is equivalent to a diminished consumption. The very contrary is the truth." His reasoning ran through a blast furnace. If it needs less coal for each ton of iron, iron gets cheaper, more is sold, capital builds more furnaces, and "the greater number of furnaces will more than make up for the diminished consumption of each." The idea now carries his name, the **[Jevons paradox](kloom:e/jevons-paradox)**, and it is raised whenever an industry offers efficiency as the answer to its energy use, as the data center industry often does.

## What economists have found

Economists call the general effect the **[rebound effect](kloom:e/rebound-effect-conservation)**. A _direct_ rebound is more use of the thing made cheaper: a more efficient car is driven farther. An _indirect_ rebound is the money saved being spent on other things that take energy to make. Across a whole economy, prices and growth shift too. Jevons's case, where use rises by more than the efficiency saved, is called _backfire_, a rebound over 100%. The plate draws the textbook case, schematically: where demand is elastic, halving the cost of a unit more than doubles the use, and the total spent rises.

No meter can show a rebound, because no one sees the world in which the efficiency never came. Every figure below is an estimate from a model or from statistics, and the two best-known reviews read the evidence differently.

The first review holds that the backfire debate distracts from whether an efficiency policy is worth its cost. The second concludes that rebound "may erode more than half of the expected energy savings" and that most global energy scenarios leave much of it out. Neither finds that backfire is the rule, and neither rules it out.

## Computing's own record

For its supporters, computing is the strongest case for efficiency, and _The efficiency argument_ tells it: computations per kilowatt-hour doubled about every year and a half from the 1940s to about 2000, by **[Koomey's law](kloom:e/koomeys-law)**, and more slowly since. The totals have not moved one way. By Berkeley's estimates American data centers held near 60 terawatt-hours a year from 2014 to 2016, then rose to 176 in 2023. Supporters read the flat years as efficiency keeping pace with demand; critics read the rise as demand outrunning it. Whether cheaper computing created its own demand, or demand would have come anyway, the records cannot separate.

## DeepSeek, January 2025

In December 2024 the Chinese company **[DeepSeek](kloom:e/deepseek)** reported that the final training run of its V3 model took 2.788 million GPU-hours, "only $5.576M" at an assumed rental price of $2 an hour. That figure is the company's own, and its report says it leaves out "prior research and ablation experiments." On January 27, 2025, after its R1 model reached the top of app-store charts, shares of **[Nvidia](kloom:e/nvidia)**, whose chips fill AI data centers, fell 17%, a loss of close to $600 billion in market value, the largest one-day fall for an American company. Analysts at the investment bank Cantor described the fear, "great angst as to the impact for compute demand, and therefore, fears of peak spending on GPUs": if AI could be built for less, fewer chips and less power would be bought.

The other reading came from **[Satya Nadella](kloom:e/satya-nadella)**, chief executive of **[Microsoft](kloom:e/microsoft)**, late on the Sunday before, by GeekWire's account: "Jevons paradox strikes again! As AI gets more efficient and accessible, we will see its use skyrocket, turning it into a commodity we just can't get enough of." Microsoft sells computing, and the GeekWire reporter who quoted him noted that the theory "would presumably benefit Microsoft." The Cantor analysts sided with him: they called the fear of falling demand "farthest from the truth."

## What happened next

In April 2026 the **[International Energy Agency](kloom:e/international-energy-agency)** estimated that data centers' electricity worldwide grew 17% in 2025, to about 485 terawatt-hours, and that AI-focused data centers' grew 50%. Both are modeled estimates, not meter readings. The agency also wrote that energy per AI task was falling by at least a factor of ten a year, and that major model providers reported three times as many active users as a year before, the companies' own figures. The four largest cloud companies' capital spending, measured in their filings, rose from about $220 billion in 2024 to about $360 billion in 2025, by our arithmetic from the table in _Is it a bubble?_.

That is the pattern Nadella predicted: cheaper per task, more in total. It does not prove rebound, because the demand might have grown as fast without DeepSeek. Whether AI's demand keeps rising faster than its efficiency, and for how long, is unmeasured, and _Is it a bubble?_ asks whether the spending will be repaid.
