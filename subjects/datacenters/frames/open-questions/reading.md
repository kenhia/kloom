This subject set out to give each side's strongest case and the evidence, and to end each frame on what is not known. Gathered together, the unknowns have a pattern. Many of the questions that would settle the argument are open less because the answers are hard to find than because no one is required to count them. **[Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory](kloom:e/lawrence-berkeley-national-laboratory)** said so of its own national estimate in 2024: "the lack of data availability significantly limits the analysis in this report." Most of the numbers in these frames are modeled, projected or reported by the companies themselves. Few are measured by someone without a stake. That is a finding in itself, and both sides can read it their own way: as a reason to require disclosure, or as a reason not to treat the critics' worst estimates as fact.

## What depends on the future

Other questions cannot be counted yet because they have not happened. How many of the announced gigawatts will be built, and whether AI revenue grows into the buildings before the loans come due (_The gigawatt campus_, _Is it a bubble?_). Whether efficiency lowers the total or, by making computing cheaper, raises it (_Efficiency and the rebound effect_). Whether a gigawatt campus will cut its load when the grid calls on the hottest afternoon (_Data centers as flexible load_). Whether the large-load tariffs written in 2025 and 2026 keep costs off households (_Who pays for the grid_). Whether the zero-water designs, once full, consume what was projected (_Zero-water cooling_). Whether restarted and new reactors run on schedule (_Nuclear power for data centers_). And, beneath several of these, whether the buildings would have come without the tax breaks offered for them: Virginia's own auditors credited the exemption with nine-tenths of the industry's activity in 2024 and one-half in 2026 (_Data center tax breaks_).

## What the evidence does settle

Not everything is open. Some popular claims, from both sides, can be checked, and these frames checked them:

- A data center's indoor cooling loop is closed, as its defenders say, but the heat still leaves through a cooling tower that evaporates water or a dry cooler that uses more electricity, and evaporative cooling is common in humid places too (_Where a data center's water goes_).
- Nationally, data centers' on-site water is a small share of the country's use, as the industry says; at a small water system on its hottest day, a campus can be a large one, as residents say (_How much water data centers use_, _Small water systems, big campuses_).
- The widely quoted 2019 figure for the carbon of streaming video was too high by far: its authors cut their own eightfold, and the IEA's estimate is lower still (_What runs in a data center_). Loudoun County's "70% of the world's internet traffic" has no traced measurement (_Data Center Alley_).
- The industry's count of 5.5 million jobs uses an expanded definition and multipliers; the count of "blocked" projects most cited by opponents was made by a group funded by a firm that does research for AI and technology companies, and listed some projects that had only moved (_Data center jobs_, _The fight over data centers_).
- Forecasts of data center power have missed in both directions (_How much electricity data centers use_).

## Weighing the next claim

The argument will go on producing numbers. Six questions, drawn from these frames, sort most of them:

1. Is it measured, modeled, projected or claimed, and for what year?
2. Who paid for it, and who reported it?
3. Is it water withdrawn or water consumed, and on site or at the power plant?
4. Is it per task or in total, and a median or a mean?
5. Is it announced, contracted or built?
6. Is it the country, one county, or one watershed on its hottest day?

The plate draws them as a sieve. A claim that passes through all six is one both sides can argue from; one that does not is where the argument usually starts. The subject began with _The fight over data centers_, and the reader now has what each side has. The judgment is the reader's.
