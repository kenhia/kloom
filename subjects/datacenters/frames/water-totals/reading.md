No federal rule requires American data centers to report their water, so no one has counted all of it. The national figures are estimates, and the most cited is **[Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory](kloom:e/lawrence-berkeley-national-laboratory)**'s, written for the Department of Energy in 2024. It modeled every kind of data center, its cooling and its climate, and estimated that they consumed 21.2 billion liters on site in 2014 and 66 billion in 2023, 84% of it in the large cloud and colocation buildings. In gallons, 2023's figure is about 17 billion for the year, or 48 million a day, by our arithmetic. The laboratory also estimates about 800 billion liters consumed at the power plants that supplied their electricity, 4.52 liters for each kilowatt-hour, and _The water behind the electricity_ looks at that number.

## What the companies report

Four of the largest operators publish their own figures, each defined its own way. Each is the company's own measurement or estimate; **[Google](kloom:e/google)**'s 2025 figures had an outside accountant's limited review.

| --------- | ------- | ----------------------------- | ------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Google    | 2025    | 13,562 million gallons        | 10,523 million gallons    | withdrawal minus discharge, metered or estimated, site by site                       |
| Microsoft | FY 2025 | 13,266 million liters, all    | 8,170 million liters, all | whole company; data centers estimated from water-use ratios since fiscal 2024        |
| Meta      | 2025    | 1,145 million gallons         | 753 million gallons       | consumption by site for the first time; 566 million gallons of construction left out |

The plate draws Google's to scale: about three-quarters of what its data centers withdrew was consumed, by our arithmetic, and the rest was discharged. Google says its total consumption rose 34% from 2024 to 2025. **[Microsoft](kloom:e/microsoft)**'s largest site, Quincy, Washington, withdrew 1,292 million liters. **[Meta](kloom:e/meta-platforms)** puts the water used by the plants making its electricity at 26,101 million gallons, about 35 times its data centers' own, by our arithmetic. **[Amazon](kloom:e/amazon-company)** gives no sites. Each company also pays for projects meant to return water to the watersheds it draws on; Google counts its 2025 projects as replenishing 78% of its freshwater consumption, by its own method. The four totals cannot be added to the laboratory's: they are worldwide, for other years, and count different things.

## Comparisons each side uses

Comparisons decide how a number feels. Google's report says its 2025 consumption was about what it takes to irrigate 73 golf courses a year in the American Southwest, and that American data centers use less than 1% of the water Americans put on their lawns. Both are arithmetic on real figures, and both use yardsticks that make the total look small: a fleet spread over the world set beside one region's courses, at about 150 million gallons each in the 2012 survey Google cites, and a national total set beside every lawn in the country. The lawn comparison also counts only on-site water; with the laboratory's indirect estimate added, the total is about thirteen times larger, by our arithmetic.

A fairer national comparison uses the **[United States Geological Survey](kloom:e/united-states-geological-survey)**'s latest modeled water use, for 2020: public water systems consumed 4,157 million gallons a day, crop irrigation 78,400. The laboratory's 2023 on-site estimate is about 1.1% of the first and 0.06% of the second, by our arithmetic, from different years. That settles the national scale. It says nothing about one town's wells.

A figure critics often repeat comes from the **[Environmental and Energy Study Institute](kloom:e/environmental-and-energy-study-institute)**, a nonprofit: a large data center can use "up to 5 million gallons per day," like a town of 10,000 to 50,000 people. Google's largest site, in Council Bluffs, Iowa, withdrew about 4.8 million gallons a day in 2025, so the figure fits the largest sites; it is a ceiling, and the median of Google's 40 listed sites consumed about 0.4 million gallons a day, by our arithmetic. The stronger argument from critics is local: Google reports 13% of its 2025 freshwater withdrawals from sources at high risk of depletion or scarcity, Microsoft half of its withdrawals from water-stressed areas, and Meta a 24% rise in consumption in highly stressed areas while its data centers' total fell 4%, by the companies' own counts.

## The gap in the record

Company reports are voluntary. In the Uptime Institute's 2025 survey, 47% of operators said they collected water data at all. In September 2026 **[California](kloom:e/california)** enacted laws that, from 2027, require data centers to estimate their water before a business license and report what they used at each renewal. Before them, a 2026 report from the Center for Law, Energy and the Environment at Berkeley's law school found, privacy rules kept the state's water utilities from publishing any one customer's use. Even reported figures for one site can differ several times over, depending on whether they count water withdrawn or consumed, which _Withdrawal and consumption_ explains.

The totals rise while the ratios improve. Amazon's water per kilowatt-hour fell from 0.25 liters in 2021 to 0.12 in 2025, and Meta's from 0.26 to 0.14, by their own counts, but the electricity grew faster: the laboratory's 176 terawatt-hours of 2023 may become 325 to 580 by 2028. It expects the national average ratio to rise, too, as more computing moves into large cloud buildings and liquid-cooled racks. How much water data centers will use in 2028 is uncertain by a factor of about two, and a national total says little about any one watershed, which is where _Data centers in dry places_ goes next.
