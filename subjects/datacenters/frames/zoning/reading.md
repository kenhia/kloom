A **[data center](kloom:e/data-center)** needs a parcel before it needs power or water, and in the United States what may be built on a parcel is decided first by the county or city it stands in. That power is **[zoning](kloom:e/zoning)**. The Supreme Court upheld it in 1926, in **[Village of Euclid v. Ambler Realty Co.](kloom:e/village-of-euclid-v-ambler-realty-co)**, after a suburb of Cleveland divided its land into districts by use, height and lot size and a landowner who wanted the land for industry sued and lost. Most American localities still zone that way. Their codes were written for houses, shops and factories, and data centers arrived in them under older names. This frame takes its examples from **[Virginia](kloom:e/virginia)**, whose northern counties hold what the research firm Synergy counts as the world's largest market for the biggest data centers, and its guide is the state's legislative auditors, the Joint Legislative Audit and Review Commission (JLARC), whose report _Data Centers in Virginia_ (December 2024) studied how those counties handled them.

## How zoning works

A county's **comprehensive plan** is its long-term map of what it wants where. Virginia law requires one, but a plan, as JLARC puts it, does "not set any legal boundaries." The zoning ordinance does. It places every parcel in one district and says, for each, what may be built and how.

| Term                                    | What it means                                                                                                                                       |
| --------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| District                                | A zone with its own list of allowed uses: rural residential, office, light industrial. An _overlay_ district adds rules over the ones beneath it    |
| By right                                | The use is allowed outright. An owner whose site plan meets the written rules may build, and the county cannot ask for more                         |
| Special exception or special use permit | Allowed only with the elected board's approval, usually after a public hearing, and with conditions it may attach. Other states say conditional use |
| Rezoning                                | Moving a parcel into another district: a public hearing, advertised in advance. In Virginia an owner may offer conditions of its own, _proffers_    |
| Setback and height                      | The least distance between a building and its property line, and the most height it may reach                                                       |

The first two rows hold most of the argument. Under a by-right use, the auditors wrote, "a locality could not require a by-right data center to be set back farther from nearby property lines than the ordinance already dictates." Under a special permit it can ask for a wider setback or a lower building, and it can refuse.

## A use the codes did not foresee

As recently as ten years ago, JLARC wrote, data centers were "much smaller facilities that were similar in size and appearance to commercial office buildings," and codes filed them that way. Until 2021, **[Fairfax County](kloom:e/fairfax-county-virginia)** counted one as a telecommunications facility, which let it be built in residential and office districts; **[Loudoun County](kloom:e/loudoun-county-virginia)** first treated it as an office. A campus now looks, in the auditors' words, like "a new distribution center or a manufacturing facility," with a median of 35 trailer-sized generators a site, cooling plant on the roof and substations beside it. JLARC called data centers "industrial facilities that are largely incompatible with residential uses."

Its measurements give the question a size. In the eight localities it mapped, over 60% of operating data center properties were more than 500 feet from residentially zoned land, and 29% were within 200 feet, property line to property line. At least 15 sites, about 10%, had drawn noise complaints that neighbors regarded as a problem, and the readings taken there, 40 to 59 decibels, were mostly within the counties' limits of 55 or 60: the complaint was a constant "drone" or "hum," and some residents told the auditors of lost sleep and migraines. A fall in nearby home values, a common fear, was not borne out by the people JLARC asked, though that rests on interviews, not on sales data.

## What counties have done

| County         | What changed                                                                                                                                                              | When           |
| -------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------- |
| Fairfax        | Data centers made a use of their own and barred from residential districts                                                                                                | 2021           |
| Fairfax        | Buildings at least 200 feet from residential property, equipment 300; a mile from any Metro station; two sound studies; several districts from by right to special permit | September 2024 |
| Loudoun        | Two districts from by right to special permit; beside homes, a 200-foot setback and limits on generator testing                                                           | December 2023  |
| Prince William | Standards for its data center overlay district, where data centers were allowed by right                                                                                  | June 2019      |
| Prince William | The overlay cut from about 9,689 acres to about 3,641; nearly every new data center to need a special use permit; a 500-foot setback held over                            | September 2026 |

What Loudoun did next, in 2025, is the last step of the trail that follows that one county. **[Prince William County](kloom:e/prince-william-county-virginia)**'s change of September 2026 passed unanimously, with a 90-day window in which owners already in the overlay could file under the old rules. A law firm's note to its clients called the protection of such filings "a good outcome to protect existing rights"; Supervisor George Stewart, who wanted no window, warned of a "gold rush," and lost, 3–5.

## Who else decides

States can take the decision away, in either direction. **[West Virginia](kloom:e/west-virginia)**'s law of 2025 exempts a certified "high impact data center" (a critical IT load of 90 megawatts or more) from county and municipal "zoning, horticultural, noise, viewshed, lighting, development, or land use ordinances." Its legislature gave the purpose as "uniform and consistent application" and new jobs and revenue: the case for predictable rules, as a state that wants the buildings makes it. Virginia's auditors made the case for local say: land-use decisions are "very site specific, and local governments are better positioned than the state to evaluate what is appropriate for a given site." They found no reason for the state to step in, whether by preemption or by a statewide ban on data centers near historic sites, proposed in 2024, which they called possibly "broader than needed." Residents and their groups, they noted, now speak against proposals at hearings far more often than they once did, and press for tighter limits on where data centers may go.

Zoning also stops at the fence. Virginia's State Corporation Commission, not a county, approves new power plants and transmission lines and sets **[Dominion Energy](kloom:e/dominion-energy)**'s rates, and the Department of Environmental Quality permits the water withdrawals that local utilities make. JLARC found state law unclear on whether a county may even require a water-use estimate or a sound study, and recommended that the General Assembly say so; its 2025 list of recommendations awaiting action still held both.

What no one has measured is what the new rules do: whether a special permit yields better sites or only slower ones, and how much building simply moves to counties with looser codes. One county's story is told year by year in the trail from here, and the next frame is a rezoning that went to court: the Digital Gateway.
