When Heatmap Pro asked registered voters in October 2026 what might make them support a [data center](kloom:e/data-center), the reason most chosen was the tax revenue it brings, as _The fight over data centers_ tells. The same poll found that one of the reasons for opposing a project was the fear that residents' own tax bills could rise to pay for it. Both rest on how a county taxes the buildings, and on what it does with the money.

## Why the bill is large

A county taxes a data center twice. It taxes the land and the building as real estate, like a warehouse. Where the state allows, as **[Virginia](kloom:e/virginia)** and **[Georgia](kloom:e/georgia-u-s-state)** do, it also taxes the equipment inside as business personal property, and the equipment is worth more than the building. In Virginia, by the figures of the state's legislative auditor (JLARC), IT and mechanical equipment made up 68% of data centers' capital investment in fiscal 2021 to 2023, and construction about 20%. Equipment loses taxable value each year on a schedule, but servers are usually replaced about every five years, and each replacement starts the schedule again. So the bill renews.

Virginia lets each locality set a lower rate for computer equipment, and the rates differ widely. JLARC worked out what the same $150 million of equipment would pay in five years:

| Locality, 2024 | Rate per $100 | Depreciation | Five-year tax |
| -------------- | ------------: | ------------ | ------------: |
| Loudoun        |         $4.15 | Moderate     |  $9.3 million |
| Fairfax        |         $4.57 | Fast         |  $8.0 million |
| Prince William |         $3.70 | Fast         |  $6.7 million |
| Mecklenburg    |         $0.66 | Moderate     |  $2.0 million |
| Henrico        |         $0.40 | Moderate     |  $1.3 million |
| Wise           |         $0.24 | Fast         |  $0.4 million |

Those are modeled sums, for one example. In Georgia, researchers at the University of Georgia's **[Carl Vinson Institute of Government](kloom:e/carl-vinson-institute-of-government)**, evaluating the state's data center tax break for its auditors in 2025, estimated the bill on a "representative" metro Atlanta complex of three buildings, worth over $2 billion with its land and equipment, at $33.6 million: $27.8 million collected and $5.9 million abated.

## What it pays for

**[Prince William County](kloom:e/prince-william-county-virginia)**, second in Virginia by data center floor space in 2022, shows the curve. Its tax on data centers' business property rose from $2.9 million in fiscal 2013 to $54.4 million in 2022, as collected and counted by the county's finance department (2022's figure preliminary); the plate draws it. The county had cut its rate on computer equipment to $1.25 in 2001 to attract the industry; it has raised it since, and in April 2026 set it at $4.50, from $4.15, for a projected $22 million more. In fiscal 2023, JLARC counted $110 million from data centers, 7% of the county's local revenue; in neighboring **[Loudoun County](kloom:e/loudoun-county-virginia)** it was 31%, which _What data centers pay Loudoun_ follows.

County staff told JLARC that the money let Loudoun and Prince William lower real estate rates, Henrico start an affordable housing trust fund and Mecklenburg build schools. Prince William gives its schools 57.23% of general revenue by agreement, and the budget that raised the equipment rate cut the real estate rate from 90.6 to 86.5 cents per $100, which it projected would lower the average residential bill by $56. A study by the consultants PFM, hired by the county in 2022, found that data centers asked little of county services: few employees, and their own security and fire suppression. By its model, the county's net benefit was $13.41 for each dollar of county cost in 2022, while saying that noise, views and emissions were left out because they could not be costed.

## The risks

Auditors and critics name four. The first is dependence on one industry: PFM warned that "hot markets can cool rapidly," and Loudoun and Prince William, their staff told JLARC, have set up revenue stabilization or reserve funds. The second is depreciation: PFM found companies equipping buildings more slowly and keeping equipment longer, which lowers what they pay, though denser equipment may partly offset it. The third is abatement. A county can cut a project's property tax, sometimes by agreeing a **[payment in lieu of taxes](kloom:e/payment-in-lieu-of-taxes)** smaller than the tax, and Good Jobs First, a nonprofit that tracks subsidies and argues for disclosing them, reported in April 2026 that local governments seldom publish what they lose to data center deals. The fourth is above the county: most states exempt data centers' equipment from sales tax, local shares included, which _Data center tax breaks_ follows.

## What is not known

None of these sources measures what a county's budget does if new building slows while the equipment it taxes keeps depreciating; PFM called the durability of any one market uncertain. Abatements are often unpublished, so many counties' net receipts cannot be checked. And the costs that fall outside a budget, from power lines to noise, are other frames' to count. Why the companies are building so fast is _The race for compute_.
