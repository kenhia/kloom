A **[community benefits agreement](kloom:e/community-benefits-agreement)** is, in its original sense, a contract between a developer and community groups: the developer promises specific benefits to the neighborhood, such as local hiring, a fund or a park, and the groups agree to support the project, or at least not oppose it. The form began in Los Angeles in the late 1990s, and its best-known example was signed for the Staples Center district. Around data centers the phrase is used more loosely, for three kinds of promise that differ in the one thing that matters most: who can make the company keep it.

## Three kinds of promise

| Kind                         | Who signs                          | Who can enforce it                   | An example                                |
| ---------------------------- | ---------------------------------- | ------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------- |
| Community benefits agreement | the developer and community groups | the groups who signed, as a contract | the Staples Center agreement, Los Angeles |
| Incentive or host agreement  | the developer and a government     | that government                      | Meta and Louisiana, 2024                  |
| Pledge or community fund     | the company alone                  | no one, as a contract                | Microsoft's pledges of January 2026       |

Most data center deals are of the second and third kinds. Even the first has a known weakness: nothing ensures that the groups who sign speak for the neighborhood. New York's Atlantic Yards agreement was criticized because the few groups that negotiated it were funded by the developer.

## One deal in Louisiana

**[Meta](kloom:e/meta-platforms)** announced its largest data center, **[Hyperion](kloom:e/hyperion-data-center)**, in **[Richland Parish](kloom:e/richland-parish-louisiana)**, **[Louisiana](kloom:e/louisiana)**, in December 2024. Its terms are spread across several documents. A tax incentive agreement and a ground lease, signed by Meta's subsidiary Laidley LLC and the state and read by the magazine _Wired_ through public-records requests in 2025, give Laidley the state's sales tax exemption on equipment and a **[payment in lieu of taxes](kloom:e/payment-in-lieu-of-taxes)** on the land and buildings. If it invests $10 billion by December 2032 and reaches the equivalent of 500 full-time jobs by the end of 2034, averaging $82,000 a year with health insurance, it pays 20% of the assessed property tax; at the lowest tier, 40%. The state leased it about 1,400 acres, with $732,000 a year in rent credited toward a $12 million price. Separately, **[Entergy](kloom:e/entergy)** Louisiana's contract to power the site runs 15 years. The Public Service Commission's consultant said Meta covers the full cost of the three gas plants built for it; _Wired_ reported that Meta finances the first 15 years of their 30-year loan, and critics asked who pays after that.

Meta's own fact sheet adds pledges outside those agreements: more than $200 million for roads and water, $1 million a year to Entergy's bill assistance program for low-income seniors and customers with disabilities, and community grants once the center runs. The plate sets the two sides out as a ledger.

What the property tax concession is worth in dollars has not been published in any source this reading found. Critics found other gaps. _Wired_ reported that the incentive agreement does not require local hiring and counts 40 hours of part-time work as a "full-time job." The Alliance for Affordable Energy, a ratepayer advocate, questioned whether the commission could enforce Meta's payments for the plants at all. Supporters answered with the need. "There's nothing that is bulletproof," said Commissioner Foster Campbell, who voted yes: "I think it will pay dividends." The state's economic development agency said it still expects 500 permanent, full-time jobs.

## Pledges without a counterparty

On January 13, 2026, [Microsoft](kloom:e/microsoft)'s president, **[Brad Smith](kloom:e/brad-smith-american-lawyer)**, announced five "Community-First" commitments for its American data centers: to "pay our way" so they do not raise electricity prices, to replenish more water than they use, to hire locally, to invest in AI training, and to add to the local tax base. "We won't ask local municipalities to reduce their local property tax rates," it says, though the pledge does not mention state sales tax exemptions, and GeekWire noted that Microsoft had accepted abatements in Ohio and Iowa before. Microsoft cites its own record in Quincy, Washington, where county property tax revenue rose from about $60 million to more than $180 million over two decades, by its figures, and more than $25 million it is spending on water and sewer work near Leesburg, Virginia. **[Amazon Web Services](kloom:e/amazon-web-services)** reports opening 26 community funds worldwide in 2025 and allocating more than $21.1 million through them. Each of these figures is the company's own.

The case for such pledges is Microsoft's own: infrastructure "will only progress when communities feel that the gains outweigh the costs," and a pledge names the gains in a place's own terms, a water main or a training program. The case against is that a pledge binds no one and a regulator's order does. In Louisiana the Union of Concerned Scientists had asked the commission for "reasonable safeguards, such as a cost cap," and speakers at the hearing asked for assurances that the jobs would go to people nearby. Neither became a binding term: Meta's promise of "a concerted effort" to hire locally is in a letter, not the contract. Building trades often seek a different contract, a **[project labor agreement](kloom:e/project-labor-agreement)**, which sets union wages for a project's construction; contractors' groups argue such agreements raise costs, and studies disagree.

## What no one knows yet

There is little case law on whether a community benefits agreement can be enforced at all, and the companies' pledges have no outside auditor. Whether Richland Parish sees its 500 jobs will be known in 2034. The other answer communities have reached for is a pause, in _Moratoriums and local votes_.
