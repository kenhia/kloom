In June 2024 David Cahn, a partner at the venture capital firm **[Sequoia Capital](kloom:e/sequoia-capital)**, which invests in AI companies, did a piece of arithmetic. Take what **[Nvidia](kloom:e/nvidia)** expected to sell in AI chips, double it for the rest of a data center's cost, and double it again for the margin its buyers would need: that is the revenue AI must one day earn to pay for what was being built. "AI's $200B question is now AI's $600B question," he wrote. The four largest spenders' capital budgets have since more than tripled, from about $220 billion in 2024 to about $700 billion planned for 2026, by our arithmetic from the table below. Whether that makes an **[AI bubble](kloom:e/ai-bubble)**, spending that will not be repaid, or the early years of an investment that will, is argued by people with money on both answers. The figures are the latest, as of November 2026.

## The case that it is a bubble

Skeptics point first to circular deals. In September 2025 Nvidia announced it intended to invest up to $100 billion in **[OpenAI](kloom:e/openai)**, "progressively as each gigawatt is deployed" of Nvidia systems that OpenAI would buy. OpenAI in turn signed a contract reported at $300 billion with **[Oracle](kloom:e/oracle-corporation)** for computing, and Microsoft, Amazon and Google have invested in the AI laboratories that buy their computing. Critics compare it to the equipment makers that lent to their own customers during the **[dot-com bubble](kloom:e/dot-com-bubble)**, when fiber was laid far ahead of demand; _Carrier hotels and colocation_ tells that history.

They point second to accounting. Chips are written off over their useful life, and in January 2025 Meta lengthened that life for certain servers to 5.5 years, which it said would cut its 2025 depreciation by about $2.9 billion. Skeptics such as the venture capitalist Paul Kedrosky warn that buildings fitted with today's chips could be obsolete before they earn back their cost. Nvidia's chief executive, Jensen Huang, replies that customers keep using older chips long after release.

They point third to how it is paid for. In the third quarter of 2026 Alphabet's free cash flow was negative $9.1 billion and Meta's negative $2.1 billion, and Meta's long-term debt rose from $58.7 billion to $80.9 billion in nine months. Over the year to September, Amazon's free cash flow was negative $42.4 billion, while Alphabet's was still positive, at $19.7 billion. All of these are measured, from the companies' releases.

## The case that it is not

Supporters answer that demand is measured too. Microsoft reported $677 billion in contracted future revenue from its commercial customers in October 2026, up 73% in a year. Alphabet's chief executive, Sundar Pichai, said its cloud growth was "powered by huge demand for our AI infrastructure." Morgan Stanley's analysts and the chair of the Federal Reserve, Jerome Powell, have each said that, unlike the dot-com companies, today's leaders earn real revenue. In July 2026 Microsoft's chief executive said it had added 31 data centers in one quarter "in response to accelerating demand." Skeptics reply that part of Microsoft's backlog is OpenAI's own commitment, reported at $250 billion, to buy its cloud services. The backlog is a contract; whether every customer pays is the forecast.

## What a bust would leave behind

On the ground, a bust would leave campuses half built, and power lines and plants built for load that never came. [Ohio](kloom:e/ohio) shows both sides of the response. In July 2025 its utility commission approved a tariff for **[AEP Ohio](kloom:e/american-electric-power)** under which a large data center pays each month for at least 85% of the capacity it contracts, whether it uses it or not, and posts collateral, which AEP says protects other customers "from being saddled with infrastructure costs in the event data centers do not operate." The commission chose that settlement, reached by AEP, its own staff and the consumers' counsel, over a rival one filed by Microsoft, Amazon, Google and the Data Center Coalition. The Ohio Manufacturers' Association, a trade group whose members also pay for the grid, said in February 2026 that the forecasts were still too high: "Customers are being asked to pay for a future that may never arrive." AEP's own forecast of large new loads fell from 30 gigawatts to 13 once the tariff asked for commitments. _Who pays for the grid_ follows that question.

## What is not known

No one knows how much of the spending is for AI rather than ordinary cloud services, how long the chips will earn money, how much of the debt sits outside the companies' balance sheets, or whether AI revenue will grow into the buildings before the loans come due. The subject's open questions are gathered in _What no one knows yet_.
