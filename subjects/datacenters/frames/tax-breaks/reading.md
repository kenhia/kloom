A **[sales tax](kloom:e/sales-tax)** is paid by whoever buys a thing to use it, and a data center buys a great deal: servers, switches, storage, chillers and generators, much of it replaced every few years. Since the late 2000s most states have agreed to waive that tax for data centers that meet a threshold of investment and jobs. **[Virginia](kloom:e/virginia)**'s legislative auditors counted 34 states with such an exemption in 2024, and four more with no sales tax at all; every state bordering Virginia has one. Some states also cut property tax, and counties negotiate abatements of their own, which _Data centers and the local tax base_ follows. The state sales tax exemption is the largest of these breaks, and the one this frame is about.

## What Virginia gives up, and gets back

Virginia's exemption dates from 2010. A data center qualifies by investing $150 million and creating 50 jobs paying at least one and a half times the local average wage, less in distressed areas, and the exemption runs to 2035, or to 2040 or 2050 for projects of $35 billion or $100 billion. Since 2021 operators must report the tax they did not pay. Those reports are the measurement, and the state does not check them.

## The case against

Good Jobs First, a research group that campaigns against corporate subsidies, published _Cloudy with a Loss of Spending Control_ in April 2025. Of 32 states with data center incentives, it found at least ten losing more than $100 million a year and twelve disclosing no cost at all, and [Texas](kloom:e/texas) revising its projected cost for fiscal 2025 from $130 million to $1 billion within 23 months. Because eligibility rules written for smaller buildings are now easy to meet, it argues, the exemptions are "virtually automatic" and uncapped, for "an extremely profitable industry." It recommends that states cancel them.

**[Georgia](kloom:e/georgia-u-s-state)**'s legislature passed House Bill 1192 in 2024, pausing new exemption certificates for two years. A 2022 University of Georgia review had projected that the state would forgo $307 million more than it collected from 2024 to 2030, as the Associated Press reported it. Governor **[Brian Kemp](kloom:e/brian-kemp)** vetoed the bill on May 7, 2024.

## The case for

Kemp's veto message states the defenders' case in brief: the legislature had extended the exemption through 2031 two years before, and an "abrupt" cutoff would undermine investments made in reliance on it. JLARC's 2024 report found that about 90% of Virginia's industry uses the exemption, that companies call it a key factor in where they build, and that it saves a typical $250 million to $325 million building $9 million to $15.5 million at the start, and more at each refresh of its equipment. Without it, the argument runs, the buildings go next door, and with them the construction work and the county property tax on everything inside. As Virginia's 2026 budget was settled, Josh Levi of the Data Center Coalition, the industry's trade group, warned that jobs were at risk as companies invest in steadier states: "The message to businesses in all industries is clear—Virginia is no longer a reliable partner."

## Where it stands

As of November 2026, Virginia's exemption stands. The Senate had proposed ending it at the end of 2026; the House and Governor Abigail Spanberger kept it, and the budget passed on June 29 left it unchanged and added a tax of 1.1 cents a kilowatt-hour on data centers' electricity for two years from July 1, capped at $600 million a year, and a legislative subcommittee is to report on the exemption by December 15. Arizona stopped accepting new applications from July 2026 to June 2029. **[Ohio](kloom:e/ohio)**'s and Illinois's governors paused new approvals in 2026, and Washington and Maine narrowed theirs, while Kentucky widened its own in 2025. Most of these are pauses and trims, not repeals.

What no one has measured is the question every estimate turns on: how many of these buildings would have come anyway. Virginia's auditors changed their answer from nine-tenths to one-half in two years. What communities ask for in return is _Community benefit agreements_.
