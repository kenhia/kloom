A data center often arrives under another name. The project has a code name; the land is bought by a company formed for the purpose; and the local officials who negotiate the deal sign a **[nondisclosure agreement](kloom:e/non-disclosure-agreement)** (NDA) promising not to say who the buyer is, or what it will need, until it is announced. **[Google](kloom:e/google)** did all three at The Dalles in 2005, a story _Google at The Dalles_ tells. Twenty years on the practice is still common, and it has become one of the arguments against the buildings themselves.

How common is hard to count, because the agreements are themselves confidential. One state's records give a measure. In July 2026 _The Times-Picayune | The Advocate_ reported that **[Louisiana](kloom:e/louisiana)**'s economic development agency had signed nearly 300 NDAs since January 2024, about one every three days, for deals of every kind, with companies including **[Meta](kloom:e/meta-platforms)**, Google and **[Amazon](kloom:e/amazon-company)** and under code names like "Project Steel" and "Project Tiger"; that 77% of state senators had signed one with the agency; and that a few were now asking to be released. Heatmap Pro's poll of October 2026 found little transparency in a deal among the arguments against a data center that registered voters found most convincing.

## Project Blue

In June 2025 the supervisors of **[Pima County](kloom:e/pima-county-arizona)**, Arizona, voted 3 to 2 to sell 290 acres near the county fairgrounds, outside **[Tucson](kloom:e/tucson-arizona)**, for a data center campus known as Project Blue. The developer was Beale Infrastructure; the company that would use it was not named. The county had been under an NDA since at least June 2024.

| ----------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 2023              | A county "project profile" names the end user                                                               |
| By June 2024      | The county is under a nondisclosure agreement                                                               |
| June 17, 2025     | Supervisors vote 3–2 to sell the land, contingent on annexation by Tucson                                   |

The city's own information page said that its NDA had "constrained fact-sharing," leaving a "communication void" filled by "misinformation and speculation." Tucson's mayor, Regina Romero, said that she did not know the company either. When the council ended talks, Beale called it "a missed opportunity for the City." **[Amazon Web Services](kloom:e/amazon-web-services)** said it had no commitments in place to develop Project Blue and had done only "standard due diligence" in Arizona. The county went ahead with the sale in December 2025, to a developer that had agreed to cool the buildings with air in a closed loop and to give $15 million to local causes.

## Two accounts

**The case for confidentiality**, as companies and development officials make it: a company weighing several sites does not want its rivals, or the other places bidding for it, to know its plans, and a known buyer pays more for land. "Why would you telegraph to your competitors … Or why would we telegraph to other states what we're offering?" asked Louisiana's economic development secretary, Susan Bourgeois. NDAs "are a standard practice for economic development projects," Tucson's city staff wrote.

**The case against**, as residents, critics and some officials make it: the people who must live with a project, and even the officials who vote on it, decide without knowing who it is for or how much power and water it will use. "I don't accept that an elected official can be bound by an NDA I never signed and didn't know about," said Pima County supervisor Matt Heinz, who voted for the sale. The board's chair, Rex Scott, had "absolutely no second thoughts" about his own vote for it, but said that "all five of us wish we had more information." Sometimes the secrecy outlasts the deal. The Dalles called Google's water use a trade secret and fought for 13 months, with Google paying its legal costs, to keep it from _The Oregonian_, before settling in 2022; _Small water systems, big campuses_ follows that fight.

Some on the companies' side have moved toward the critics. In January 2026 **[Microsoft](kloom:e/microsoft)**'s president, **[Brad Smith](kloom:e/brad-smith-american-lawyer)**, acknowledged to GeekWire that the industry had bought land under NDAs to keep prices down, leaving communities in the dark. "That is clearly not the path that's going to take us forward," he said.

## The rules since

Pima County's rules of September 2025 limit an NDA to 180 days, with extensions, and require a project's details to be made public at least 90 days before any county vote. In September 2026 the board extended them, 4 to 1, beyond economic development to other land projects, and widened its review of any project that would use large amounts of water or power. Steve Christy, the vote against, had warned in 2025 that the rules would say "Pima County is not open for business." In Congress, a bill introduced in July 2026 to make large AI data centers disclose their energy and water use had not come out of committee by November. Whether disclosure rules drive projects away, as their critics fear, has not been measured, and no one keeps a national count of the agreements.
