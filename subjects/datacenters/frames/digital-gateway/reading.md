The **Prince William Digital Gateway** was a plan for as many as 37 data centers on farms and woods along Pageland Lane, in the west of **[Prince William County](kloom:e/prince-william-county-virginia)**, **[Virginia](kloom:e/virginia)**, beside **[Manassas National Battlefield Park](kloom:e/manassas-national-battlefield-park)**, where the two Battles of Bull Run were fought in 1861 and 1862. Two companies were to build it: **[QTS](kloom:e/quality-technology-services)**, owned since 2021 by the investment firm Blackstone, and **[Compass Datacenters](kloom:e/compass-datacenters)**. It went through every step that the frame on zoning describes, a change to the comprehensive plan and then three rezonings, and it ended in the courts, over how the county had advertised the hearing that approved it.

## The plan, and the case for it

The three rezonings, as the **[Court of Appeals of Virginia](kloom:e/court-of-appeals-of-virginia)** summed them up:

| Rezoning | Applicant                          | Acres | Floor area, square feet | Height cap         |
| -------- | ---------------------------------- | ----: | ----------------------: | ------------------ |
| Compass  | H&H Capital Acquisitions (Compass) |   884 |              11,500,000 | 85 feet, most bays |
| DG North | GW Acquisition Co. I (QTS)         |   534 |               7,000,000 | 83 feet            |
| DG South | GW Acquisition Co. (QTS)           |   342 |               3,700,000 | 60 feet            |

That is 1,760 acres and 22.2 million square feet by our arithmetic. The developers' proffers capped noise at 60 decibels by day and 55 by night at the edge of any land zoned residential, though not for construction or for generators in an outage. QTS's petition to the Supreme Court claimed the project would bring "$40 to 50 billion in private investment," millions a year in taxes and "thousands of high-paying, permanent technology jobs." Wheeler, who led the board that approved it, has described it as a way to move a county that leaned on homeowners' property taxes toward a larger commercial base. By her account the county's budgets projected about $65 million from data centers in fiscal 2020 and about $550 million for fiscal 2027.

Many of those selling wanted to. The idea came, late in 2020, from landowners along Pageland Lane. Their website said "PAGELAND IS NO LONGER RURAL," pointing to transmission lines, industrial building nearby and the "Pageland Autobahn" of through traffic. Land sold for data centers in the county had risen, by one trade publication's tally, from about $270,000 an acre in 2018 to about $1 million in early 2021. More than 100 landowners signed contracts in 2022.

## The case against it

The opponents were neighbors, led by residents of the Oak Valley subdivision, and a coalition of preservation and environmental groups: the **[American Battlefield Trust](kloom:e/american-battlefield-trust)**, the **[National Parks Conservation Association](kloom:e/national-parks-conservation-association)**, the **[Piedmont Environmental Council](kloom:e/piedmont-environmental-council)** and the National Trust for Historic Preservation. The battlefield trust's president, David Duncan, said it does not oppose data centers in general, but that some of the land was what the National Park Service counts as core battlefield, and "you could move a data center" a short way and spare it. In 2022 Fairfax Water, the neighboring county's utility, asked for a study of the effect on the **[Occoquan Reservoir](kloom:e/occoquan-reservoir)**, which it said supplies 800,000 people. The Piedmont council estimated the campus would need 3 to 6 gigawatts and new 500-kilovolt lines, a claim from one side. Wheeler said the buildings would have been air-cooled and used little water. The National Trust pointed out that the site lay outside the overlay district where the county had planned data centers to go.

## A notice, not a verdict

Virginia law required the county to advertise the hearing in a newspaper twice, at least six days apart, the first no more than 14 days before it. The first advertisement never ran. The county blamed the newspaper, _The Washington Post_; the trial judge, Kimberly A. Irving, found that the county's clerk had not confirmed the order. Three others ran, on December 2, 5 and 9, but the ordinances they referred readers to were not available until December 7. The Court of Appeals held that the rezonings were void from the start. A circuit judge in a parallel case had ruled for the county, and the real estate and home builders' associations that joined its side argued that it would be "absurd" to punish a county for giving more notice than required. The court granted that argument "logical force" and ruled on the other defects instead. Mac Haddow, president of the Oak Valley association, answered that the General Assembly had made public notice on such projects "a fundamental requirement." The plan amendment, which residents had challenged separately, still stands.

Whether the project would have survived without the notice is a matter of opinion. Betsy Merritt of the National Trust holds that public opposition, through the supervisors elected after the vote, "was ultimately what killed the project"; that cannot be tested. The board under Jefferson withdrew from the appeal in April 2026, having spent $1.72 million on it. Some landowners, among them Mary Ann Ghadban, who led the assembling of the land, sued QTS to get out of their contracts; others were glad it was over. The parcels returned to their old zoning, though the plan still marks them for technology use, and any new rezoning starts again. No one can now measure the taxes it would have paid or the effects its neighbors feared. The next frame turns from land to the state's tax code: data center tax breaks.
