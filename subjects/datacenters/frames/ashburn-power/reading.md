In July 2022 **[Dominion Energy](kloom:e/dominion-energy)**, the utility that serves eastern **[Loudoun County](kloom:e/loudoun-county-virginia)**, began telling **[data center](kloom:e/data-center)** companies that some of their new buildings in **[Ashburn](kloom:e/ashburn-virginia)** and the rest of the county's east would not get power on the schedule they had planned, and that the delays could last until 2026. The shortage was not of power plants. It was of **[transmission](kloom:e/electric-power-transmission)**: the high-voltage lines and **[substations](kloom:e/substation)** that carry electricity into the county could not take more load without breaking the grid's reliability standards. By the account of the legislature's auditors in **[Virginia](kloom:e/virginia)**, the Joint Legislative Audit and Review Commission (JLARC), Dominion paused new data center connections for about three months. That September it resumed them "on an incremental basis," a spokesman said, and county staff reported that most planned projects would get "a significantly reduced allocation" of the power they expected through January 2026.

## The lines

Dominion's answer was a ring of new 500 kV and 230 kV lines around Ashburn that it calls the Loudoun Reliability Loop. Each needs a certificate from the **[State Corporation Commission](kloom:e/state-corporation-commission-virginia)** (SCC), Virginia's utility regulator, which decides whether a line is needed and where it runs. The county has no formal role in choosing a route; it can only take part in the case.

| Project           | Filed          | The SCC's decision                                                                      | What it adds                                                                                                   |
| ----------------- | -------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Mars–Wishing Star | October 2022   | approved April 2023                                                                     | a 500/230 kV line and two substations near Brambleton; Dominion expected it in service by summer 2026          |
| Aspen–Golden      | March 7, 2024  | approved February 5, 2025; affirmed by the Supreme Court of Virginia, February 19, 2026 | 9.4 miles of 500 kV and 230 kV line and two substations; $688.6 million (2023 dollars), the company's estimate |
| Golden–Mars       | March 28, 2025 | Route 3A ordered June 29, 2026                                                          | about nine miles of 500 kV and 230 kV line                                                                     |

The Aspen–Golden filing gives the scale. Its 500 kV circuit can carry 4,357 megavolt-amperes in summer and its 230 kV circuit 1,573, hung together on steel structures averaging about 170 feet tall, as the plate draws them. At each end, **[transformers](kloom:e/transformer)** in the new substations step the voltage down.

Golden–Mars was the line most fought over. Residents of Loudoun Valley Estates in Ashburn, the county and **[Loudoun County Public Schools](kloom:e/loudoun-county-public-schools)** asked for it to be buried. The commission found an underground route "not feasible." In April 2026 it chose Route 4, across the shared campus of a high school and an elementary school, which needed the school board's consent; the board did not give it. On June 29 the commission ordered Route 3A, which it had called inferior "by almost every objective measure," finding that further delay "unreasonably jeopardizes the provision of reliable electric service." "Nobody deserves a 185-foot transmission line in their backyard," the resident Vicky Hu told WJLA. Dominion called it "an urgent reliability project." JLARC had noted the other side of burying: an underground line costs substantially more, and that cost is spread across all of a utility's ratepayers.

The lines reach past the county, too. A 765 kV line proposed by Valley Link, a venture of Dominion and two other transmission companies, would run from West Virginia to Maryland across Loudoun's northwest; the county's Board of Supervisors voted in September 2026 to oppose it as proposed, before any application had been filed.

These are measured, by Dominion's own meters across its whole territory, and reported by the company to **[PJM Interconnection](kloom:e/pjm-interconnection)**, the grid operator, in September 2026. What lies ahead is projected. Dominion said it then held contracts of some kind for about 54 gigawatts of data center capacity; it left 19 gigawatts of the earliest-stage agreements out of its forecast and assumed, from its own history, that contracted capacity ramps up over five years to 63% use. Its forecast for 2040 was 16,460 megawatts, against about 12,650 in its forecast of a year before, by our reading of the company's chart.

JLARC's own forecast of December 2024, made for it by the University of Virginia's Weldon Cooper Center, found that unconstrained demand for power in Virginia would double within ten years, driven mainly by data centers, and that it largely matched PJM's forecast.

## Who pays, and each side's account

JLARC's consultants found that Virginia's rates in 2024 charged data centers their full cost of service. The Data Center Coalition, the industry's trade group, points to that finding. The same report projected that a typical Dominion household's generation and transmission charges could rise by $14 to $37 a month by 2040, in 2024 dollars, from the growth in data center demand, depending on how much of it is met. How the cost of regional lines is shared among PJM's states is the subject of _Who pays for the grid_.

For the supervisor Laura TeKrony, Loudoun's "electric grid and community are at a breaking point," with "unsightly power lines and substations" going up near homes and schools. To Amy Weinhouse of the **[Piedmont Environmental Council](kloom:e/piedmont-environmental-council)**, each case judges one line, with "no analysis of the cumulative impacts of all the lines." The industry's account stresses partnership: "Access to reliable energy has been a critical factor in the data center industry's growth in Northern Virginia," the Coalition's president, Josh Levi, told _Loudoun Now_ in 2022. Dominion says the new lines serve "homes, businesses, schools, hospitals" as well as data centers, and the commission found the need for Golden–Mars urgent.

What is not known is how much of the contracted load will appear, whether lines built for it will carry it for the decades their costs are spread over, and how many more will be needed. Meanwhile the county changed how it approves the buildings themselves: _Loudoun ends by-right data centers_.
