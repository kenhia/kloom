In March 2026 the polling firm **[Gallup](kloom:e/gallup-inc)** asked Americans, for the first time, whether they favored or opposed building a **[data center](kloom:e/data-center)** for artificial intelligence in their local area. Seventy-one percent opposed it, 48% strongly. In the same survey 53% opposed a nuclear power plant nearby. That spring, by an April tally, **[Microsoft](kloom:e/microsoft)**, **[Alphabet](kloom:e/alphabet-inc)**, **[Amazon](kloom:e/amazon-company)** and **[Meta](kloom:e/meta-platforms)** planned to spend about $725 billion on capital investment in 2026, mostly on data centers and the chips inside them, up 77% from the year before. The buildings were going up faster than ever, in places where most people said they did not want one. That gap is the fight this subject follows. The subject does not settle it.

## What each side says

Gallup asked the people who answered an open question in April: why? It sorted their answers into themes, and a person could give more than one.

| Opponents' reasons           | Share of opponents | Supporters' reasons       | Share of supporters |
| ---------------------------- | -----------------: | ------------------------- | ------------------: |
| Use of resources (any)       |                50% | Local economic benefits   |                 66% |
| — water                      |                18% | — jobs                    |                 55% |
| — energy, power, the grid    |                18% | — tax revenue             |                 13% |
| Quality of life              |                22% | Technology benefits       |                 17% |
| Costs (utility bills: 15%)   |                20% | Personal benefits         |                 10% |
| Pollution (noise: 9%)        |                16% | Help the US lead, not lag |                  3% |
| Jobs lost to AI, the economy |                14% |                           |                     |
| Dislike or distrust of AI    |                14% |                           |                     |

In October 2026, a Heatmap Pro poll of 2,537 registered voters, run by Embold Research, let people choose up to three reasons. The reason most chosen for supporting a project was the tax revenue it brings. The one most chosen for opposing it was that it could raise power or water bills. That a data center "enables the building of AI" persuaded the fewest people either way. More than 70% still opposed a hypothetical one in their area, though support rose for the first time in over a year.

## The strongest case on each side

**The case for**, as the industry and its supporters make it: data centers are the physical base of nearly every digital service, from email to hospital records to AI. A large campus pays property tax on very costly equipment and asks little of a county in return, sending no children to its schools. And the work has to happen somewhere. The Data Center Coalition, the industry's trade group, paid **[PwC](kloom:e/pwc)** to estimate the industry's reach: about 5.5 million American jobs in 2024, counting construction, suppliers and the spending of wages, about 1 million of them in the industry itself. That is an estimate, commissioned by the industry, and _Data center jobs_ looks at how it was made.

**The case against**, as residents and critics make it: a large campus draws as much power as a city, and the new lines and power plants may be paid for by every ratepayer. Some campuses cool with water in places short of it. Neighbors hear the fans and the backup generators. The permanent jobs are few for the land and the tax breaks involved. And many deals are negotiated under nondisclosure agreements and code names, so residents learn late. Each of these is a claim too, and each has its own frame here.

## A fight across parties

Gallup found majorities of Democrats, independents and Republicans opposed. Democrats were more likely to be strongly opposed (56%) than Republicans (39%). The opposition's best-known tally comes from Data Center Watch, which counts projects with public values of at least $156 billion blocked or delayed in 2025, and local opposition groups in 42 states. It says data centers became an issue in the 2025 elections in **[Virginia](kloom:e/virginia)**, New Jersey and **[Georgia](kloom:e/georgia-u-s-state)**. Who does the counting matters. Data Center Watch is funded by 10a Labs, which does research for AI and technology companies. Engineers at the consulting firm Exponent noted that its first report listed as "blocked" some projects that no local group had opposed, which the company had moved to a site nearby.

Opponents are often called **[NIMBY](kloom:e/nimby)**s: not in my back yard. The word is itself an argument: it says the objection is to where a thing goes, not to the thing. Gallup's open answers hold both kinds: some opponents named the land and "don't want it in this area," and others named AI itself, or the power and water the buildings use wherever they stand.

## How to read this subject

Every number in this subject is one of three kinds, and the reading says which:

- **Measured**: counted after the fact, by a meter, a survey or a filing, with its method published.
- **Projected**: a forecast, given with its range and what it assumes.
- **Claimed**: stated by someone with a stake in the answer and not checked independently. It may still be true.

Each reading also says who paid for a study, gives both sides' readings of the same facts where they differ, and ends on what is not yet known. Next, the building itself.
