A **[moratorium](kloom:e/moratorium-law)** is a pause: a government stops approving something for a set time, usually while it writes the rules for it. From 2025, counties and towns across the United States paused data centers this way, and in 2026 two states did. Voters recalled officials, replaced a town council and, in 2026, decided dozens of local ballot measures. This frame follows them in the United States.

## Pauses, place by place

Most local pauses are short and say what they are for: time to study the effects and write zoning rules.

| Where                       | Adopted               | Length                       | What it pauses                                                             |
| --------------------------- | --------------------- | ---------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| DeKalb County, Georgia      | July 2025             | 100 days                     | permits for new or expanded data centers in unincorporated areas           |
| Dane County, Wisconsin      | June 2026             | 18 months                    | hyperscale data center development                                         |
| Missoula County, Montana    | July 2026             | up to a year, renewable once | data centers on zoned and unzoned county land, outside the city            |
| Montgomery County, Maryland | July 2026             | 18 months                    | new data centers, voted unanimously beside a zoning amendment defining one |
| Pima County, Arizona        | September 2026        | 120 days                     | projects that had not yet applied for permits, voted 3–2                   |
| New York State              | July 14, 2026         | up to a year                 | state environmental permits for data centers of 50 megawatts or more       |
| Texas                       | August–September 2026 | until state audits finish    | grid connections in the ERCOT region, then all state data center permits   |

The two states came from opposite parties. **[Kathy Hochul](kloom:e/kathy-hochul)**, [New York](kloom:e/new-york-state)'s Democratic governor, paused state permits while regulators write a generic environmental impact statement on data centers' energy, water and air, saying data center development "threatens to hike up utility bills, deplete our natural resources." **[Greg Abbott](kloom:e/greg-abbott)**, the Republican governor of **[Texas](kloom:e/texas)**, first ordered the grid operator, **[ERCOT](kloom:e/electric-reliability-council-of-texas)**, to hold new data center connections until each project was audited, then told the state's environmental agency to issue no permits: "Data centers must pay their own way, protect our grid and water, and complete the ERCOT and TWDB audits." A state water survey had drawn answers from as few as 28% of data centers, the _Texas Tribune_ reported.

No one counts local pauses with a published method. The National Association of Counties' newspaper wrote in October 2026 that hundreds of counties had adopted one, most since that summer: a reporter's estimate. Data Center Watch, funded by 10a Labs and weighed in _The fight over data centers_, claims at least 75 projects worth about $130 billion were blocked or delayed in the first quarter of 2026.

## Votes

In **[Cascade Locks, Oregon](kloom:e/cascade-locks-oregon)**, a town of 1,379, the port offered a vacant building to a new company, Roundhouse Digital Infrastructure, for a $100 million data center. Opponents doubted the town's power supply could carry it and that the company could finance it. On June 13, 2023, voters recalled two port commissioners who had backed it, 285 to 130 and 266 to 136, on a turnout of 41% (the county's official count). The campaign also turned on the port's management, so the vote was not about the data center alone. Five weeks later the port and the developer ended the project: "the level of community support required for its successful implementation was not achieved."

In **[Warrenton, Virginia](kloom:e/warrenton-virginia)**, the town council approved an **[Amazon](kloom:e/amazon-company)** data center 4–3 in February 2023. Residents sued, arguing that the 2021 zoning change allowing it was void. In November 2024 voters elected five council candidates who had pledged openness, and engineers at Exponent wrote that voters had removed every council member who supported the project. The new council removed data centers as a permitted use in July 2025. Construction has been on hold under a consent order since January 2025; Amazon says its rights to build have vested. A judge heard final arguments in June 2026, Amazon's lawyer warning that zoning open to challenge at any time would bring "chaotic conditions beyond all comprehension." We found no ruling since.

In **[Independence, Missouri](kloom:e/independence-missouri)**, three of the five council members who voted in March 2026 for tax breaks for Nebius's AI campus lost their seats: two in April's election, and one recalled on September 1 by about 68% of his district. Construction continued.

At the ballot box, **[Port Washington, Wisconsin](kloom:e/port-washington-wisconsin)**, passed the first local referendum restricting data centers in April 2026, about two to one. On November 3, **[Ballotpedia](kloom:e/ballotpedia)** counted 41 local measure contests on data centers. In 40, voters sided with limits:

| Result on November 3, 2026                                      | Contests |
| --------------------------------------------------------------- | -------: |
| Limits approved: bans, partial bans, moratoriums                |       20 |
| Limits approved: voter approval, zoning, guarantees, opposition |        7 |
| Measures favoring data centers defeated                         |       13 |
| A review requirement defeated (Harwood, N.D.)                   |        1 |

By November 13 its count for the year was 46 of 47.

## For pausing, and against

**The case for pausing**, as the counties make it: a hyperscale campus is new, and the codes were written for smaller buildings. "Back then, a data center meant something very different than what it means right now," a Missoula commissioner said of his county's 2021 rules. Both governors gave similar reasons: bills, the grid and water. A pause buys time to learn what to ask.

**The case against**, as the industry makes it: the Data Center Coalition, its trade group, told Maryland's legislature in February 2026 that a statewide moratorium "would still send a strong negative signal to industry" and would keep counties from pursuing investment they want. A county planner in Washington State called pauses "just kicking the can down the road." Developers point to cost: in [Montgomery County](kloom:e/montgomery-county-maryland) one said it could not proceed, noting that a power transformer takes 12 to 36 months to obtain. Analysts at the **[Brookings Institution](kloom:e/brookings-institution)** granted that a pause can give communities "the time they need," but warned it "will not slow the national momentum" behind projects already underway, and urged oversight instead. Opponents want more than a pause: "We're looking for them to strengthen the rules on data centers," said a member of Tucson's No Desert Data Center Coalition when [Pima County](kloom:e/pima-county-arizona) voted.

## What is not known

Most of these pauses have not yet run out, so whether they end in rules, bans or approvals is unknown. No one has measured whether a project stopped in one county is built in the next. Each ballot measure was voted on in a place that chose to hold the vote, so its margin describes that place, not the country. New York's review, Texas's audits and Warrenton's case were all unfinished in November 2026. The trail _Elsewhere_ follows the countries that paused first, beginning with Singapore in 2019.
