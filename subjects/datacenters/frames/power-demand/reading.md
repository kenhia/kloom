No meter counts the electricity every data center uses. The national figures are estimates, built up from how many servers, drives and switches were shipped, how much power each draws and how the buildings are cooled. The best-known American series is **[Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory](kloom:e/lawrence-berkeley-national-laboratory)**'s, made for the Department of Energy at Congress's request. Its December 2024 report put data centers at about 60 terawatt-hours a year from 2014 to 2016, 76 in 2018 (1.9% of the country's electricity) and 176 in 2023 (4.4%). Its June 2026 update revised the past slightly down and put 2024 at 192 TWh, 4.7%. For the world, the **[International Energy Agency](kloom:e/international-energy-agency)** estimated about 415 TWh in 2024, around 1.5% of all electricity, and in April 2026 about 485 TWh in 2025, up 17% in a year.

These past figures are not meter readings. They are modeled, and the report says that "the lack of data availability significantly limits the analysis." Other methods land close: the **[Electric Power Research Institute](kloom:e/electric-power-research-institute)**, a nonprofit that does its research with more than 450 companies, estimated 177 to 192 TWh for 2024 from data on the buildings themselves.

The Berkeley range turns on how many AI chips will be shipped, how hard they will be run and how they will be cooled; EPRI's turns on how many announced projects get built. EPRI raised its own forecast by about 60% in under two years. The grid operator **[PJM Interconnection](kloom:e/pjm-interconnection)** raised its 2030 peak by 16 gigawatts in January 2025, then trimmed the near years in January 2026 once it required firm commitments, such as signed service agreements, before counting a data center.

## The forecasters' record

High forecasts have been wrong before. In 1999 Mark Mills, in a report for the Greening Earth Society, claimed that the internet used about 8% of American electricity and the "digital economy" 13%, and that the sector would use half within a decade. He and Peter Huber summed it up in [_Forbes_](kloom:e/forbes) as "Dig more coal—the PCs are coming." **[Jonathan Koomey](kloom:e/jonathan-koomey)** and colleagues at Berkeley, in work funded by the EPA, checked his inputs against measurements and found his total too high by about a factor of eight: all office, telephone and network equipment came to about 3%. Mills defended his estimate before a House subcommittee in 2000. Koomey's rebuttal named Mills's sponsor as an offshoot of the **[Western Fuels Association](kloom:e/western-fuels-association)**, a coal consortium. Later estimates bore Koomey out: data centers used 1.5% of the country's electricity in 2006, by the 2007 report to Congress, and their use grew only about 4% from 2010 to 2014.

Low forecasts have been wrong too. The laboratory's 2016 report expected about 73 TWh in 2020; its 2024 successor put 2018 alone at 76. And since 2023 most forecasts have been raised. The 2024 report offers a caution for both directions: "historical utility demand forecasts consistently overestimate both peak and average demand."

## Requests and buildings

Some of the demand in the forecasts is a request, not a building. EPRI writes that "many announced projects are speculative"; the IEA that data centers "often oversize their grid connections initially." Requests can be counted twice: in 2025 PJM proposed that utilities ask whether a data center's request duplicates one made elsewhere. After **[Ohio](kloom:e/ohio)** regulators made new data centers commit to paying for most of the capacity they reserve, **[American Electric Power](kloom:e/american-electric-power)** cut its data center forecast there from 30 GW to 13 GW. The Ohio Manufacturers' Association, whose members are large customers themselves, says even that is inflated by at least 3.3 GW. By ICF analysts' count, utilities in PJM cut their near-term forecasts by another 6.5 GW in October 2026. The opposite risk is real as well: PJM's last two capacity auctions bought less than its reliability standard requires, and a consultant it hired projects a shortfall of about 11.5 GW by 2030.

The demand that does arrive is concentrated. Fifteen states held about 80% of it in 2023, by EPRI's estimate, and in **[Virginia](kloom:e/virginia)** data centers used about a quarter of all electricity, the only state over 20%. EPRI projects 39% to 57% there by 2030.

## What is not known

No one publishes a meter reading for the industry, so even the past is an estimate, and the future depends on chips, prices and AI's uptake. A forecast is also a decision: utilities build, and charge, for the load they expect. Who pays for that grid is the next frame's question.
