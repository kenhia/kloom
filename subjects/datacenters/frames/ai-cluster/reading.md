An AI training cluster is a [data center](kloom:e/data-center) built for one job: teaching a model. Thousands of graphics chips work on it together for weeks, and at every step each one waits for the others, so they must sit close together and talk over very fast networks. In May 2020 **[Microsoft](kloom:e/microsoft)** announced that it had built such a machine in its cloud for **[OpenAI](kloom:e/openai)**: more than 285,000 processor cores, 10,000 graphics chips and 400 gigabits a second of network for each GPU server. It would not say where. In May 2023 its president, Brad Smith, said that it had built an AI supercomputing data center in Iowa so that OpenAI could train **[GPT-4](kloom:e/gpt-4)**, which "was really made in Iowa". The Associated Press placed it at Microsoft's campus in **[West Des Moines](kloom:e/west-des-moines-iowa)**.

## Sixteen thousand chips as one machine

The fullest public description of such a cluster is **[Meta](kloom:e/meta-platforms)**'s own, in the 2024 paper on its **[Llama](kloom:e/llama-language-model)** 3 models. The largest, Llama 3 405B, was trained on up to 16,384 **[graphics processing units](kloom:e/graphics-processing-unit)**, **[Nvidia](kloom:e/nvidia)**'s H100, "each running at 700W TDP", its rated power. Eight GPUs share a server, two servers a rack, and the racks are joined in three layers of switches, a design the paper calls a Clos network. The plate draws it.

| Layer of the network | What it joins | GPUs at that layer |
| -------------------- | ------------- | -----------------: |
| Top-of-rack switch   | 2 servers     |                 16 |
| Cluster switches     | 192 racks     |              3,072 |
| Aggregation switches | 8 pods        |       about 24,000 |

Those 16 GPUs are rated at 11.2 kilowatts together, by our arithmetic, before the processors, memory and fans beside them: more than the under 10 kilowatts that most operators in the Uptime Institute's 2026 survey reported for their usual rack, though well short of the 132-kilowatt racks of the newest liquid-cooled chips described in _Inside a data center_. Meta's model card gives 30.84 million GPU-hours for the 405B model. At the 700-watt rating that is about 21.6 gigawatt-hours, by our arithmetic again, and an upper bound: **[Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory](kloom:e/lawrence-berkeley-national-laboratory)** measured H100 servers in compute-heavy training drawing about 78% of their rated power. Meta estimated the training's emissions at 8,930 tons of carbon dioxide equivalent by the grid's mix where it ran, and zero by its own accounting, which counts the renewable electricity it buys to match its use. Both are Meta's figures, and which accounting should count is argued in _Counting a data center's carbon_.

Working as one has a cost the grid can see. "During training, tens of thousands of GPUs may increase or decrease power consumption at the same time," the paper says, when they wait for a checkpoint to be saved or the job starts or stops, causing "instant fluctuations of power consumption across the data center on the order of tens of megawatts, stretching the limits of the power grid." The **[International Energy Agency](kloom:e/international-energy-agency)** wrote in 2026 that AI data centers "see repeated swings of server load of more than 50% of rated capacity within a second." Batteries on site can smooth them, and whether such loads can also bend to the grid's needs is the question of _Data centers as flexible load_.

## Water at one cluster

In 2023 Matt O'Brien and Hannah Fingerhut of the Associated Press asked how much water the Iowa cluster used. Microsoft has said it cools with outside air until the temperature passes 29.3 °C (about 85 °F), and only then draws water. In July 2022, the month before OpenAI says it finished training GPT-4, the West Des Moines Water Works counted about 11.5 million gallons delivered to Microsoft's data centers there, about 6% of the district's water that month: a utility's own meter reading, reported by the AP. The water works had written in 2022 that it would "only consider future data center projects" from Microsoft that cut peak water use; it also told the AP that the company had been a good partner and was working with local officials to reduce its water use. How that month compares with the national totals is _How much water data centers use_.

## Training and running

A model is trained once, or a few times; _inference_, running the trained model to answer people, happens every time it is used. Which uses more is mostly estimated. **[Google](kloom:e/google)**'s researchers measured their own company's machine learning from 2019 to 2021 and found about three-fifths of its energy went to inference and two-fifths to training. The Berkeley laboratory's 2024 report says inference "could dramatically exceed training" but is "significantly harder to model with confidence"; it assumed inference servers draw half the power of training ones, and estimated that American AI servers used more than 40 terawatt-hours in 2023, from under 2 in 2017. The IEA reported in 2026 that the energy used per AI task has been falling "by at least an order of magnitude annually," while newer uses, video and reasoning among them, can take "hundreds or thousands of times more energy per query." No one publishes a measured national split.

The densest chips are now cooled by liquid piped to the chip itself, as Nvidia's GB200 NVL72 racks are. What that does to a site's water depends on how the heat leaves the building, which is _Where a data center's water goes_.

What is not known is how much of this power and water each kind of AI use takes, since companies report totals, if anything. The clusters, meanwhile, have outgrown their buildings: the next step is the gigawatt campus.
