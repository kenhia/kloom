A grid is built for its busiest hours: a hot afternoon, a cold morning, when every air conditioner or heater is on. The rest of the year much of that capacity sits partly idle. If a new data center agreed to cut its draw in those few hours, it might connect to the wires and power plants already there, sooner and without new ones being built for it. That is the case for _flexible load_, an old idea, **[demand response](kloom:e/demand-response)**, applied to a new kind of customer. Its supporters see the fastest way to add data centers without raising everyone's bills. Its doubters ask how much of a data center's work can really wait.

## The study behind the idea

In February 2025 four researchers at **[Duke University](kloom:e/duke-university)**'s Nicholas Institute, Tyler Norris, Tim Profeta, Dalia Patino-Echeverri and Adam Cowie-Haskell, published _Rethinking Load Growth_. They took nine years of hourly demand, 2016 to 2024, reported to the [Energy Information Administration](kloom:e/energy-information-administration) by 22 of the largest grid operators and utilities, covering 95% of the country's peak. For each they added a constant new load and asked how large it could be before demand passed the highest peak the system had already served, if the new load gave way whenever it otherwise would.

| New load cut back, share of its year | New load the grids could take | Hours a year cut back, on average | Average length of a cutback |
| -----------------------------------: | ----------------------------: | --------------------------------: | --------------------------: |
|                                0.25% |                         76 GW |                                85 |                   1.7 hours |
|                                 0.5% |                         98 GW |                               177 |                   2.1 hours |
|                                   1% |                        126 GW |                               366 |                   2.5 hours |

These numbers are **modeled**, and the authors say what the model leaves out: whether the transmission lines could carry the new load, how fast plants can ramp, and what the new load's own daily pattern would be. They called their result "a first-order assessment" whose results "cannot be taken as an accurate estimate of the load that can be added to the system", and argued that the potential is large even if overstated. The plate draws their method on one invented week: a data center on top of a grid's demand, cutting back only where the two together would pass the planned peak.

## Who is trying it

In October 2024 the **[Electric Power Research Institute](kloom:e/electric-power-research-institute)** started DCFlex, a set of field trials whose founding members included **[Google](kloom:e/google)**, Meta and **[Nvidia](kloom:e/nvidia)**, utilities, and two grid operators, [PJM](kloom:e/pjm-interconnection) and the **[Electric Reliability Council of Texas](kloom:e/electric-reliability-council-of-texas)** (ERCOT). In August 2025 Google announced agreements with Indiana Michigan Power, for a new data center in Fort Wayne, and with the **[Tennessee Valley Authority](kloom:e/tennessee-valley-authority)**, to cut back machine-learning work when the grid is strained: its first demand response aimed at AI rather than at tasks such as processing videos. By March 2026 it said it had written 1 gigawatt of demand response into its utility contracts. Each of these is **claimed**: what the company and its partners have announced.

The fullest public account of a trial that we found is a vendor's own. Emerald AI, a start-up selling the scheduling software, ran 256 AI chips in an [Oracle](kloom:e/oracle-corporation) cloud data center in Phoenix and, on May 1 and 3, 2025, during the peaks of Arizona Public Service and the **[Salt River Project](kloom:e/salt-river-project)**, cut their power by 25% for three hours, to targets the utilities set, with every job finishing within its agreed limits. Its paper, written with engineers from Nvidia, EPRI and the Salt River Project, reports **measured** power, but for one cluster over two afternoons, not a campus over a summer.

## The law, and the doubts

In June 2025 **[Texas](kloom:e/texas)** made a version of the idea law. Senate Bill 6 requires large customers connected to the transmission system after 2025 to be fitted so they can be cut off in a rolling blackout, and lets ERCOT, in an emergency, order a customer of 75 megawatts or more that has backup generators to switch to them or curtail. It also asks ERCOT to buy demand reductions from such loads, called with at least a day's notice.

That clause is one of the doubts. A data center that leaves the grid by starting its own diesel or gas engines relieves the grid but moves the emissions to its neighbors (_Backup diesel generators_). Another is the work itself. Emerald AI's paper says that "not all AI workloads are temporally flexible", and it left real-time services untouched; Google writes that "there are limits to how flexible a given data center can be" for services such as Search and for customers in healthcare. Grid operators have also seen the opposite of a planned cutback. On July 10, 2024, a fault on a 230,000-volt line in the East led about 1,500 megawatts of data centers, by their own protective controls, to drop off the grid at once and run on backup power. The **[North American Electric Reliability Corporation](kloom:e/north-american-electric-reliability-corporation)** reported that operators had not anticipated it, and warned planners to keep such losses from growing to intolerable levels.

## What has been measured

Curtailment by large flexible loads has a record in Texas, but mostly from cryptocurrency mines, which can stop at will: the Duke report counts more than 750 megawatts of mining registered with ERCOT as controllable load, and cites one miner's own account of cutting 95% at peaks. For AI data centers the public record, as of November 2026, is announcements, contracts and short trials. Whether a gigawatt campus will cut back when called, on the hottest afternoon of a heat wave, for as long as asked, has not yet been shown, and the next section turns from power to water.
