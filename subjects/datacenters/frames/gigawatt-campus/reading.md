A gigawatt is a billion watts. A campus drawing one gigawatt around the clock for a year would use 8.76 billion kilowatt-hours. The Energy Information Administration counted the average American home's purchases at 10,791 kilowatt-hours in 2022, so that is the yearly electricity of about 812,000 homes, by our arithmetic, or about 593,000 homes in **[Louisiana](kloom:e/louisiana)**, where homes use more. A campus rarely runs at its full rating all year, so this is a ceiling. For scale, the Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory's estimate for every data center in the country in 2023 works out to an average draw of about 20 gigawatts, by our arithmetic. Since 2024, single campuses have been announced at one to five gigawatts.

**[Stargate](kloom:e/stargate-llc)** was announced at the White House on January 21, 2025, by **[OpenAI](kloom:e/openai)**, **[SoftBank](kloom:e/softbank-group)** and **[Oracle](kloom:e/oracle-corporation)** with the investor MGX, as a company that "intends to invest $500 billion over the next four years." That is a stated intention, and the case was made in the same words: the buildings would "secure American leadership in AI, create hundreds of thousands of American jobs," and serve "the national security of America and its allies." In September 2025 OpenAI said its sites came to "nearly 7 gigawatts of planned capacity." The first is at **[Abilene](kloom:e/abilene-texas)**, **[Texas](kloom:e/texas)**, where Crusoe builds on land owned by the developer Lancium and Oracle runs the buildings. On its earnings call in September 2026 Oracle said six of the eight buildings, 618 megawatts, had been delivered. Epoch's estimate, which draws on that statement and on its own images, has all eight running by November. A further expansion there was dropped in March 2026, Bloomberg reported, amid financing difficulties and OpenAI's changing forecasts of demand; OpenAI said it had considered expanding but "ultimately chose to put that additional capacity in other locations."

**[Meta](kloom:e/meta-platforms)** chose **[Richland Parish](kloom:e/richland-parish-louisiana)**, a farming parish in northeast Louisiana, for **[Hyperion](kloom:e/hyperion-data-center)**, announced in December 2024 on a site of 2,250 acres, since enlarged. Meta says it will pay for the energy, water and infrastructure it uses. Mark Zuckerberg wrote in July 2025 that it would reach five gigawatts, and a company spokesperson said two would come online by 2030. Its gas plants, and who else may pay for them, are _Gas plants for data centers_. **[Microsoft](kloom:e/microsoft)** says its **Fairwater** in Wisconsin is cooled in a closed loop, which _Zero-water cooling_ examines. **[xAI](kloom:e/spacexai)** built **[Colossus](kloom:e/colossus-data-center)** in a former factory in **[Memphis](kloom:e/memphis-tennessee)** in 2024; the gas turbines that powered it are _xAI's gas turbines in Memphis_.

## Who decides what is real

Opponents ask what such scale costs everyone else. The International Energy Agency, which advises its member governments, wrote in 2026 that campuses "are likely to trigger a need for new generation and grid investments in systems that host them," and that a mismatch between quick buildings and slow power investments "could raise electricity prices in some places if unmitigated." The reply is that large loads spread a grid's fixed costs over more sales: the Berkeley laboratory and the Brattle Group found that from 2019 to 2025 real prices generally fell in the states whose demand grew most, though not where supply was tight (_Who pays for the grid_). An announcement costs little. A connection to the grid is where a project becomes real, and a developer may ask more than one utility for power for the same project. The agency also wrote that "actual peak load from data centres is often uncertain," as campuses fill with servers over time and "often oversize their grid connections initially." Texas answered with Senate Bill 6, signed on June 20, 2025: a customer asking for 75 megawatts or more must disclose whether it "is pursuing a substantially similar request for electric service in this state," pay at least $100,000 for the first study, show it controls the site and put up money for the lines. The law's stated aims run both ways, to "support business development" while "minimizing the potential for stranded infrastructure costs." How far requests outrun what gets built is in _How much electricity data centers use_.

## The land

The plate draws three sites to one scale. Hyperion's 2,250 acres are about three and a half square miles. In Abilene, Lancium owned about 1,045 acres in 2022, before the buildings went up. Fairwater's first phase covers 315 acres. In Richland Parish, Meta says the project's taxes made teachers' yearly bonus checks 400% higher than the year before; some residents of nearby Holly Ridge have complained of the construction traffic. What neighbors gain and lose is the subject of _The neighbors_.

## Where it is going

Everything after today in this frame is projection. Epoch expects Hyperion's first phase to run by 2028, and the IEA projects the world's data centers to use about 950 terawatt-hours in 2030, roughly twice 2025's 485. Abilene's dropped expansion, moved elsewhere by OpenAI's account, shows that an announced figure can change. What is not known is how many announced gigawatts will be built, who will pay for the power if demand falls short, and whether the work these campuses do is worth their cost, which is the question _Why we build them_ takes up.
