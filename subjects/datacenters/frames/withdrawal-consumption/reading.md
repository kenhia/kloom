Water that passes through a building can be counted in three ways, and the **[United States Geological Survey](kloom:e/united-states-geological-survey)** (USGS), which keeps the national water accounts, defines each. A _withdrawal_ is water taken from a river, a lake or an aquifer, directly or through a town's utility. _Consumptive use_ is the part of it "evaporated, transpired, incorporated in products or crops, consumed by humans or livestock, or otherwise removed from the immediate water environment." The rest goes back as _return flow_: down a sewer to a treatment plant, or out of a pipe into a stream, where it is available to the next user downstream, sometimes warmer or carrying more dissolved salts than when it came in. Withdrawal minus return flow is consumption. A data center can honestly be said to use two very different amounts of water.

## One site's three numbers

Few operators publish all three numbers for each site. **[Google](kloom:e/google)** does. These are the company's own figures for 2025, from meters or water bills where it has them and from estimates where it does not; a third party gave them limited assurance.

| 2025, million gallons    | Withdrawn | Discharged | Consumed | Share consumed |
| ------------------------ | --------: | ---------: | -------: | -------------: |
| Council Bluffs, Iowa     |   1,746.4 |      400.4 |  1,346.0 |            77% |
| All Google data centers  |    13,562 |      3,039 |   10,523 |            78% |
| Google, with its offices |    14,689 |      3,820 |   10,869 |            74% |

**[Council Bluffs](kloom:e/council-bluffs-iowa)** was the company's largest user. Most of what a site like it consumes leaves its **[cooling towers](kloom:e/cooling-tower)** as vapor. The rest of the withdrawal is mostly _blowdown_. As water evaporates in a tower it leaves its calcium, magnesium, chloride and silica behind, and the concentration rises until scale or corrosion threatens the equipment; so part of the circulating water is drained off and replaced with fresh. The guidance of the **[Environmental Protection Agency](kloom:e/united-states-environmental-protection-agency)** says that operators usually hire a water-treatment vendor to dose the water with chemicals against scale, corrosion and microbes. The discharge therefore carries several times the minerals of the water that came in, with traces of those chemicals, as any cooling tower's does, and goes to a sewer and a treatment plant, or back to a river. _How a cooling tower works_ follows that budget.

## Two numbers, mixed

The public argument often blurs the two, on both sides. In June 2025 the Environmental and Energy Study Institute, a Washington nonprofit that advocates a move away from fossil fuels, wrote that "large data centers can consume up to 5 million gallons per day, equivalent to the water use of a town populated by 10,000 to 50,000 people"; the same piece defined "water consumption" as "the amount of water withdrawn." In October 2026 USAFacts, a nonprofit data site founded by Steve Ballmer, the former chief executive of Microsoft, tried to trace the 5 million figure and ended at "researchers say": "It's a number that's often referenced, but never sourced." Its analyst, noting a Virginia utility's agreement to _reserve_ up to 2 million gallons a day for one campus, wondered whether the figure "would be better characterized as 'reserved' rather than 'used.'"

Mixing can also make a data center look smaller. USAFacts set the 17 billion gallons that [Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory](kloom:e/lawrence-berkeley-national-laboratory) estimates American data centers consumed on site in 2023 beside a household's 300 gallons a day, about 155,000 homes. But a home's 300 gallons is a withdrawal, most of it returned through the drains. Eric Masanet of the University of California, Santa Barbara, whose 2026 review of the field was funded by the ClimateWorks Foundation, his university and Japan's trade ministry, says such a comparison is "misleading": on a consumption basis the number of homes "would be much higher." Of the 18 studies he found that offered a comparison, 11 were technically incorrect, vague or unsourced.

Companies, for their part, lead with consumption. Google's report gives its 10.9 billion gallons in the text, beside "73 golf courses," and its withdrawal of 14.7 billion in its data tables, site by site. Google's engineers make the case for that choice: consumption "best represent[s] our impact on local water availability, as it represents the volume of water that is evaporated and therefore unavailable for reuse." Over a river basin and a year, the USGS's definition bears that out. But a town's water utility has to pump, treat and deliver every gallon withdrawn, on the hottest afternoon of the year, and treat what comes back. Withdrawal, at its peak, is what sizes its pipes. _Small water systems, big campuses_ takes up that case.

## Which water

The kind of water matters too. Of Google's 13.6 billion gallons withdrawn for data centers in 2025, 81% was potable water, treated to drinking standard; 12% was non-potable, and 7% was reclaimed wastewater. The company leaves seawater and brackish water out of its figures altogether.

For scale, the USGS's latest national assessment, for the water years 2010 to 2020, puts freshwater withdrawals for irrigation, public supply and thermoelectric power at an average of 223,594 million gallons a day. Irrigation consumed 75,698 million of them. Power plants withdrew 82,656 million and consumed only 2,904, the widest gap of the three, which _The water behind the electricity_ explains.

What is not known is the three numbers for most sites: discharge is often estimated rather than metered (Google uses "engineering principles" where it lacks a meter), most operators publish little, and the national estimates are models. Where the evaporating happens, and why it takes so much, is the next frame's subject.
