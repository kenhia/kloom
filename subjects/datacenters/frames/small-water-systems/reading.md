A large data center usually takes its water from the same public system as the houses around it. In a city of millions that is one customer among many. In a small town, or a county whose growth paused in 2008, it can be the largest customer the utility has, and the argument becomes local and specific: whose water, on which days, and who pays for the next pipe. Two places show the argument from both ends, and a 2026 study puts a number on the part that yearly totals miss.

## The Dalles: the numbers made public

In 2021 Mike Rogoway of **[_The Oregonian_](kloom:e/the-oregonian)** asked **[The Dalles](kloom:e/the-dalles-oregon)**, Oregon, how much water **[Google](kloom:e/google)**'s data centers used. The city refused, calling the figures a trade secret. When the Wasco County district attorney ordered them released, the city sued the newspaper to keep them private, a fight Google paid for, contributing more than $100,000. Google worried that competitors would learn how it cools its servers, the mayor said. After thirteen months, in December 2022, the city settled with the paper, represented by the **[Reporters Committee for Freedom of the Press](kloom:e/reporters-committee-for-freedom-of-the-press)**, releasing ten years of records and promising future years on request. Google said it would no longer treat any American site's water use as a trade secret.

The city's records are measured: they are its meters. In 2021 Google took 29% of the city's water. The later years, in the accent color, are Google's own reports of what it withdrew; it says it consumed 469 million gallons of 2025's 654, and Oregon Public Broadcasting put its share at about a third of the city's water in 2024. Google's side of the account is investment: more than $28 million for the city's wells, reservoirs and pumps, including an aquifer storage system, finished in 2025, that banks winter water for summer, given to the city with its water rights. The city plans about $260 million of upgrades over twenty years, which it expects Google's payments to cover almost a quarter of; water rates are projected to rise 99% by 2036. Google "is really not a factor in this at all," Mayor Richard Mays told OPB of a planned reservoir expansion; John DeVoe of WaterWatch of Oregon answered that the water is not mainly for residents. The mayor later called OPB's reporting inaccurate and biased, and OPB stood by it.

## Newton County: what was and was not measured

In July 2025 Eli Tan of **[_The New York Times_](kloom:e/the-new-york-times)** reported from **[Newton County](kloom:e/newton-county-georgia)**, **[Georgia](kloom:e/georgia-u-s-state)**, where **[Meta](kloom:e/meta-platforms)** began building in 2018. Beverly and Jeff Morris, whose well is 1,000 feet from the campus, said their taps filled with sediment and slowed to a trickle months after construction began; three neighbors described problems too. Meta told the paper it had commissioned a study of the Morrises' well and that it was "unlikely" its data center had affected the groundwater. A spokesman for the development authority that runs the industrial park called the cause unknown: "One thousand feet away is a pretty significant distance." No measurement linking the well to the site, or clearing it, has been published that we could find. What the county's water authority did report is scale: Meta's campus uses about 10% of the county's water, and nine more companies had applied, some for as much as 6 million gallons a day, more than the county uses. Meta says it pays the full cost of its water and wastewater service.

## Peak, not annual

A utility builds its plant for the hottest day of the year, and that is when evaporative cooling uses the most. A 2026 preprint by Yuelin Han, Pengfei Li, Adam Wierman and Shaolei Ren, of the University of California, Riverside and Caltech, "Small Bottle, Big Pipe," finds that a data center's busiest day often draws six times its average, sometimes more than ten, far above other users'. If water use per unit of computing stays at 2024's level, they project, American data centers will need 697 to 1,451 million gallons a day of new capacity by 2030, against New York City's average supply of about 1,000; if it falls 10% a year, 227 to 604. These are projections from companies' 2024 reports and an average peak factor of 4.5, which the authors call conservative; the paper is not yet peer-reviewed, and two of its authors wrote the higher of the estimates that _The water in a chatbot answer_ compares.

The drawing is a schematic of the problem, not of any town: a summer of daily demand under a plant's capacity, with a campus's evaporative load added on hot days.

## What a town can ask for

The town's case is revenue and pipes paid for by someone else; the residents' case is that capacity promised to a campus is gone for them. Between the two, towns write water agreements, as The Dalles did; cap large users, as Phoenix has (_Data centers in dry places_); or supply recycled wastewater, which **[Loudoun Water](kloom:e/loudoun-county-sanitation-authority)** in Virginia has piped to data centers since 2010, more than 750 million gallons in 2025. The preprint's authors propose "Pipe Neutral" commitments: a campus adds as much capacity as it takes. Whether any town has the peak-day records to hold a company to that is not yet known. The designs that need no evaporation at all are the subject of _Zero-water cooling_.
