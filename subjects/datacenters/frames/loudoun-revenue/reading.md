In **[Loudoun County](kloom:e/loudoun-county-virginia)**, a data center pays more tax on the computers inside it than on the building around them: in the county's 2027 budget, about twice as much. **[Virginia](kloom:e/virginia)** lets its counties tax business equipment as personal property, every year, at a rate each county sets, and Loudoun taxes computer equipment at its general rate: $4.20 per $100 of assessed value until 2022, $4.15 since. In the fiscal year that ended in June 2025, the county's budget records $699.8 million collected on computer equipment in data centers, measured, after the fact. That is 28% of all the local tax revenue in its general fund, by our arithmetic. The county's own summary for fiscal 2026 counts $1.2 billion in real and personal property tax from data centers, 39% of its budget, and its adopted budget for 2027 projects $1.3 billion, 40%.

## How a server is taxed

The tax falls on a share of what the equipment cost, a share that shrinks each year it is kept. The county's Commissioner of the Revenue sets the schedule, and lengthened it for 2026 after a benchmarking study.

| Year of purchase, counted back from the tax year | Share assessed before 2026 | Share assessed from 2026 |
| ------------------------------------------------ | -------------------------: | -----------------------: |
| Last year                                        |                        50% |                      60% |
| Two years before                                 |                        40% |                      45% |
| Three years before                               |                        30% |                      30% |
| Four years before                                |                        20% |                      15% |
| Five years before                                |                        10% |                      10% |
| Six or more years before                         |                        10% |                       5% |

A worked example, by our arithmetic: a server that cost $10,000 in 2025 is assessed at $6,000 for 2026 and taxed $249. Over its first five years it pays about $664, against $623 under the old schedule. Data center equipment, the state's auditors at JLARC found, is "typically replaced every five years," and new equipment starts the schedule again at the top, as the plate draws. That is why the revenue follows how fast operators refit as much as how many buildings stand. The rate matters as much: JLARC worked out that $150 million of equipment would pay anywhere from $10.8 million to $0.4 million over five years, depending on the Virginia county it sat in.

The buildings and land pay real property tax besides. Until 2027 the budget did not show data centers' share of it; the 2027 budget does, and expects $417.7 million, against $879.1 million from their equipment.

## What it paid for

The county says the money let the Board of Supervisors "generally lower the real property tax rate for homeowners every year for the past decade," fund increases for **[Loudoun County Public Schools](kloom:e/loudoun-county-public-schools)** "amounting to tens of millions of dollars year-over-year," pay for deputies, fire and rescue, parks and libraries, and cut the tax on residents' cars from $4.15 to $3.09 for 2026.

| -------- | ---------------------------: | -------- | ---------------------------: |
| 2013     |                        1.205 | 2021     |                        0.980 |

| 2017     |                        1.125 | 2024     |                        0.865 |
| 2019     |                        1.045 | 2026     |                        0.805 |

Supporters read this as a county that diversified out of trouble, and the reading is not theirs alone: "The data centers kind of bailed them out," Sarah Parmelee of the **[Piedmont Environmental Council](kloom:e/piedmont-environmental-council)**, a regional conservation group, told NOTUS in 2026. Critics answer that a lower rate is not a lower bill: by our arithmetic from the budget's averages, the tax on an average home rose from about $4,940 in 2015 to about $6,470 in 2026, because values rose faster than the rate fell, and the county says itself that bills "have varied due to increased property values." Supporters reply that without the equipment tax the rate would have had to be higher still; the budgets cited here do not say by how much. They add that the revenue is only one side of the ledger. "We can't just pretend that it's just revenue," said John McAuliff, elected to the **[Virginia General Assembly](kloom:e/virginia-general-assembly)** from western Loudoun in 2025. The power lines the buildings need, for one, are approved by the state rather than the county, and their cost is settled outside its budget.

## The county's own cautions

The county does not treat the money as permanent. Its finance staff expect equipment revenue to "plateau in the next five to 10 years," after which, if spending keeps growing, the board "will need to consider an increase in tax rates." Since fiscal 2020 it has put part of the equipment tax into one-time capital projects, so a fall would cut projects rather than services, and in October 2023 it set up a revenue stabilization fund, sized at 10% of data center property tax, against a shortfall. JLARC put data centers at 31% of Loudoun's local tax revenue in fiscal 2023, against 7% in neighboring Prince William.

When the growth stops, how fast new chips are bought and replaced, and whether the grid can serve the buildings already approved are all unknown, and the forecast rests on them. The last of these is the question of _Ashburn's power lines_.
