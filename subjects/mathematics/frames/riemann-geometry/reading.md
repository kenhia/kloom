An ant on an orange can tell that its world is curved without ever
leaving the peel. It has only to walk a large triangle and add up the
angles. **[Carl Friedrich Gauss](kloom:e/carl-friedrich-gauss)** proved in 1827 that a surface's curvature
can be measured entirely from inside it, and in 1854 his student
**[Bernhard Riemann](kloom:e/bernhard-riemann)** extended the idea to spaces of any number of
dimensions, including our own. Sixty years later it was the mathematics
[Einstein](kloom:e/albert-einstein) needed for gravity.

## Surveying Hanover

Gauss was an astronomer by profession. From 1820 he also ran an arc
measurement for the Kingdom of Hanover: a chain of triangles, carried from
hilltop to hilltop, to find the size and shape of the Earth along a
meridian, as Delambre and Méchain had done through France thirty years
before. In the summers from 1821 to 1825 he directed the work in the field
himself, with soldiers as assistants, sighting the stations with the
_heliotrope_, a mirror he invented to flash sunlight from one hill to the
next.

The survey turned Gauss to the geometry of curved surfaces. On 8 October
1827 he presented to the Royal Society of Göttingen the _Disquisitiones
generales circa superficies curvas_ (_General Investigations of Curved
Surfaces_), printed the next year. Its most famous result he called
remarkable, and it has been the **[Theorema Egregium](kloom:e/theorema-egregium)** since: "If a curved
surface is developed upon any other surface whatever, the measure of
curvature in each point remains unchanged." Bend a surface without
stretching it, and its curvature does not change, because the curvature
depends only on distances measured within the surface. That is why a
sheet of paper rolls into a cylinder but will not wrap a ball without
creasing, and why no flat map of the Earth can be true everywhere.

## Curvature from the inside

The paper also proves how an inhabitant of the surface could find the
curvature. On a flat plane a triangle's angles add up to 180°. On a
sphere they add up to more, and the excess, in radians, is the triangle's
area multiplied by the curvature, which for a sphere of radius _R_ is
1/_R_². The plate draws the simplest case: from the North Pole down two
meridians a quarter-turn apart to the equator. All three angles are right
angles, the sum is 270°, and the excess of 90°, a quarter of a turn or
π/2, is exactly one eighth of the sphere's area 4π on a sphere of radius 1.

The Earth's curvature is gentle, and the triangles of a survey are small
against it. The largest Gauss measured ran from the Hoher Hagen near
Göttingen to the Brocken in the Harz and the Inselsberg in the Thuringian
Forest, with sides of about 69, 85 and 107 km. His paper gives the excess
of its angles over 180° as 14.85348 seconds of arc. By our arithmetic,
Heron's formula gives the triangle an area of about 2,930 km², and on a
sphere of radius 6,378 km that area makes an excess of 14.85″, matching
Gauss. For him the excess was a correction to be made to measured
angles; the idea behind it was the larger gift.

## On the hypotheses at the foundations of geometry

To qualify as a lecturer at the **[University of Göttingen](kloom:e/university-of-gottingen)**, Riemann had
to give a trial lecture, and he proposed three topics. Two were on electricity
and one on geometry; against his expectation, Gauss chose geometry. On 10
June 1854 Riemann gave "Über die Hypothesen, welche der Geometrie zu Grunde
liegen", with Gauss in the audience. As his biographers tell it, the lecture
exceeded all Gauss's expectations, and he spoke of it afterwards to
Wilhelm Weber with rare enthusiasm.

Riemann defined a space of any number of dimensions, a _manifold_, and gave
it a rule for measuring: the length of a tiny step is the square root of a
quadratic expression in its coordinates, as in Gauss's surfaces, with
coefficients that may vary from place to place. From that rule alone the
curvature can be computed at every point and in every direction. Whether
our own space is flat, he argued, is therefore a matter of fact for
experiment, "not necessary, but only of empirical certainty". He ended by
suggesting that the ground of space's measure might lie "in binding forces
which act upon it", a question that "leads us into the domain of another
science, of physic". The lecture was published only in 1868, two years
after his death, by **[Richard Dedekind](kloom:e/richard-dedekind)**.

## Einstein's geometry

In 1912 **Albert Einstein**, back in Zurich and convinced that gravity
must bend the geometry of space and time, asked his old classmate **[Marcel
Grossmann](kloom:e/marcel-grossmann)**, now a professor of mathematics, for help. Grossmann showed
him **[Riemannian geometry](kloom:e/riemannian-geometry)** and the calculus that Ricci and Levi-Civita had
built on it. In the theory Einstein completed in November 1915, spacetime
is a four-dimensional Riemannian manifold, of a kind with one sign
changed, and its measuring rule is fixed by the matter in it, as
Riemann's closing guess had allowed.

Riemann's manifolds also asked which spaces can be deformed into which, if
lengths are ignored altogether. That is the subject of the next frame,
topology.
