Railway lines, which never meet, run together at the horizon. Painters of
the early fifteenth century in Florence made that into a rule: all the
lines that run straight away from the viewer are drawn to one point. The
rule is a piece of geometry, and two centuries later a French engineer
turned it into a new kind of geometry, one in which parallel lines really
do meet.

## A painting with a hole in it

The story of the first demonstration comes from the biography of **[Filippo
Brunelleschi](kloom:e/filippo-brunelleschi)** written in the 1480s, usually attributed to Antonio Manetti.
Brunelleschi, it says, painted the **[Florence Baptistery](kloom:e/florence-baptistery)** on a panel about
half a _braccio_ square (some 30 cm), as seen from about three _braccia_
inside the central door of the cathedral opposite. Where the sky should be
he laid burnished silver, to reflect the real clouds. He bored a hole through the panel, as small as a lentil on
the painted side and widening "like a woman's straw hat" behind. The viewer
looked through the back of the hole at a mirror held at arm's length, and
saw the painting where the building stood. "I have had it in my hands and
seen it many times," the biographer adds.

The panel is lost, and no document dates it. Estimates run from about 1415
to 1425: the historian Samuel Edgerton puts it around 1425. Manetti's details do not
all agree with one another. That it
happened, and that Florentine painters such as Masaccio soon painted by the
new rule, is not in doubt.

## Alberti's window

The first written account of the method is **[Leon Battista Alberti](kloom:e/leon-battista-alberti)**'s
_De pictura_ (_On Painting_), in Latin in 1435 and in Italian the next
year, with a dedication to Brunelleschi. A picture, Alberti said, is a
window through which we see the scene: a plane cut through the pyramid of
rays that runs from the eye to the things seen. Lines at right angles to
the window run to a single "centric point" level with the eye, and similar
triangles give how much each row of floor tiles shrinks. Edgerton notes
that Alberti put a window where Brunelleschi had put a mirror, and
added a frame strung with a grid of threads to draw through.

![A woodcut: two men in a room with a lute on a long table. A string runs from a point on the wall, through a wooden frame on the table, to the lute; one man holds the string at the lute while the other marks where it crosses the frame on a hinged board already covered in dots](durer-lute.jpg)

**[Piero della Francesca](kloom:e/piero-della-francesca)** worked the method out for solid bodies in
_De prospectiva pingendi_, probably between 1474 and 1482, and began the habit of teaching it
with drawings: solid bodies, and a human head, set out point by point.
**[Albrecht Dürer](kloom:e/albrecht-durer)** studied it from Italian painters and from Piero's and
Luca Pacioli's books, and his _Underweysung der Messung_
(Nuremberg, 1525) shows machines for doing it by hand. In the woodcut, the
string plays the ray of light, the nail in the wall plays the eye, and the
frame is Alberti's window: each point of the lute is fixed where its
string crosses the frame.

## Two triangles

**[Girard Desargues](kloom:e/girard-desargues)** was an architect and engineer from Lyon. His
**theorem** on triangles in perspective was first printed in 1648, in a
book on his method by his friend, the engraver Abraham Bosse. The plate
draws it.

Take a point _O_ and draw three lines out of it. Put one triangle, _abc_,
with a corner on each line, and a second, _ABC_, further out on the same
three lines. Now extend each side of the small triangle to meet the
matching side of the large one: _ab_ with _AB_, _bc_ with _BC_, _ca_ with
_CA_. However the triangles are chosen, the three meeting points lie on one
straight line, the axis.

The proof is a painter's. Suppose the two triangles lie in two different
planes, as a figure on the ground and its image on an upright picture do.
The lines _Oa_ and _Ob_ meet at _O_, so they lie in one plane, and in it
_ab_ and _AB_ must meet. That meeting point lies on the line _ab_, so it is
in the plane of the small triangle, and on _AB_, so it is in the plane of
the large one. The same goes for the other two pairs. Two planes meet in a
line, so all three points lie on it. The flat case follows by lifting the
figure out of the page and projecting it back.

## Points at infinity

But what if _ab_ and _AB_ are parallel? In 1639 Desargues had printed a
short, dense book on conic sections, the _Brouillon project_, which treated
parallel lines as lines that meet at an infinite distance, as the
painter's parallels meet at the horizon. With that point added, the
theorem has no exceptions. Few read him, and a printed copy of the book was
found only in 1951.

The idea came back through a soldier. **[Jean-Victor Poncelet](kloom:e/jean-victor-poncelet)**, an
engineer in Napoleon's army, was left for dead at the battle of Krasnoi in
November 1812 and marched to a prison camp at Saratov on the Volga. There,
in 1813 and 1814, with no books, he worked out from memory what he had
learned from his teachers and went beyond it. His _Traité des propriétés
projectives des figures_ (1822) studied what survives projection from a
point, and named **[projective geometry](kloom:e/projective-geometry)**. It added a point at infinity to
every line and a line at infinity to every plane, so that any two lines in
a plane meet.

Projective geometry kept Euclid's lines and gave up his measure. The next
frame gives up his postulate of the one parallel.
