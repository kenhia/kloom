Around 300 BC, in **[Alexandria](kloom:e/alexandria)**, the new Greek city on the coast of
Egypt, a teacher named **[Euclid](kloom:e/euclid)** put the mathematics of his time into
thirteen books. We know almost nothing else about him. What we have comes
from writers six and seven centuries later, and the best-known story, that
he told King Ptolemy there was "no royal road to geometry", is told of
another mathematician and Alexander the Great as well. What survives is the
book. The **[_Elements_](kloom:e/euclids-elements)** holds 465 propositions, from the triangle to the
five regular solids, and most of them were not new: the later commentator
Proclus says Euclid collected many of Eudoxus's theorems and perfected
many of Theaetetus's. What was new was the order.

## First principles

Book I begins with 23 definitions ("A point is that which has no part"),
then five postulates and five common notions, which everything after it is
to rest on. The postulates are things a geometer may do: draw a straight
line from any point to any point, extend it, "describe a circle with any
centre and distance". The common notions are about equality: "things which
are equal to the same thing are also equal to one another."

The fifth postulate is not like the others. It says that if a line crosses
two others and makes the inner angles on one side less than two right
angles, the two lines, extended far enough, meet on that side. Euclid put
off using it until Proposition 29. It is now called the
**[parallel postulate](kloom:e/parallel-postulate)**, and what came of the long attempt to prove it from
the other four is the story of non-Euclidean geometry, told later in this
subject. The plate draws it beside the first proposition: two lines, crossed
by a third, produced until they meet.

## Proposition 1

The first proposition shows the form every other one takes. In Thomas
Heath's translation of 1908 it reads: "On a given finite straight line to
construct an equilateral triangle."

Let the line be _AB_. With center _A_ and distance _AB_, draw a circle
(postulate 3). With center _B_ and distance _BA_, draw another (postulate
3 again). From _C_, where the circles cut one another, join _CA_ and _CB_
(postulate 1). Now _CA_ equals _AB_, because both are radii of the first
circle, and _CB_ equals _BA_, as radii of the second. Things equal to the
same thing are equal to one another (common notion 1), so _CA_ equals
_CB_, and all three sides are equal. "Being what it was required to do."

Each step gives its reason, and every reason is a postulate, a definition,
a common notion or an earlier proposition. That is the idea the _Elements_
passed on. It is also where later readers found the first gap: nothing in
the postulates says the two circles meet at all. Euclid took it from the
figure, and modern axiom systems had to add it.

Book VII opens with the procedure now called Euclid's algorithm: subtract the smaller of two numbers from the larger, again and again, and the last remainder measures both. Book IX has the proof that the **[primes](kloom:e/prime-number)** never end (Proposition 20). Take
any primes you like, multiply them and add one. The result is either a new
prime or divisible by one, and that prime cannot be any of those you took,
since it would then divide 1. The primes trail follows the question on
from here.

## Copies of copies

Euclid's own copy is long gone. The oldest scraps are ostraca from
Elephantine of the third century BC and a papyrus from Oxyrhynchus of
about AD 100. Nearly every Greek manuscript descends from the edition of
**[Theon of Alexandria](kloom:e/theon-of-alexandria)** in the fourth century AD, and Theon says in his
commentary on Ptolemy that he added a result to the end of Book VI himself.
In 1808, when Napoleon had manuscripts sent from Italian libraries to
Paris, the mathematician François Peyrard found that one of them, Vatican
manuscript 190, of the tenth century, lacked Theon's addition and his name.
It was older than Theon's text, and Heiberg's edition of the 1880s, which
Heath translated, rests on it. The historian Wilbur Knorr warned that "we
have never had a 'genuine' text of Euclid, and we never will have one."

The book reached Latin Europe from Arabic, in Adelard of Bath's translation
of about 1120. In 1482 **[Erhard Ratdolt](kloom:e/erhard-ratdolt)** printed it at Venice, the first
mathematical book of any importance to be printed. In his dedication, as
Heath reports it, Ratdolt says that little mathematics had been printed
because no one had managed to print the figures, and that he had found a
way to print them as easily as letters. Well over a thousand editions have
followed.

## Two thousand years of school

For most of that time the _Elements_ was geometry. The historian W. W. Rouse
Ball thought that its having been "the usual text-book on the subject" for
two thousand years raised "a strong presumption that it is not unsuitable
for that purpose." Its form traveled further than its figures. **[Isaac
Newton](kloom:e/isaac-newton)** set out his _Principia_ of 1687 in the same order: definitions,
then "axioms, or laws of motion", then propositions proved from them. Only
in the nineteenth century did schools give Euclid up for newer textbooks,
over the protests of Lewis Carroll, among others.

Archimedes, a generation later in Syracuse, could take Euclid's
propositions for granted, and used them to measure the circle.
