In 1637 the printer Jan Maire of Leiden brought out a book in French, not
Latin, with no author's name on its title page: a _Discourse on the Method
for rightly conducting one's reason_, followed by three essays "which are
trials of this method", on optics, on the weather, and on geometry. The
author was **[René Descartes](kloom:e/rene-descartes)**, a Frenchman who had lived in the Dutch
Republic since 1628. The **[_Discourse on the Method_](kloom:e/discourse-on-the-method)** is remembered for "I
think, therefore I am". The third essay, **[_La Géométrie_](kloom:e/la-geometrie)**, joined algebra and geometry so that every curve could be
written as an equation, and every equation drawn as a curve.

## Lengths that multiply

The book opens with a promise, here in our translation: "All the problems of geometry can easily be
reduced to such terms that one needs afterwards to know only the lengths of
certain straight lines in order to construct them." Arithmetic has four or
five operations, Descartes goes on, and geometry can do each of them to
lines, once one line is chosen as the unit.

The plate draws his first two figures, with numbers of our own invention.
To multiply BD by BC, take the unit AB along the same line as BD, join A to
C, and draw DE parallel to CA. The triangles BAC and BDE are similar, so BE
is to BC as BD is to the unit: BE = BD × BC. With BD = 2 and BC = 1½, BE
comes out 3. To take a square root of GH, set the unit FG beside it, draw a
half circle on FH, and raise a perpendicular at G to meet it at I. By
similar triangles again, GI × GI = FG × GH, so GI = √GH: with GH = 4, GI is 2.

The point is what the product is. To the Greeks, and still to François
Viète a generation before, the product of two lengths was an area, and of
three a solid; a fourth power stood for no figure at all, and many held
such equations unreal. For Descartes, _a_²
and _a_³ were lines like any other, and an equation could have any degree.

Then he names them. Known lines are _a_, _b_, _c_, unknown ones _z_, _y_,
_x_, and powers are written with a raised number, _a_³, as the page shows,
though for a square he usually kept _aa_. Most of what a school algebra
book looks like began here. His sign for equality did not last: later
editions put our = in its place.

## Pappus's problem

Descartes chose his test from **[Pappus of Alexandria](kloom:e/pappus-of-alexandria)**, who, in the fourth
century AD, described a problem that, Descartes wrote, Euclid had begun,
**[Apollonius](kloom:e/apollonius-of-perga)** had pursued, and nobody had finished. Given three or four straight lines, find every point from
which lines drawn to them at given angles satisfy one condition: for four
lines, that the product of two of the distances equals the product of the
other two. Pappus said the points lie on a conic section, but did not show
which, nor what curve served for five lines or more.

Descartes called one given line's segment AB _x_ and the line from B to the
point, BC, _y_. Every other distance then comes out as a sum of an _x_
term, a _y_ term and a known number, so the condition for four lines is an
equation of the second degree in _x_ and _y_, and its curve is a conic. With
more lines the degree rises and so does the curve, "and so on to infinity".
This is **[analytic geometry](kloom:e/analytic-geometry)**: a curve known by the equation
between two lengths that fix each of its points.

It was not yet the grid of every classroom. Descartes measured from one
line only, at whatever angle the problem gave, and left much to the reader
("it already wearies me to write so much about it"). The two perpendicular
axes came in the Latin editions of **[Frans van Schooten](kloom:e/frans-van-schooten)** of Leiden, from
1649, whose commentaries made the book readable, and it was van Schooten's
second edition that the young Isaac Newton studied.

## Fermat's own way

**[Pierre de Fermat](kloom:e/pierre-de-fermat)**, a lawyer in Toulouse, had the same idea
independently. His _Introduction to Plane and Solid Loci_ was circulating in
Paris in 1637, before the _Discourse_ appeared, and his eulogist in 1665
said so; it was printed only in 1679, after his death. Fermat began with an
equation and asked for its curve, Descartes with a curve and asked for its
equation. Historians credit both, though the grid is named for only one of
them: Cartesian.

The third book of _La Géométrie_ turned to equations themselves: that one
of degree _n_ has _n_ roots, a rule of signs for counting its positive ones,
and a name for the roots that are not real, "imaginary". With curves now
equations, the next question was how fast a curve rises at a point and how
much area lies beneath it, and within thirty years it had two answers: the
calculus.
