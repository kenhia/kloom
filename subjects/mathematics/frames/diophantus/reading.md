Of **[Diophantus](kloom:e/diophantus)** we know almost nothing but his book. He worked in
**[Alexandria](kloom:e/alexandria)**, and he could have lived at any time between about 170 BC,
since he quotes Hypsicles, and AD 350, when Theon of Alexandria quotes
him; he probably flourished in the third century AD. The book is the
_Arithmetica_, thirteen books of problems about numbers, dedicated to one
Dionysius. It is the oldest surviving work that solves
problems of arithmetic by what we would call algebra.

## A sign for the unknown

Diophantus did not invent algebra, though Lagrange called him its
inventor: it was practised and passed on by word of mouth before him, and
he took up its techniques. What is new in his book is the
notation. In the nineteenth century Georg Nesselmann sorted algebra into
three stages: _rhetorical_, written out in words; _syncopated_, with
abbreviations for the things that recur; and _symbolic_, ours. Diophantus
is the first known writer of the second kind.

He wrote the unknown, the _arithmos_, with a sign like a final sigma, ς.
Its square, the _dynamis_, was Δʸ, and its cube, the _kubos_, Kʸ. A unit
was M̊, and "equals" was ἴσ, short for _isos_. There was no sign for
plus: terms simply stood side by side, and every term to be subtracted
went after a single sign for minus, which the manuscripts draw as an
upturned, truncated Ψ. Our _x_³ − 5_x_² + 8_x_ − 1 comes out as
Kʸα ς η ⋔ Δʸε M̊α. He stated the rule of signs too, in words that Thomas
Heath rendered literally as "a wanting multiplied by a wanting makes a
forthcoming".

His answers had to be positive, and they could be fractions. An equation
whose answer came out negative he called absurd.

## Dividing a square

Problem 8 of Book II shows the method whole. It asks "to divide a given
square number into two squares", and Diophantus takes the square to be 16. Call the first square _x_². Then 16 − _x_² must also be a square.
Diophantus takes the square of "some multiple of *x* minus the root of
16", choosing 2_x_ − 4:

(2_x_ − 4)² = 4_x_² − 16_x_ + 16 = 16 − _x_², so 5_x_² = 16_x_, and _x_ = 16/5.

The two squares are 256/25 and 144/25, and they add up to 400/25, which is 16. Multiply the three sides by five and they are 16, 12 and 20: a right
triangle with whole sides, the kind of triple listed on the Babylonian tablet
**[Plimpton 322](kloom:e/plimpton-322)**.

The plate shows what the recipe is doing, though Diophantus never drew
it. The squares that add to 16 are the points of the circle _x_² + _y_² = 16,
and his choice is a straight line from the point (0, −4) with a slope of 2. Any line from that point with a rational slope meets the circle again
at a rational point, so the same recipe gives endless answers. The slopes
other than 2 in this table are our own choices:

| Slope of the line | First square | Second square |
| ----------------: | -----------: | ------------: |
|  2 (Diophantus's) |      (16/5)² |       (12/5)² |
|                 3 |      (12/5)² |       (16/5)² |
|                 4 |     (32/17)² |      (60/17)² |
|               3/2 |     (48/13)² |      (20/13)² |

Diophantus hints that he knew this. The historian Norbert Schappacher
points out that the phrase "some multiple of _x_" is unusually general for
him, and in problem III.19 he says that we have learned to divide a
square into two squares "in an infinite number of ways".

## Ten books of thirteen

For centuries only six books were known, in Byzantine Greek copies. The
Byzantine encyclopedia called the _Suda_ says that **[Hypatia](kloom:e/hypatia)** of
Alexandria wrote a commentary on the _Arithmetica_. It is lost under
that name, but the historian Alan Cameron argues that it survives in the
checks and extra problems added to the Arabic text below, while Wilbur
Knorr thought those additions too trivial to be hers. In the ninth century **[Qusta ibn Luqa](kloom:e/qusta-ibn-luqa)** translated
the work into Arabic in Baghdad. In 1463 Regiomontanus lamented that no
one had put Diophantus into Latin. Wilhelm Xylander did, in 1575, and in
1621 **[Claude Gaspar Bachet de Méziriac](kloom:e/claude-gaspar-bachet-de-meziriac)** published the first edition
with the Greek text, beside his Latin.

In 1910 Heath wrote that there was no sign the Arabs had ever possessed
the missing books. Then four of them turned up in a manuscript copied in
1198 and kept in the shrine library at Mashhad, in Iran. The historian of
science Fuat Sezgin found it in 1968, by Jan Hogendijk's account;
Schappacher dates its resurfacing to around 1971, and says it had been
catalogued under the translator's name. Jacques Sesiano and Roshdi Rashed
edited it, and showed that these are Books IV to VII, so that the last
three Greek books must come later in the work.

## A riddle and a margin

The only story of his life is a riddle in the **[Greek Anthology](kloom:e/greek-anthology)**:
his boyhood was a sixth of his life, his youth a twelfth more, and a
seventh more passed before he married; a son came five years later,
lived half his father's span, and Diophantus died four years after him.
So _x_/6 + _x_/12 + _x_/7 + 5 + _x_/2 + 4 = _x_, and _x_ = 84. Heath
thought a friend wrote it soon after his death. Wikipedia attributes the
puzzle to Metrodorus, a grammarian of the fifth or sixth century, and
says its facts cannot be confirmed.

Bachet's edition had one famous reader. **[Pierre de Fermat](kloom:e/pierre-de-fermat)**, a lawyer in
Toulouse, worked through a copy and wrote in its margins, and beside
problem II.8 he wrote the note that opens the trail of Fermat's Last
Theorem.
