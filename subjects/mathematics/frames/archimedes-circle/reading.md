**[Archimedes](kloom:e/archimedes)** lived in **[Syracuse](kloom:e/syracuse-sicily)**, on Sicily, from about 287 BC until a
Roman soldier killed him in the sack of the city in 212. His lever, his
floating bodies and the prayer book that hid his writings are physics'
story. This one is the circle: how long its edge is, how much it holds,
and how the answer could be proved and not merely measured.

Every earlier culture had a working value. The Rhind papyrus used about
3.16, and a Babylonian tablet in effect 3⅛. Archimedes wanted something different: two
numbers that the ratio of the circumference to the diameter, which we call
**[π](kloom:e/pi)**, provably lies between.

## Measurement of a Circle

The short treatise called **[_Measurement of a Circle_](kloom:e/measurement-of-a-circle)** survives in three
propositions, probably a fragment of something longer, and the second of
them cannot be in its original place, since it uses the third. The first
proves that a circle's area equals a right triangle whose two shorter sides
are its radius and its circumference, which is our _A_ = ½ × _r_ × 2π_r_ =
π_r_².

![The opening page of Measurement of a Circle in the first printed edition of Archimedes, Basel, 1544: the Latin title Archimedis circuli dimensio, a decorated initial, a column of dense type, and figures of a circle with inscribed and circumscribed polygons and a long thin triangle beside the text](basel-1544.jpg)

It is proved by the **[method of exhaustion](kloom:e/method-of-exhaustion)**, which Archimedes had from
Eudoxus. Suppose the circle is larger than the triangle. Inscribe a square,
then an 8-gon, a 16-gon, halving the arcs each time, until the slivers
left between polygon and circle add up to less than the excess. That
polygon is now larger than the triangle, yet its perimeter is shorter than
the circumference and its distance from the center to each side shorter
than the radius, so it must be smaller. The circle is not larger. A
matching argument with polygons drawn outside shows it is not smaller
either. Nothing infinite is ever used; each step is a finite polygon.

## Ninety-six sides

The third proposition bounds π. Archimedes starts with a regular hexagon
outside the circle and another inside, whose perimeters are easy to
compute, and doubles the number of sides four times, to 12, 24, 48 and 96.
Each doubling needs a square root, done by hand in fractions and always
rounded in the direction that keeps the bound safe. He needs √3 at the
start and takes it as lying between 265/153 and 1351/780, bounds he does
not explain; historians still argue how he found them. The plate draws the
circle between the first three pairs of polygons.

The table gives the perimeters in diameters, by our arithmetic, to five
places:

| Sides | Polygon inside | Polygon outside |
| ----: | -------------: | --------------: |
|     6 |        3.00000 |         3.46410 |
|    12 |        3.10583 |         3.21539 |
|    24 |        3.13263 |         3.15966 |
|    48 |        3.13935 |         3.14609 |
|    96 |        3.14103 |         3.14271 |

His rounded fractions give 3 10/71 < π < 3 1/7, that is, between 3.1408 and
3.1429. The true value, 3.14159…, lies a little nearer the lower bound, and
the upper one, 22/7, is still the value many people take π to be. The
method could be pushed as far as patience allowed, and for well over a
thousand years it was how π was computed: in 1630 the astronomer
Christoph Grienberger reached 38 digits with a polygon of 10⁴⁰ sides.

## A sphere on his tomb

Archimedes thought more of another result. In _On the Sphere and
Cylinder_ he proved that a sphere is two-thirds of the cylinder that just
holds it, in volume and in surface too. Properties like these, he wrote to
his friend Dositheus in Heath's translation, "were all along naturally
inherent in the figures", but unknown to every geometer before him. He
asked, Plutarch says, for the sphere and cylinder to be carved on his
grave.

More than a century later **[Cicero](kloom:e/cicero)**, serving as quaestor in Sicily,
went looking for it. The Syracusans, he says, denied that it survived, but
among the tombs by one of the city gates he saw "a small column standing
out a little above the briers, with the figure of a sphere and a cylinder
upon it", had the brambles cut away, and read what was left of the
verses.

## Sand to fill the universe

In _The Sand Reckoner_, addressed to King Gelon of Syracuse, Archimedes
answered people who thought the grains of sand were too many to number.
Greek counting words stopped at a myriad, ten thousand. He built on the
myriad of myriads, 10⁸, as the unit of a second "order", and so on, far
past anything he needed, and then filled with sand a universe far larger
than the usual one, the one proposed by **[Aristarchus of Samos](kloom:e/aristarchus-of-samos)**, in which
the Earth goes round the Sun. The count came to fewer than 10⁶³ grains.
The book is one of the few witnesses that Aristarchus proposed a
Sun-centered world at all.

The circle had been measured. Apollonius of Perga, a generation younger,
turned to the curves a plane cuts from a cone.
