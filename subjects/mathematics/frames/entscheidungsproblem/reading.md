In 1928 **[David Hilbert](kloom:e/david-hilbert)** and his assistant Wilhelm Ackermann published a textbook of logic, _Grundzüge der theoretischen Logik_, and in it they set a problem: the **[Entscheidungsproblem](kloom:e/entscheidungsproblem)**, the decision problem. It would be solved, they wrote, "once we know a procedure that allows us to decide, by means of finitely many operations, whether a given logical expression is universally valid". They called it the main problem of mathematical logic. It asked for a mechanical test that would take any statement of logic and say, yes or no, whether it holds.

Solve it, and a great deal of mathematics would follow by calculation. Hilbert's assistant Heinrich Behmann had called such a method a philosophers' stone. Others hoped it would never be found. **John von Neumann** wrote in 1927 that there seemed to be no general criterion, and admitted that "no clue whatsoever exists how such an undecidability proof would have to be conducted". In Cambridge in 1928 G. H. Hardy told an audience that if such rules existed, "our activities as mathematicians would come to an end".

## What is a procedure?

Gödel's theorems of 1931 showed that no system proves every truth, but they left the decision problem open: there might still be a mechanical way to tell what a given system can prove. To show that no procedure exists, someone had first to say exactly what a procedure is. In 1936 three logicians did it, each his own way.

| Who           | Where     | A procedure is…                                              | Published                      |
| ------------- | --------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------ |
| Alonzo Church | Princeton | a function definable in the λ-calculus, or general recursive | 1936, a paper and a short note |
| Alan Turing   | Cambridge | a machine reading and writing symbols on a tape              | received 28 May 1936           |
| Emil Post     | New York  | a worker marking and unmarking boxes by a list of directions | received October 1936          |

**[Alonzo Church](kloom:e/alonzo-church)**, with his student Stephen Kleene, had built the **[lambda calculus](kloom:e/lambda-calculus)**, a notation for functions, and proposed that "effectively calculable" should mean definable in it. Gödel, when Church put the idea to him, called it "thoroughly unsatisfactory". Church went ahead. In a paper printed in April 1936 he showed a problem about his own notation to be unsolvable, and in a note of two pages he drew the conclusion: "The general case of the Entscheidungsproblem of the engere Funktionenkalkül is unsolvable." The engere Funktionenkalkül was Hilbert's first-order logic.

**[Alan Turing](kloom:e/alan-turing)**, in Cambridge, reached the same answer without knowing of Church's, by imagining a clerk reduced to a machine; the ai subject's frame on the Turing machine tells his paper in full. He added an appendix proving that his machines and Church's functions compute the same things, and Church, reviewing it, wrote that Turing's definition made the identification with effectiveness "evident immediately". **[Emil Post](kloom:e/emil-leon-post)**, a schoolteacher turned lecturer at the City College of New York, published a third model almost the same as Turing's, written independently: a worker moving along a row of boxes, marking, erasing and checking marks according to numbered directions. Post objected to calling any of these a definition. Better, he wrote, to treat the match as a "working hypothesis" to be tested, perhaps one day a natural law. Kleene later named it Church's thesis; today it is the **[Church–Turing thesis](kloom:e/church-turing-thesis)**.

## The diagonal

Here is the argument in its modern form, the **[halting problem](kloom:e/halting-problem)**; the name came later, from Martin Davis, and Turing's own version asked whether a machine ever prints a given symbol. Suppose some program _H_, given any program _P_ and an input, always says correctly whether _P_ halts on that input or runs forever. List every program, _P_₁, _P_₂, _P_₃, …, and set out a table: the row for each program, a column for each input number, and in each cell whether it halts. The plate draws a corner of such a table, with entries invented. Now build a program _D_ that uses _H_ to read the diagonal and does the opposite: on input _n_, if _P_ₙ halts on _n_, _D_ runs forever; if _P_ₙ runs forever, _D_ halts. _D_ differs from every program in the list at its own diagonal cell, so it is not in the list. But it is a program, built from _H_, so it must be. The contradiction means _H_ cannot exist. Turing then wrote, for each machine, a formula of logic that is provable exactly when the machine eventually prints a 0. A decision procedure for logic would therefore settle the undecidable, so there is none.

## Whose idea

How much Church and Turing owed Gödel is argued. Martin Davis noticed in 1965 that Gödel's theorem of 1931 already implied the result, and some writers now call Turing's paper a restatement of Gödel's. Kleene, who worked beside Church, thought otherwise: Church had used Gödel's numbering, but Turing "had his own train of thought, quite unalloyed by any input from Gödel". Hilbert and Ackermann removed their hopeful sentence from the book's second edition in 1938. Hilbert's tenth problem fell the same way in 1970, when no procedure for equations in whole numbers turned out to be possible. In 1985 the physicist David Deutsch argued that the Church–Turing thesis hides a claim about physics, and restated it as a principle: every finitely realizable physical system can be perfectly simulated by a universal computing machine.

Machines could not decide mathematics. Within forty years, one would help to prove a theorem that no one had proved by hand.
