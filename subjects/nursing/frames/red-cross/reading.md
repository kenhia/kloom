On the evening of 24 June 1859 a Genevan businessman named **[Henry Dunant](kloom:e/henry-dunant)**, traveling in Lombardy to petition Napoleon III about a venture in Algeria, reached the edge of the field where the French and Sardinian armies had just beaten the Austrians at the **[Battle of Solferino](kloom:e/battle-of-solferino)**. Some 40,000 men were dead or wounded. Over the next days he worked among them at Castiglione delle Stiviere, a town a few miles off, where the wounded lay in every church, barracks and courtyard. In the church he names as the Chiesa Maggiore, nearly five hundred soldiers were packed inside and at least a hundred more lay on straw outside, under cloths stretched against the sun. Women of the town went from man to man with jars of water "to quench thirst and moisten wounds", in our translation, and boys ran buckets from the fountains. When the women saw that Dunant made no distinction of nation, they followed him, and tended Austrians as they tended French. "Tutti fratelli," they said: all brothers.

## A book and a committee

He told it in **[_A Memory of Solferino_](kloom:e/a-memory-of-solferino)** (_Un souvenir de Solférino_), printed at his own expense in Geneva in 1862, 1,600 copies, which he sent to statesmen and generals across Europe. After the battle and the wards it asks a question, which we translate:

> Would there not be some means, in a time of peace and quiet, of forming relief societies whose aim would be to have the wounded cared for in wartime by zealous, devoted volunteers, well qualified for such a work?

And it asks for "some international principle, conventional and sacred", agreed in advance by the military powers, on which those societies could rest. Two proposals, then: trained volunteers organized before a war, and a treaty to let them work. Dunant's book praises **[Florence Nightingale](kloom:e/florence-nightingale)**'s nurses in the Crimea, and in a paper read in London in 1872 he said that her work there was what had moved him to go to Italy in 1859. Her biographer Cook, reporting that paper, calls him a Swiss physician; he was a man of business, and it was his business that later failed.

In February 1863 a committee of five in Geneva took up the book: Dunant, the lawyer Gustave Moynier, the surgeon Louis Appia, the physician Théodore Maunoir and General Guillaume-Henri Dufour. It called an international conference that October, and the Swiss government a diplomatic one the next August. The committee took its present name, the **[International Committee of the Red Cross](kloom:e/international-committee-of-the-red-cross)**, in 1876.

## Ten articles

On 22 August 1864 twelve states signed the **[First Geneva Convention](kloom:e/first-geneva-convention)**, "for the Amelioration of the Condition of the Wounded in Armies in the Field": Switzerland, Baden, Belgium, Denmark, Spain, France, Hesse, Italy, the Netherlands, Portugal, Prussia and Württemberg. It has ten articles. The ones that touched the people who nursed, in the translation the United States later printed:

| Article | What it says                                                                                                                                                                                                     |
| ------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1       | Ambulances and military hospitals "shall be acknowledged to be neuter", protected and respected "so long as any sick or wounded may be therein"                                                                  |
| 2       | "Persons employed in hospitals and ambulances, comprising the staff for superintendence, medical service, administration, transport of wounded, as well as chaplains" share that neutrality "whilst so employed" |
| 3       | They may stay at their work after the enemy occupies the place, or leave to rejoin their own side                                                                                                                |
| 5       | Inhabitants "who may bring help to the wounded shall be respected, and shall remain free"; a house that takes in wounded men is spared the quartering of troops                                                  |
| 6       | "Wounded or sick soldiers shall be entertained and taken care of, to whatever nation they may belong"                                                                                                            |
| 7       | A flag for hospitals and ambulances, and an arm-badge for neutral persons: "a red cross on a white ground"                                                                                                       |

The word nurse is not in it. A nurse in a field hospital was protected as one of the staff "whilst so employed", and the women of Castiglione were exactly the helpers Article 5 now covered, inhabitants who brought help to the wounded. What the convention gave them was a rule the armies had promised in advance: the wounded of both sides are patients, and those who tend them are not targets.

## The emblem

A rule on a battlefield is only as good as what a soldier can see from a distance. Article 7 made the sign: the flag over the hospital, beside the national flag, and the arm-badge on the person, which "the military authority" was to issue, so that no one could simply claim it. The red cross was first worn in war that same year, when Appia and a Dutch officer, Charles van de Velde, went as neutral delegates to the war between Denmark and Prussia. Where the design came from is less certain. The ICRC says it reverses the Swiss flag, as the convention's revision of 1906 recorded; the Red Cross historian Pierre Boissier found no clear evidence of that origin.

## Clara Barton

The United States did not sign in 1864. **[Clara Barton](kloom:e/clara-barton)**, who had nursed and supplied the Union wounded in the Civil War, met Appia in Geneva in 1869 and read Dunant's book there, then worked beside the Grand Duchess of Baden in the Franco-Prussian War. From 1873 she pressed Washington to join. President Hayes declined; under President Arthur she won, partly by arguing that an American society could serve in floods, fires and epidemics as well as in war. Her **[American Red Cross](kloom:e/american-red-cross)** held its first meeting at her Washington apartment on 21 May 1881. Arthur signed the accession on 1 March 1882, the Senate consented on 16 March, and the convention bound the United States from 9 June 1882. Barton led the society until 1904. Sixty years after its founding it would open its first blood donor center, as the frame on Blood for Britain and dried plasma tells. On the main spine, the next frame turns from the battlefield to the bedside thermometer.
