On Monday 16 July 1860 the Prince Consort opened the fourth session of the International Statistical Congress in the large hall of King's College, London, before delegates from twenty-four countries. Nearly six hundred members came to some part of it. The papers for its section on sanitary statistics were written by **[Florence Nightingale](kloom:e/florence-nightingale)**, [John Sutherland](kloom:e/john-sutherland-physician) and **[William Farr](kloom:e/william-farr)**, and hers was a proposal for a uniform plan of hospital statistics. The Congress adopted it "with a few additions", and the report of its secretary, James Hammack, thanks her among those who entertained the delegates.

## Why hospitals could not be compared

Every hospital kept its register in its own way, naming diseases as its physicians chose. Even a death rate meant different things: some hospitals reckoned it on the number of sick on an average day and some on the cases treated in the year, as [John Simon](kloom:e/john-simon-pathologist) complained in 1864. Hospital mortality statistics, Nightingale wrote, had "given little information on the efficiency of the hospital", because they took no account of what kinds of cases it admitted, how many recovered or how long they stayed. A high death rate could mean bad wards or grave cases, and nobody could tell which. Her answer was to make every hospital fill in the same form, sorting every patient by the same list of diseases. The list was the one the Congress had agreed in Paris in 1855, "essentially the same as that used by the Registrars-General of the United Kingdom": Farr's. Its first class was the _zymotic_ diseases, smallpox, measles, cholera, typhus and the fevers, with hospital gangrene and pyaemia among them; then the constitutional, then the local diseases by the organ they struck.

## The form

She printed the form in the third edition of _Notes on Hospitals_ in 1863. It is one large ruled sheet:

![The top of a large printed form headed "Hospital General Statistical Form", with columns for ages from 0 months to "95 and upwards" and a total across the top; down the left, "Class I.—Order I. (Zymotic Diseases.)" listing small pox, measles, whooping cough and other diseases, each with an empty ruled row; and in the right margin, printed instructions for counting the rarer diseases](statistical-form.jpg)

| Part of the sheet     | What it holds                                                                                           |
| --------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Across the top        | Ages: 0, 3 and 6 months; 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5 years; then every five years to 90, "95 and upwards"; a total |
| Down the left         | The common diseases by class and order, each with a line "Others"                                       |
| Down the right margin | The rarer diseases, to be counted on the "Others" line of their order with the age written in           |
| The heading           | Written in by pen: the hospital, the sex, the date, and which of the seven elements the sheet counts    |

Each patient was a stroke in the cell for his disease and age. The same sheet was filled in seven times a year, once for each element:

| Element | What it counts                                                                |
| ------: | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
|       1 | Remaining in hospital on the first day of the year                            |
|       2 | Admitted during the year                                                      |
|       3 | Recovered or relieved                                                         |
|       4 | Discharged incurable, unrelieved, for irregularities, or at their own request |
|       5 | Died                                                                          |
|       6 | Remaining on the last day of the year                                         |
|       7 | Mean duration of cases, in days and fractions of a day                        |

By our reckoning the first six must balance, since every patient who came in or was there already either left, died or stayed. From them she drew the rates. The beds constantly occupied were the mean of the numbers remaining at the start and end of the year, or the total days in hospital divided by 365, or, she noted, the year's diets issued divided by 365. The "sick treated" were the mean of the admissions and of the discharges from every cause, deaths included. An invented hospital shows the sums:

| Element                                     | Patients |
| ------------------------------------------- | -------: |
| Remaining, 1 January                        |       96 |
| Admitted                                    |    1,180 |
| Recovered or relieved                       |    1,010 |
| Discharged otherwise                        |       60 |
| Died                                        |      104 |
| Remaining, 31 December (96 + 1,180 − 1,174) |      102 |

It kept 99 beds filled, the mean of 96 and 102, and treated 1,177 cases, the mean of 1,180 admitted and 1,174 gone. Its deaths are 8.8 per 100 cases treated, but 105 per 100 beds occupied, and its patients stayed about 31 days each (99 × 365 ÷ 1,177). Counting deaths against beds, as the table in the frame on _Notes on Hospitals_ does, makes a busy hospital look deadly; counting them against cases, as the form allowed, does not. The Congress added twelve recommendations of its own: to record the date of attack as well as of admission, the diseases a patient caught in the hospital, the place the disease began, the empty beds, the cost of each patient, and the size of every ward.

## What came of it

St Thomas's, University College and St Mary's hospitals filled in trial sheets for her, and by 1863, she wrote, the forms were in use at **[St Bartholomew's Hospital](kloom:e/st-bartholomews-hospital)** and the London Hospital, and "a greater uniformity" had come to the records of several others. We have not found how long they kept them. The statisticians Jan Beyersmann and Christine Schrade, reading her forms as a modern analyst would, found them "conceptually more complete than many reported competing risks analyses today": she counted every way a stay could end, not deaths alone.

The classification outlived the forms. The **[International Statistical Institute](kloom:e/international-statistical-institute)**, which succeeded the Congress, adopted the classification of causes of death prepared by **[Jacques Bertillon](kloom:e/jacques-bertillon)** of Paris at Chicago in 1893, and it was built, the World Health Organization's history says, on Farr's principle of separating general diseases from those of one organ or site. Revised every ten years or so from 1900, it became the **[International Classification of Diseases](kloom:e/international-classification-of-diseases)**. Lists for sickness were added beside it from 1900 but little used. What Nightingale had asked for, one list for the sick in hospital as well as for the dead, came with the sixth revision in 1948, the first the World Health Organization adopted.
