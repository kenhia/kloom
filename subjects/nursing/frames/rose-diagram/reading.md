Late in 1858 **[Florence Nightingale](kloom:e/florence-nightingale)** had a colored plate printed into _Notes on Matters Affecting the Health, Efficiency, and Hospital Administration of the British Army_, her confidential report to the government on the **[Crimean War](kloom:e/crimean-war)**. Its title was "Diagram of the causes of mortality in the army in the East". It shows two wheels of twelve wedges, one wedge for each month. The wheel on the right is the first year of the war, April 1854 to March 1855, reading clockwise from the left; the wheel on the left is the second year, joined to the first by a dotted line. Blue wedges are deaths from what the diagram calls "Preventible or Mitigable Zymotic diseases", red from wounds, and black from all other causes. She printed it again in 1859, in _A Contribution to the Sanitary History of the British Army_, and gave it to Harriet Martineau for her book _England and Her Soldiers_.

## The numbers under it

The diagram draws a table in the Royal Commission's appendix, reprinted in her _Mortality of the British Army_ (1858). For every month it gives the army's average strength in the East, its deaths in hospital under the three heads, and each as an "annual rate of mortality per 1,000": the deaths there would be in a year among 1,000 men if that month's rate went on. The rate is the month's deaths × 12 × 1,000 ÷ the strength. For January 1855, the worst month:

| January 1855     | Deaths | Rate per 1,000 a year |
| ---------------- | -----: | --------------------: |
| Zymotic diseases |  2,761 |               1,022.8 |
| Wounds, injuries |     83 |                  30.7 |
| All other causes |    324 |                 120.0 |
| Strength: 32,393 |  3,168 |               1,173.5 |

Each line checks: 2,761 × 12,000 ÷ 32,393 is 1,022.8. The total, 1,174 in her rounding, was a rate at which the whole army would have been dead within a year, and she pointed out that it was higher than London's in the worst month of the Great Plague, September 1665. By our arithmetic from the same table, the first year's hospital deaths were 11,157 from zymotic disease (cholera, dysentery, fevers), 772 from wounds and 1,365 from other causes; the second year's were 3,319, 986 and 383.

## Area, not radius

The diagram's legend begins: "The Areas of the blue, red, & black wedges are each measured from the centre as the common vertex." A wedge's area, not its length, stands for the rate. Each wedge has a twelfth of the circle, 30°, so its area is π_r_²/12, and a wedge of area equal to the rate has radius √(12 × rate ÷ π). The worked radii, in the same units for every month:

| Month         | Zymotic rate | Radius |
| ------------- | -----------: | -----: |
| July 1854     |        150.0 |   23.9 |
| November 1854 |        340.6 |   36.1 |
| January 1855  |      1,022.8 |   62.5 |
| June 1855     |        247.6 |   30.8 |
| January 1856  |         11.4 |    6.6 |

January 1856's rate is a ninetieth of January 1855's, but its wedge is about a tenth as long: the square root tames the extremes. The plate draws the first year's blue wedges on this construction, with the rings of equal rate crowding together as they go out.

![A large folding plate headed "Diagrams representing the relative mortality from zymotic diseases (blue), from wounds &c (red), and from all other causes (black)": two circles with month names around their rims, and an enormous pale-blue polygon spilling far outside the right-hand circle](batwing.jpg)

She had not started this way. The booklet _Mortality of the British Army_, printed early in 1858, drew the same table as "Diagram K", a shape Hugh Small calls the "bat's wing", in which the length of each month's radius was proportional to the rate, so January 1855's spike ran off the circle. The eye reads such a shape by its area, which grows as the square of the rate, and Small found that she inserted an erratum slip and replaced it with the wedges in the later documents.

## Who made it

**[William Farr](kloom:e/william-farr)**, the statistician of the General Register Office, helped her with the figures, and the booklet's tables of causes follow "the Classification of Causes of Death proposed by Dr. Farr", in which _zymotic_, from the Greek for fermenting, was his name for the epidemic diseases. Small finds most of the booklet's diagrams like those Farr had drawn in the Registrar-General's reports, and the bat's wing and the wedges so unlike them that they may be her own. The polar-area form was not hers. Michael Friendly, a historian of statistical graphics, traces it to **[André-Michel Guerry](kloom:e/andre-michel-guerry)**, whose _courbes circulaires_ of 1829 showed winds, births and deaths through the day and the year, and to Léon Lalanne's wind rose of the 1840s. **[William Playfair](kloom:e/william-playfair)** had printed the first pie charts in 1801, and the wedges are often called a variant: a **[pie chart](kloom:e/pie-chart)** whose slices have equal angles and unequal radii. Her biographer Edward Cook wrote that few books impressed her more than those of **[Adolphe Quetelet](kloom:e/adolphe-quetelet)**, the Belgian who applied statistics to society. And she never called the diagram a "coxcomb". Small found that her "coxcombs" were the 2,000 copies of the booklet itself, the showy crest on a report whose appendix, she told Sidney Herbert, "None but scientific men ever look into".

## What it was for

The wedges argue. The blue dwarfs the red: soldiers died of disease, not of the enemy. And the second wheel is small: after the Sanitary Commission reached the hospitals in March 1855, the rates fell. Small reads the diagram as aimed at more than the war, at John Simon, England's chief medical officer, who had written in 1858 that deaths from epidemic disease were "practically speaking, unavoidable". Friendly calls her arguably the first to use statistical graphics for political persuasion. Whether the sanitary works made the fall, or the end of trench duty, as some army doctors said, was disputed then, and Small in 1998 left the question open. The trail _Nightingale's numbers_ follows her statistics from here.
