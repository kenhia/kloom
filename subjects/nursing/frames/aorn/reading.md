By the middle of the twentieth century the operating room was a department with its own nurses, and in 1949 those nurses founded an association of their own. The **[Association of Operating Room Nurses](kloom:e/association-of-perioperative-registered-nurses)**, AORN, says on its website that it was founded that year "to establish a national community for operating room nurses who sought to share best practices for patients undergoing surgery". A recent biography in the Army's _Medical Journal_ names its founder as **Edith Dee Hall** (1897–1971), who trained at the **[Army School of Nursing](kloom:e/army-school-of-nursing)** during the **[First World War](kloom:e/world-war-i)** and gave the association its aim, "Better and Safer Care for the Patient in the Operating Room". Where the first meeting was held and who else was there, the sources we could reach do not say: the association's own early histories, in the _AORN Journal_, are not open to read. Local groups of operating-room nurses had formed in several American cities since 1916. The national association held its first congress in New York on February 1–3, 1954, began the _AORN Journal_ in 1963, and in 1965 published a booklet of _Standards for Administrative and Clinical Practice in the Operating Room_, the ancestor of the guidelines it publishes today.

## Two nurses at the table

What the association organized around was a division of the work that had been settling for half a century. In 1916 Amy Armour Smith's primer for pupil nurses already had an "instrument nurse", scrubbed and gowned at the table, a "clean nurse" at the supply table, and an unscrubbed "floater" who fetched what was wanted. Later texts call them the scrub nurse and the circulating nurse. The US Department of Labor's description of the job in 1963, written for the operating-room technicians who by then also filled both places, sets out who did what:

| Part of the case | The scrub                                                                                                                                                                               | The circulator                                                                                          |
| ---------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Dress            | Scrub suit, mask and cap; a complete scrub; sterile gown and sterile gloves                                                                                                             | Scrub dress; not sterile, and so never touches anything that is                                         |
| Before           | Lifts the instrument tray from the autoclave with a towel; lays the instruments on the Mayo stand; fits the knife blades; sets out the pack                                             | Puts the pack on the instrument table; fetches supplies; brings, identifies and positions the patient   |
| The count        | Counts the sponges with the surgeon and reports the number                                                                                                                              | Records it, to compare with the count after the operation                                               |
| During           | Stands by the instrument table; anticipates the surgeon; puts each instrument in his hand as he will use it; watches for breaks in sterile technique and changes gloves at any puncture | Fetches more equipment and supplies for anyone on the team; helps protect the sterile area              |
| After            | Either: has the final dressing ready; shakes out and looks over every soiled drape, so that no instrument goes to the laundry                                                           | Either: helps with the adhesive of the dressing; identifies the patient again before the patient leaves |

The division follows the sterile field. Everything inside it, the gowned team, the draped patient, the instrument table and the Mayo stand, has come from the sterilizer and may touch only what is sterile; the circulator's job is to keep it supplied without breaching it, and to be the room's eyes and hands outside it. The plate draws the room that way.

## Why the nurses organized

The war changed who stood at the table. The Army and Navy had trained medics and corpsmen as operating-room technicians, and Wikipedia's history of the **[surgical technologist](kloom:e/surgical-technologist)** says that after the **[Korean War](kloom:e/korean-war)**, short of operating-room nurses, hospitals hired them: the former medics circulated while the registered nurse scrubbed, an arrangement that, by its account, was reversed from 1965. With more people in the room who were not nurses, AORN's written standards of 1965 said what the nurse's part was. It published _Teaching the Operating Room Technician_ in 1967, and in 1968 set up an association for technicians, which became independent in 1973 as the Association of Surgical Technologists.

## Perioperative

The name changed with the idea of the work. **[Perioperative nursing](kloom:e/perioperative-nursing)** is care around the operation: the nurse's care of the patient before, during and after it, not only at the table. Wikipedia lists the roles under it now as the circulating nurse, the scrub or instrument nurse, the nurse who receives the patient before surgery, the recovery-room nurse and the registered nurse first assistant, who assists the surgeon. The association kept its initials and became the Association of periOperative Registered Nurses, a change it explained to members in the _AORN Journal_ of December 1998. As of October 2026 it publishes _Guidelines for Perioperative Practice_, which it says are used in every American hospital and surgical center.
