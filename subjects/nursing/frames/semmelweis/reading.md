The maternity hospital of the **[Vienna General Hospital](kloom:e/vienna-general-hospital)** had two clinics under one roof, and they were run as if someone had designed a trial. The First Clinic taught medical students and the Second taught only **[midwives](kloom:e/midwife)**. A woman in labor went to whichever clinic was admitting when she arrived: admission passed from one to the other every twenty-four hours at four in the afternoon, and once a week the First Clinic took forty-eight, so it had four admitting days a week to the Second's three. The plate draws the week as a ring. The women came from the same city, to the same building, by the day of the week. They did not die at the same rate. Over six years, by the counts **[Ignaz Semmelweis](kloom:e/ignaz-semmelweis)** printed in 1861, the doctors' clinic lost about one mother in ten to **[puerperal fever](kloom:e/postpartum-infections)**, childbed fever, and the midwives' clinic about one in thirty:

The real gap was wider, he added, because when deaths mounted the First Clinic sent its sick women into the general hospital, where they died on its books instead. Women knew the numbers. Some begged on their knees not to be taken into the First Clinic.

## Our fingers

Semmelweis, a Hungarian of twenty-eight, became assistant in the First Clinic in July 1846. The usual explanations were epidemic influences in the air, overcrowding, the priest carrying the last rites through the wards, and the foreign students, said to examine more roughly than the midwives. On that last theory the students were cut from forty-two to twenty, foreigners were almost shut out, and examinations kept to a minimum. Deaths fell from December 1846 to March 1847, and then, under the same rules, 57 women died in April and 36 in May.

His explanation, in his own book, does not spare himself. On 20 March 1847 he took up the post of assistant again after a few months' wait. Each morning before rounds he went to the dead-house and examined the bodies of women for his own gynecological studies, and then went to the labor ward to examine every woman in labor, as the assistant had to. That winter, as a stand-in, he had examined only the unusual cases, and the regular assistant had seldom gone to the dead-house. The difference, he wrote, in our translation, was "my finger contaminated with cadaver particles". Then his friend **[Jakob Kolletschka](kloom:e/jakob-kolletschka)**, professor of forensic medicine, was nicked by a student's knife during a dissection and died on 13 March of inflammation of the veins, the lymph vessels, the pleura, the pericardium, the peritoneum and the meninges. The women died of the same list. Semmelweis concluded that particles of corpses, carried on the hands of the people who examined them, were doing to the mothers what the knife had done to Kolletschka.

## The basin at the door

In the second half of May 1847 (he could not remember the day) he made every student wash in chlorine solution before examining a woman, first with chlorine water, _chlorina liquida_, then, because that was dear, with the much cheaper **[chlorinated lime](kloom:e/calcium-hypochlorite)**. The notice that **[Ferdinand von Hebra](kloom:e/ferdinand-ritter-von-hebra)**, editor of the journal of Vienna's medical society, published in December 1847 gives the rule and the recipe: in the labor room, everyone who wished to examine a pregnant woman must first wash his hands in an aqueous solution of one ounce of chlorinated lime to two pounds of spring water. By our arithmetic that is one part in twenty-four by weight, about 4 per cent.

The failures taught him as much as the successes. In September, Hebra reported, several women died after students who had been dissecting neglected the washing. In October a woman with a discharging cancer of the womb was examined again and again, without washing between, and others fell ill after her, as they did beside a woman with a foul ulcer on her leg. So the danger came from living patients too, and the hands had to be washed between examinations, not only after the dead-house. In 1848, with washing all year, the First Clinic lost 45 women of 3,556, 1.27 per 100, and the midwives' clinic 43 of 3,219, 1.33. In March and August not one mother died in the First Clinic.

## Why he was not believed

The midwives had been the control all along. They examined women by hand as the students did; they did not dissect. Hebra's notice drew the moral, in our translation: this might explain why schools for midwives showed such favorable mortality compared with the schools for obstetricians, except for the Maternité in Paris, where, as everyone knew, the dissections were done by midwives.

Semmelweis had no mechanism to offer, only a poison he could not see, and he was slow to publish: others reported his work for him, often wrongly, until his essays of 1858 and his book of 1861. His successor in the First Clinic, Carl Braun, later listed thirty causes of childbed fever, cadaveric infection twenty-eighth. Semmelweis left Vienna, cut deaths again in Pest, and wrote open letters calling his opponents murderers. On 30 July 1865 he was taken to an asylum in Vienna, by Hebra among others, was beaten there by guards, and died on 13 August of an infected wound of the hand.

The explanation came from Louis Pasteur's germs, and the next frame follows it into surgery, in Glasgow.
