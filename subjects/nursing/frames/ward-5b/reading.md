In 1983 nobody knew what caused **[AIDS](kloom:e/hiv-aids)** or how it spread, and at **[San Francisco General Hospital](kloom:e/san-francisco-general-hospital)**, the city's public hospital, patients with it were scattered through the wards. Diane Jones, a nurse on the general medicine unit, remembered that the worst of their treatment was neglect: staff avoided the rooms. In **Cliff Morrison**'s account, meals were left outside doors and beds went unchanged. Morrison, a clinical nurse specialist, was asked to plan a ward for them. He wheeled five or six patients who could walk into a circle on the empty ward and asked what they needed. Little of it, he concluded, was medical. One man told him he wanted to decide for himself whom he saw and when, and that the hospital was not to define his family. On 25 July 1983 **Ward 5B** opened on the fifth floor with twelve beds and twelve nurses, every one of whom had asked to work there, and with counselors from the **[Shanti Project](kloom:e/shanti-project)** on the staff from the first day. By the hospital's account it was the first inpatient AIDS unit in the country.

## The rules of 5B

The hospital's infection-control committee, chaired by two physicians, W. Keith Hadley and Mary Anne Johnson, with the nurse Grace Lusby as its coordinator, wrote the ward's precautions in February 1983, five months before it opened. They assumed that the unknown agent spread like **[hepatitis B](kloom:e/hepatitis-b)**, through close contact, needles and blood products, and that patients whose immunity had failed needed protecting from everyone else as much as everyone else from them.

| What      | The rule for 5B, 1983                                                                              | Why                                                |
| --------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------- |
| Room      | A private room with its own sink and toilet, air neither drawn in nor pushed out                   | To keep other infections from the patient          |
| Hands     | Washed after every contact with a patient, even when gloves were worn                              | The first defense against everything               |
| Gloves    | For drawing blood, starting an IV, and touching urine, stool, vomit or other body fluids           | The hepatitis B model                              |
| Gowns     | Only where heavy soiling was expected, as in intensive care or during a bronchoscopy               | Not for entering the ward, or a room               |
| Masks     | On a coughing patient leaving his room; on staff and visitors near a patient coughing or intubated | Cytomegalovirus, and nobody could rule out the air |
| Needles   | Never broken or recapped; dropped whole into a puncture-proof box in the room                      | Recapping was a common cause of needlesticks       |
| Specimens | Labeled "Hepatitis or AIDS Precautions" and sealed in a bag                                        | To protect messengers and the laboratory           |
| Visitors  | No set hours; the patient named his own family and the "significant other" to share his decisions  | Morrison's policy, which he wrote himself          |

The committee refused to make 5B a gowned and masked ward. Gowning at the door, it warned, might do harm, because staff who felt protected might skip the precautions a particular patient needed. A temporary barrier at the entrance and knee-operated sinks at the door and in the hall would remind everyone where they were. Pregnant women were not to be assigned to it. Morrison gave every patient a primary nurse, who planned and coordinated all of his care, and the nurses voted to work twelve-hour shifts. From the beginning, a newspaper feature later recalled, they decided they would touch their patients, hold them and cry with them.

## Fear, and denial

Elsewhere in the hospital, fear ran the other way. Jones remembered a hospital-wide fight over whether staff should gown, mask and glove just to say good morning, dietary workers who masked to deliver a tray, and four nurses from another unit who sued in 1984 for the right to wear whatever protection they chose. Their union, Local 790 of the Service Employees International Union, led by Luisa Blue, a nurse in the intensive care unit beside 5B, refused to back a right to withhold care but pressed the hospital to teach its staff the real risks. Jones named a mistake of 5B's own, too. Its twelve first nurses were all white, while the Filipino nurses of that intensive care unit, who had nursed AIDS patients since the first cases, came to be painted, unfairly in her view, as the hospital's fearful ones.

The nurses of 5B had a blind spot of their own. Jones had eight or ten **[needlesticks](kloom:e/needlestick-injury)**, she said, and shrugged each off as proof that the disease was not casually caught; in her memory the hospital's first infection of a worker on the job came in 1987. A feature reprinted with her oral history reports a nurse there who stuck herself with a needle she had used on a patient with AIDS, tested positive, and left her job. That year the **[Centers for Disease Control](kloom:e/centers-for-disease-control-and-prevention)** counted three infections among 351 health workers it had followed after a needle or a cut exposed them to infected blood: by our arithmetic, about one in 117. On 21 August 1987 it set out **[universal precautions](kloom:e/universal-precautions)**. Since no history or examination could find every infected patient, gloves, masks, gowns and care with needles were to be used with every patient whenever blood or body fluids might be met, with sharps containers "as close as practical to the use area". 5B's rules had been written for patients known to have AIDS; the new rules were for everyone.

## The toll

By the end of 2025 the city's health department had counted 22,419 deaths among people with AIDS. The unit grew to twenty beds as Ward 5A in early 1986, in January by Morrison's résumé and in March by the hospital's own account, and the deaths did not fall steeply until 1996 and 1997. The documentary **[_5B_](kloom:e/5b-film)** (2018) tells the ward's story through its nurses. It was a nursing unit, designed by a nurse and staffed by volunteers.
