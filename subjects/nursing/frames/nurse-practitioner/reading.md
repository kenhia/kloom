In 1965, at the University of Colorado in Denver, a professor of public health nursing and a professor of pediatrics started a course to teach nurses to do what had been a doctor's work: examine a child, decide what was wrong, and treat it. The nurse was **[Loretta Ford](kloom:e/loretta-ford)**, then 44, who had spent the 1940s and 1950s as a public health nurse in rural Boulder County, where she and her colleagues ran makeshift clinics because there was no one else. The pediatrician was Henry Silver. Their reason was arithmetic: in 1930 an American child made about two visits a year to a doctor, and by 1967 about five, while the supply of doctors, and of primary-care doctors above all, fell behind. Their first student was a nurse named Susan Stearly, and in 1967 the three of them described the program in _Pediatrics_, under the plain title "A Program to Increase Health Care for Children". They called what it made a pediatric **[nurse practitioner](kloom:e/nurse-practitioner)**.

## The Colorado program

The students were already registered nurses with a bachelor's degree, some with a master's in public health nursing. They spent four months at the Medical Center and then twenty months of continued training in practice in the community. The paper's summary set out what a graduate would do, and the pediatrician Robert Hoekelman, looking back in 1998, what the role had become:

| The 1967 paper: she would                                      | Hoekelman, 1998: she does                      |
| -------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------- |
| Give comprehensive care to well children                       | Comprehensive well-child care                  |
| Identify and appraise acute and chronic conditions             | Appraise and manage most acute minor illnesses |
| Evaluate an emergency and manage it until a physician is found | Contribute to the care of chronic conditions   |

The nurse practitioner did not replace the pediatrician: she took the well children and the common complaints, and what needed a physician went to one. The plate draws the course on its time line and the visit as a branching path. Silver went on to start a course for "child health associates", one of the first physician assistant programs, in 1968, and one for school nurse practitioners in 1970.

## Did it work?

The question was asked at once. A study by Duncan, Smith and Silver, from charts at a health center in a low-income neighborhood, found pediatric nurse practitioners' physical assessments of children as adequate as pediatricians'. In 1974 Walter Spitzer, **[David Sackett](kloom:e/david-sackett)** and their colleagues published the Burlington randomized trial, run in a family practice of two physicians in Burlington, Ontario, that compared nurse practitioners' care with the physicians'. We have read it only as others report it: the nurse practitioners' prescribing was as adequate as the doctors', their patients' physical, emotional and social function came out the same, and, in the nurse practitioners' own association's summary, they referred to a physician when they should. In 1986 Congress's **[Office of Technology Assessment](kloom:e/office-of-technology-assessment)** reviewed the studies, many of which it judged small or flawed, and concluded that within their areas of competence nurse practitioners, physician assistants and nurse midwives gave care "whose quality is equivalent to that of care provided by physicians". Nurse practitioners were better at communication, counseling and interviewing; physicians, at problems that needed a technical answer.

## Who objected

Opposition came from two sides. Many nursing faculty saw doctors teaching nurses medicine and feared that medicine would take nursing over. Many physicians, the Office found, were readier to hand over routine tasks such as taking histories than technical ones such as physical examinations, and in 1985 a nursing newsletter reported that the **[American Medical Association](kloom:e/american-medical-association)** had resolved to fight the expanding practice of nurses. What a nurse practitioner may do is settled state by state, in each state's laws: whether she may diagnose, prescribe and practice alone, or only under a physician's supervision or a written agreement with one. The American Association of Nurse Practitioners sorts the states into full, reduced and restricted practice; Wikipedia's article put the full-practice states at 27 in 2023.

## Four advanced roles

The nurse practitioner was the newest of what are now called the advanced practice nurses, each a registered nurse with graduate training. The **[nurse anesthetist](kloom:e/nurse-anesthetist)** was the oldest: in 1877 nuns were giving anesthetics at St Vincent's Hospital in Erie, Pennsylvania. The _nurse midwife_'s American prototype, in Hoekelman's account, was the **[Frontier Nursing Service](kloom:e/frontier-nursing-service)**, which Mary Breckinridge, trained in midwifery in Britain, founded in the Kentucky mountains in 1925; its nurses rode to births on horseback, and it opened its own school of midwifery in 1939. The first graduate program for the _clinical nurse specialist_ was **[Hildegard Peplau](kloom:e/hildegard-peplau)**'s, at Rutgers, where she taught from 1954, to prepare clinical specialists in psychiatric nursing. As of 2026 the association counts more than 461,000 nurse practitioners licensed in the United States. Ford lived to see most of them, and died in January 2025, aged 104.
