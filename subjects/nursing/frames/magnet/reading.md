In the early 1980s most American hospitals could not find enough nurses, and a few could. In 1981 the **[American Academy of Nursing](kloom:e/american-academy-of-nursing)** appointed a task force of four nurses, **Margaret McClure**, **Muriel Poulin**, **Margaret Sovie** and **[Mabel Wandelt](kloom:e/mabel-wandelt)**, to find out what the few were doing. Their report of 1983, _Magnet Hospitals: Attraction and Retention of Professional Nurses_, named those hospitals for the pull they had on nurses, and its findings became the frame for how nursing in a hospital is led and judged.

## How the task force found them

| Step | What was done                                                                                                              |
| ---- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1    | Fellows of the Academy nominated hospitals known among nurses as good places to practice                                   |
| 2    | The hospitals nominated sent in their nurse vacancy and turnover rates                                                     |
| 3    | 41 of the 163 were chosen: low vacancy and low turnover, in local markets where other hospitals were short of nurses       |
| 4    | In each of eight places, one of the four interviewed the chosen hospitals' chief nurse executives together, in the morning |
| 5    | In the afternoon, a second group interview, of staff nurses, each picked by their own chief nurse                          |
| 6    | From the interviews, the task force named the characteristics the 41 seemed to share                                       |

It was a study by reputation and conversation. The hospitals were chosen by what nurses said of them, the staff nurses by their bosses, and only the 41 were interviewed, so nothing showed that other hospitals lacked what the magnets had. What the four heard fell into three groups:

| Group                    | What the magnet hospitals had                                                                                                                                                                                   |
| ------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Administration           | Quality of leadership; organizational structure; management style; staffing; personnel policies and programs                                                                                                    |
| Professional practice    | Professional practice models; quality of care; quality assurance; consultation and resources; autonomy; the community and the hospital; nurses as teachers; the image of nursing; nurse–physician relationships |
| Professional development | Orientation; in-service and continuing education; formal education; career development                                                                                                                          |

These became the "forces of magnetism", fourteen of them as later counted. Looking back more than twenty years on, McClure wrote that in the magnet hospitals a culture supportive of nursing and of quality "seemed to be almost a part of the bricks and mortar."

## From a reputation to a credential

In June 1990 the **[American Nurses Association](kloom:e/american-nurses-association)** incorporated the **[American Nurses Credentialing Center](kloom:e/american-nurses-credentialing-center)** to run its credentialing programs, and in December its board approved a recognition program built on the 1983 study. After a pilot in five hospitals, the first designation went in 1994 to the **[University of Washington Medical Center](kloom:e/university-of-washington-medical-center)** in Seattle. The program took its present name, the **[Magnet Recognition Program](kloom:e/magnet-recognition-program)**, in 2002, and in 2008 it grouped the fourteen forces into five components: transformational leadership; structural empowerment; exemplary professional practice; new knowledge, innovations and improvements; and empirical outcomes. A hospital now applies with written evidence, which in 2008 ran to 164 topics, is visited by appraisers, and must apply again every four years. In 2011, by the center's count, about 6.6 percent of registered American hospitals held the designation.

## One patient more

In 2002 **[Linda Aiken](kloom:e/linda-aiken)** and her colleagues at the University of Pennsylvania linked a survey of 10,184 staff nurses to the records of 232,342 surgical patients discharged from 168 Pennsylvania hospitals between April 1998 and November 1999. After adjusting for the patients' illnesses and the hospitals' size, teaching and technology, each additional patient per nurse went with 7 percent higher odds of dying within 30 days of admission (odds ratio 1.07, 95 percent confidence interval 1.03 to 1.12) and of failure to rescue after a complication. For the nurses, each added patient went with 23 percent higher odds of burnout and 15 percent of job dissatisfaction. The odds compound: by our arithmetic, a nurse with eight patients rather than four means odds of death 1.07⁴, about 1.31 times as high.

The study was cross-sectional: it shows an association, not that adding nurses saves lives. The same research group had found in 1994 that 39 of the original magnet hospitals had Medicare mortality 4.6 percent lower than 195 matched hospitals, and in 2013 that patients in 56 designated Magnet hospitals had 14 percent lower odds of death after surgery than in 508 others. Better work environments and more nurses with bachelor's degrees and specialty certification explained much of the Magnet difference, and the 14 percent was what remained after allowing for them. All three studies come from one group, Aiken's.

## The chief nurse

The work the magnets did well is the work of a nurse executive. Federal rules for hospitals in Medicare require the director of the nursing service to be a registered nurse, responsible for the service and for deciding the types and numbers of nursing staff every area needs; a registered nurse must supervise and evaluate each patient's care and assign it to staff by competence; and every patient needs a current nursing care plan. The Magnet program asks more: its chief nursing officer must hold at least a master's degree and sit on the hospital's highest decision-making and strategic-planning body, and its nurse managers, who answer around the clock for a unit's nurses, its budget, hiring and quality, must hold nursing degrees. Aiken's arithmetic is a nurse executive's budget line. The next frame turns to a single safeguard that a nurse built into the medication pass.
