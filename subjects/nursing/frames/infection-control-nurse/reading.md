Between 1957 and 1959 a strain of staphylococcus ran through **[Torbay Hospital](kloom:e/torbay-hospital)** in Torquay, on the south coast of Devon. A survey of the main men's surgical ward in 1957 found staphylococci of phage group 80 everywhere: in the patients' wounds, and in the styes, boils and carbuncles of the nurses who dressed them. So many nurses were off sick with skin sepsis that the wards were short-staffed. Penicillin had made the 1940s hopeful, but by the 1950s **[_Staphylococcus aureus_](kloom:e/staphylococcus-aureus)** had learned to resist it, and staphylococcal epidemics ran through hospitals in Britain and the United States alike.

![A round culture dish covered in a pale lawn of bacteria, with five small paper discs, each in a dark ring where nothing grew](staph-plate.jpg)

The photograph, from a United States government library of 1972, shows the test that told a hospital which drugs still worked: paper discs of antibiotics laid on a lawn of _S. aureus_, each clearing a ring where the drug stopped it growing.

Early in 1959 the hospital tried something new. At the suggestion of **Brendan Moore**, of the Public Health Laboratory at Exeter, whose idea his obituary says it was, it appointed a whole-time Infection Control Sister, a nurse whose whole working day went to cross-infection. Her name was **E. M. Cottrell**. The Infection Prevention Society, which grew out of the nurses' own association, dates her appointment to April 1959 and counts her as the first **[infection control nurse](kloom:e/infection-prevention-and-control)**. When she left, a man, H. Street, took the post.

## What the sister did

Moore and the hospital's control of infection officer, A. M. N. Gardner of its department of surgery, published what she did in 1963, as the method of a survey of surgical patients in three quarters between April 1959 and June 1960:

| Step           | What was done                                                                    |
| -------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| On admission   | A swab from the patient's nose, before he reached the ward if possible           |
| First dressing | The sister inspected every wound and swabbed it                                  |
| Sepsis seen    | The control of infection officer inspected the wound with her                    |
| After          | Swabs weekly, or more often; patients followed to outlying hospitals and clinics |
| The laboratory | Swabs sent by overnight post to Exeter; every _S. aureus_ phage-typed            |
| The theater    | Nasal swabs from every surgeon and theater nurse (later, every two months)       |
| The record     | Each patient entered in a special book, then on punched cards for counting       |

The hospital laboratory was too busy for the work, which is why the swabs went by post. **[Phage typing](kloom:e/phage-typing)** was what made the counting mean something: a lawn of each patient's staphylococcus was spotted with a grid of drops of different bacteriophages, and the pattern of clear spots where the bacteria died named its type, so that two wounds infected with the same type pointed to one source. The plate draws such a dish.

Of 559 patients, 71 wounds (12.7%) became septic and 48 (8.6%) grew staphylococci. The typing found that at least five of the infections came from two members of the theater staff who carried group 80 staphylococci in their noses and had septic skin themselves.

## Counting, and telling

The sister's numbers were rates, and a rate is only as good as its denominator. Moore and Gardner counted per hundred operations. Later practice also counts per thousand patient-days, because a patient who stays longer has more days in which to be infected. An invented example shows why both are needed:

|                        | Ward A | Ward B |
| ---------------------- | -----: | -----: |
| Admissions             |    200 |    200 |
| Patient-days           |  1,000 |  3,000 |
| Infections             |     12 |     12 |
| Per 100 admissions     |    6.0 |    6.0 |
| Per 1,000 patient-days |   12.0 |    4.0 |

Per admission the wards look the same; per day, Ward A's patients are three times as likely to be infected, which points to its practice rather than its patients.

A count did nothing until somebody saw it. In the United States the **[Centers for Disease Control](kloom:e/centers-for-disease-control-and-prevention)** began studying hospital infections in eight community hospitals in 1965, and its national surveillance system followed in 1970. The new specialists formed a society of their own in 1972, the Association for Practitioners in Infection Control, now the **[Association for Professionals in Infection Control and Epidemiology](kloom:e/association-for-professionals-in-infection-control-and-epidemiology)**. In Britain the Infection Control Nurses Association had formed two years before, with Moore as its president.

## Did it work?

In 1974 the CDC set out to answer that, in the Study on the Efficacy of Nosocomial Infection Control, SENIC. Its teams read the charts of random samples of patients in 338 hospitals, from 1970 and from 1975–76, and interviewed their staff. Before 1970, only 6% of the hospitals had an infection control nurse; by 1977, over 80% had at least one, typically a woman, paid as a nursing supervisor. Across the country, the study estimated, there were 5.7 **[hospital-acquired infections](kloom:e/hospital-acquired-infection)** for every 100 admissions, over two million a year.

Robert Haley and his colleagues published the answer in 1985. Hospitals whose programs had organized surveillance and control, a trained physician, an infection control nurse for every 250 beds and a system for reporting wound infection rates to the surgeons who operated had cut their infection rates by 32%. Hospitals without such programs had seen theirs rise by 18%. Since few hospitals had all the parts, only about 6% of the nation's infections were being prevented.

Britain's first infection control nurse is named; the first in the United States is not, in any source we found. The method is older than either. It was **[Florence Nightingale](kloom:e/florence-nightingale)**'s: count the sick the same way in every ward, compare, and tell the people who can change it, as the frame on Nightingale's hospital statistics shows. The next frame follows the nurse's hands, the commonest route of all.
