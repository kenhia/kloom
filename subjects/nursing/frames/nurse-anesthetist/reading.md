Saint Mary's Hospital in Rochester, Minnesota, was opened in September 1889 by the Sisters of Saint Francis, with the three surgeons of the Mayo family on its staff. Its practice grew into the **[Mayo Clinic](kloom:e/mayo-clinic)**. In 1893 the brothers William James Mayo and **[Charles Horace Mayo](kloom:e/charles-horace-mayo)** took on a nurse, **[Alice Magaw](kloom:e/alice-magaw)**, as their anesthetist. Born in Ohio in 1860, she had trained at the Women's Hospital School of Nursing in Chicago from 1887 to 1889 and worked as a private-duty nurse. In December 1906 she published "A Review of Over Fourteen Thousand Surgical Anæsthesias" in _Surgery, Gynecology and Obstetrics_: "In 14,380 anesthetics given by me, I have yet to see a death directly from the anesthetic." In 1905 alone the hospital gave 3,080 anesthetics, 2,847 of them ether. Charles Mayo, Wikipedia says, called her "the mother of anesthesia".

## Open drop, as she gave it

**[Diethyl ether](kloom:e/diethyl-ether)** had been given for surgery since 1846, and by 1906, she wrote, it was more in favor than ever. Magaw tried nitrous oxide before ether (in 1,000 cases), scopolamine and morphine (in 73), and chloroform with ether, and each time went back to what she called the "drop method", used for over ten years. She set it out exactly:

| Stage       | What she did                                                                                                                                                                                                                                           |
| ----------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| The can     | A four-ounce ether can with an ordinary cork, a groove cut down either side, one groove filled with absorbent cotton standing out an inch as a wick; the size of the drop set by how the cotton was clipped. Two cans: a large dropper and a small one |
| The patient | Walks in and climbs on the table; false teeth and chewing gum out; hands bandaged loosely across the chest; moist cotton over the eyes                                                                                                                 |
| The mask    | An improved Esmarch inhaler, a wire frame with two thicknesses of stockinet; the frame boiled and the stockinet changed after every patient                                                                                                            |
| Going under | Drop slowly "as though it were chloroform" until the face flushes; then add a few layers of gauze and drop a little faster; three to five minutes                                                                                                      |
| During      | Back to the first covering and the small dropper; jaw held up and forward; patient prepared for the operation at the same time                                                                                                                         |

She gave no rate in drops a minute: the dose, she wrote, "cannot be estimated so as to be of any value", and only "the educated anesthetist" could judge it. She judged by the patient. "If the jaw is relaxed and in place, respiration deep and regular, color normal, quality of pulse good, there need be no fear about the rate of pulse or the pupils." Touching the cornea "tells us nothing"; a patient taking back control of the jaw needed more ether; one breathing badly needed the mask lifted and air. With chloroform she took the pulse at the temple and watched the pupil, since "a rapidly dilating and fixed pupil is a danger signal". She talked patients to sleep, never let them count aloud and never told them to "breathe deep", because it made them feel they were suffocating.

The figure of 14,380 is her own, from her own records, and "directly from the anesthetic" is her own judgment of each death; nobody audited it. What she published was a method precise enough for others to follow.

## Why a nurse

Magaw put the case herself: "The dangers of general anesthesia depend more on the lack of experience and incompetency of the anesthetist than on the drug itself." In Kentucky in 1917, a court heard that anesthetics there had long been given by unlicensed medical students, and that many licensed physicians had never made a special study of them. Nurses had given anesthetics before her. Catherine S. Lawrence, nursing an amputation ward at the Armory Square Hospital in Washington after the second battle of Bull Run, wrote in her autobiography of 1896: "I also tied arteries and administered chloroform." Wikipedia puts that on the battlefield in 1863; her own book puts it in the ward, after a battle fought in August 1862. Sister Mary Bernard Sheridan was giving anesthetics at St. Vincent's Hospital in Erie, Pennsylvania, by 1877.

## Whose work it was

Physicians pushed back. In Kentucky the surgeon Louis Frank employed a nurse, Margaret Hatfield, who had given anesthetics to more than 1,200 patients, and the State Board of Health maintained, and a lower court agreed, that she was practicing medicine without a license. On May 4, 1917, in _Frank v. South_, the Court of Appeals of Kentucky reversed: a trained nurse who gave an anesthetic in the presence and under the direction of the surgeon, neither diagnosing nor prescribing, was not practicing medicine. The agreed facts noted that at the Mayo Clinic, in a hundred thousand operations, the anesthetics had "invariably" been given by trained nurses. In Ohio the medical board ruled in 1916 that only a physician could give anesthesia, and the Cleveland surgeon **[George Washington Crile](kloom:e/george-washington-crile)** won an exemption from the legislature in 1917 for nurses trained for it.

Crile's own anesthetist, Agatha Hodgins, had served with him in the Lakeside Unit at the American Ambulance at Neuilly in France in 1914–15, and in 1915 she opened the Lakeside Hospital School of Anesthesia in Cleveland, a six-month course for nurses, physicians and dentists. On June 17, 1931, she and 47 other nurse anesthetists founded what became the American Association of Nurse Anesthesiology. The **[nurse anesthetist](kloom:e/nurse-anesthetist)** was the first nurse specialist of the operating room; the next frame turns to the nurses who stayed at the table.
