In 1888 **[Paul Fürbringer](kloom:e/paul-furbringer)**, director of the Friedrichshain city hospital in Berlin, published a small book on a question he called both ancient and entirely modern: how should a doctor disinfect his hands? Cleanliness had always meant the absence of visible dirt. Bacteriology now asked for the absence of living germs, and Fürbringer cultured what he scraped from under fingernails to see what survived each method. His answer was three steps of one minute each, with alcohol between the soap and the antiseptic, and he claimed it made hands germ-free "with a probability bordering on certainty". Every surgical scrub since has been the same idea, timed and argued over: clean mechanically, then disinfect chemically, and keep the hands up.

## The scrub, then and now

Fürbringer's rules, in our translation: the nails are freed of visible dirt "by the dry route"; the hands are brushed all over for one minute with soap and really warm water, working the spaces under the nails; they are washed for one minute in alcohol of not less than 80 percent; and at once, before it evaporates, they go into the antiseptic, a 1:500 solution of **[mercuric chloride](kloom:e/mercury-ii-chloride)** (sublimate) or 3 percent carbolic acid, the antiseptic of Joseph Lister, for one minute more. The texts that followed lengthened the scrub and changed the chemicals:

Through the twentieth-century texts run the same rules: hands higher than elbows, so water runs off at the elbow and never back; the scrub carried two inches above the elbow; the hands dried with a sterile towel from fingertip to elbow without going back over any part. The Army manual of 1943 allowed five minutes between clean cases, but after a dirty one "she must scrub the full time".

## Counting the bacteria

The long scrubs had little evidence behind them until **Philip B. Price** of **[Johns Hopkins University](kloom:e/johns-hopkins-university)** found a way to count. In 1938 he scrubbed his hands and arms in a standard way for exactly one minute in each of a row of basins of sterile water and cultured each basin. The counts fell on a regular curve, from which he could work out how many bacteria were on the skin and how many each step removed. Scrubbing with soap and brush halved the resident flora about every six minutes, whatever the soap. Alcohol at 70 percent by weight did in one minute what several minutes of scrubbing did, and mercury solutions, the old mainstay, did almost nothing: they left a film on the skin under which the bacteria lived and multiplied.

Price's own hands had been disinfected two days earlier, and the steep first fall is the transient bacteria he had put on them for the test. His advice was seven minutes of scrubbing and three "by the clock" in alcohol. In 1951 he turned on the fashion that had replaced it, a three-minute wash with **[hexachlorophene](kloom:e/hexachlorophene)** soap, which he found far weaker; a surgeon in the discussion complained that a salesman had sold the new scrub to operating-room nurses before the surgeons had studied it. The Food and Drug Administration restricted hexachlorophene in 1972 after deaths from its absorption. **[Chlorhexidine](kloom:e/chlorhexidine)**, patented by Imperial Chemical Industries in 1954, and povidone-iodine took its place. The **[World Health Organization](kloom:e/world-health-organization)**'s guidelines of 2009 call ten-minute scrubs unnecessary, and its surgical guidelines of 2018, the latest we have read, accept either an antimicrobial scrub or an alcohol rub, finding no difference between them in three trials.

## Gown and gloves

The scrubbed nurse picks up the folded gown by its inside and lets it fall open, and the circulating nurse ties it from behind. The gloves were the rubber ones **[William Stewart Halsted](kloom:e/william-stewart-halsted)** had made for **[Caroline Hampton](kloom:e/caroline-hampton)**, the nurse in charge of his operating room, the story of the anchor frame of this trail. In 1958 the nurse put them on by the open method, touching only the glove's turned-down cuff with her bare hand. The Army's later subcourse prefers the closed method: the hands stay inside the sleeves, each glove is laid palm down on the opposite sleeve with its fingers toward the shoulder, and its cuff is stretched over the sleeve, so bare skin never meets the glove's outside.

## What the scrub cannot do

Price's flora could be brought low "but practically never to zero", and under rubber gloves it doubled every forty to fifty minutes. The skin is never sterile, which is why the gloves are. The World Health Organization counted tiny punctures in about 18 percent of gloves after operations, most unnoticed, and noted that no randomized trial has ever shown that surgical hand antisepsis itself prevents infection, nor would one now be thought ethical.
