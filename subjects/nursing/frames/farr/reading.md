On 5 May 1857 Queen Victoria signed a warrant for a Royal Commission to inquire into the health of the army, its hospitals and its medical department. Its chairman was **[Sidney Herbert](kloom:e/sidney-herbert-1st-baron-herbert-of-lea)**, who had sent **[Florence Nightingale](kloom:e/florence-nightingale)** to the **[Crimean War](kloom:e/crimean-war)**, and its eight other members included a colonel, the head of the Army Medical Department, two physicians and the sanitary reformer John Sutherland. Nightingale was not one of them. She gave the commissioners written evidence, which she printed in 1859, and for its numbers she went to **[William Farr](kloom:e/william-farr)**.

Farr was the son of poor parents in Shropshire, who had studied medicine in Paris on a small legacy and heard **[Pierre Louis](kloom:e/pierre-charles-alexandre-louis)**, who weighed treatments by counting their results, lecture there. Since the late 1830s he had been the compiler of abstracts at the **[General Register Office](kloom:e/general-register-office-for-england-and-wales)** in London, which recorded every birth, marriage and death in England and Wales. Out of those records he built national life tables (how many of a thousand people born alive are still alive at each age) and a classification of causes of death, in which the epidemic diseases of filth and crowding were _zymotic_. He brought the method; she brought the army's returns, the questions and the will to make something of the answers.

## Like with like

Farr's way was to compare a group with the people it was drawn from, at the same ages, as a rate: deaths in a year, divided by the number of men living, times 1,000. Soldiers were picked men, enlisted at about nineteen after a surgeon had turned away any with signs of disease, so the fair comparison was with civilian men of twenty to forty, not with a population full of infants and the old. The last column is ours. "That is to say," she wrote, "that if the army were as healthy as the population from which they are drawn, they would die at one-half the rate they die now." Even Manchester, among the unhealthiest towns in the kingdom, buried 12.4 of its men of army age a year, against 17.5 for the army at home. The Commission's report, signed in 1858, made the same sum over fifteen years: from 1839 to 1853 the army at home and abroad served 1,762,037 man-years and lost 58,139 men, 33.0 a year per 1,000; at the civilian 9.2, it would have lost 16,211, an excess of 41,928.

The comparison had a known bias, and the report said which way it ran. A soldier broken by service was invalided out and died a civilian, so the army's figure was flattered, not inflated. The plate follows a thousand recruits of twenty to forty at the table's rates: by our arithmetic about 820 civilians would live to forty, and about 692 soldiers.

## As if they were shot

Nightingale put the numbers into words in her confidential _Notes on Matters Affecting the Health, Efficiency, and Hospital Administration of the British Army_, printed in 1858. "We hear with horror of the loss of 400 men on board the '_Birkenhead_,' by carelessness at sea," she wrote, "but what should we feel" "if we were told that 1,100 men are annually doomed to death in our Army at home by causes which might be prevented?" In her concluding remarks she supposed a home army of 100,000 Guards brought to the health of the civilians around it: "we should save nearly 1,500 good soldiers yearly", who were "as certainly killed by the neglects specified as if they were drawn up on Salisbury Plain and shot." The neglects were chiefly in the barracks, crowded and unventilated, and the Commission's report asked that every man be given no less than 600 cubic feet of air in his barrack room, with three feet between beds.

## What came of it

The report asked for barracks and hospitals to be inspected and rebuilt, a medical school for army surgeons, and a statistical branch in the Army Medical Department to collect sickness and deaths on one plan and publish them. All three came. Four sub-commissions carried the work, a statistical branch under Thomas Graham Balfour opened in 1859, and the Army Medical School began at Fort Pitt, Chatham, in 1860, moving to the new hospital at Netley in 1863. On 21 December 1858 the Statistical Society of London, today's **[Royal Statistical Society](kloom:e/royal-statistical-society)**, elected her a Fellow, its first woman. The Society's minutes give the day; Wikipedia's article on her says 1859, the year its journal printed them.

Farr, meanwhile, wanted his life tables printed by machine: in 1857 his office ordered a copy of the Swedish Scheutz **difference engine** to compute and set the next English Life Table. Their next questions were about the hospital itself: how it should be built, which the frame on _Notes on Hospitals_ takes up.
