Nurses are the largest part of the health workforce on earth. In its report of May 2025, _State of the World's Nursing_, the **[World Health Organization](kloom:e/world-health-organization)** counted 29.8 million nurses in 2023, up from 27.9 million in 2018, and found that they make up about 57 percent of the five occupations it tracks most closely: doctors, nurses and midwives, dentists and pharmacists. This frame gives what was known as of October 2026, and every figure carries its own date, because each comes from a survey that took a year or two to gather.

The American figures come from the National Council of State Boards of Nursing's survey of 2024, which sampled licensed nurses across the country and weighted their answers to the whole. A nurse licensed in two states holds two licenses, which is why the count of people is smaller. Hospitals and nursing homes remain the main places they work. The share holding the **[Bachelor of Science in Nursing](kloom:e/bachelor-of-science-in-nursing)** or a higher degree has been rising: 39 percent of American registered nurses in 2015 had first qualified with a bachelor's degree, and 46 percent in 2024.

## The shortage

![Bar chart of nurses per 10,000 people in 2023 by WHO region: Africa 14.1, Eastern Mediterranean 15.5, South-East Asia 17.4, Western Pacific 44, Americas 72.2, Europe 76.9, and the world 37.1](density.svg)

The world is short of nurses where it has fewest of them. High-income countries, with 17 percent of the world's people, have 46 percent of its nurses, and in the high-income countries that report it, 23 percent of their nurses were born abroad. The WHO expects the shortage to shrink by 2030, but 69 percent of what remains to be in Africa and the Eastern Mediterranean. Its nurses are young, a third of them under 35, with 19 percent aged 55 or more and near retirement.

In the United States the shortage is less of numbers than of nurses who stay. The 2024 survey asked those planning to leave why: 61.4 percent named retirement, 41.3 percent stress and burnout, 32.8 percent workload and 26.0 percent understaffing, and many named more than one. The **[COVID-19 pandemic](kloom:e/covid-19-pandemic)** sharpened all of it. The survey describes the departure of hundreds of thousands of experienced nurses during its emergency phase, some of whom later came back. Others did not survive it. The **[International Council of Nurses](kloom:e/international-council-of-nurses)**, the federation of national nursing associations founded in 1899, had confirmed 1,500 nurses' deaths in 44 countries by October 2020, and in 2021 the WHO estimated that between 80,000 and 180,000 health and care workers had died of COVID-19 from January 2020 to May 2021, most likely about 115,500, while warning that deaths were badly under-reported.

## A shift now

How many patients a nurse carries is the question [Linda Aiken](kloom:e/linda-aiken)'s research measured, and that states have begun to answer by law. Federal rules ask only for "adequate numbers" of nurses. California set minimum ratios first: its law of 1999 took effect in January 2004, with one nurse to six patients on a medical-surgical unit, and to five from 2005. Oregon passed a law of the same kind in 2023, with ratios hospitals had to meet from June 2024:

| Unit                                    | Most patients for one registered nurse, Oregon                            |
| --------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Operating room                          | 1                                                                         |
| Intensive care                          | 2                                                                         |
| Labor and delivery                      | 2, or 1 in active labor or with complications                             |
| Post-anesthesia care                    | 2                                                                         |
| Intermediate care                       | 3                                                                         |
| Oncology, cardiac telemetry, pediatrics | 4                                                                         |
| Medical-surgical                        | 5, and 4 from July 2026 under the act as passed                           |
| Emergency department                    | an average of 4 over a 12-hour shift, never more than 5; 1 trauma patient |

A ratio fixes the floor, not the work. On a medical-surgical unit that means up to five patients for a shift: their assessments, their medications scanned one by one, their care plans, their families and their discharges, and the judgment of which one needs watching most. In California, Aiken's group found, 88 percent of medical-surgical nurses had five patients or fewer on their last shift, against 19 percent in New Jersey and 33 percent in Pennsylvania.

Behind the thirty million are single nurses keeping watch at single bedsides. The last frame is for one of them.
