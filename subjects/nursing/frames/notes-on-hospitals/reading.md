In October 1858 two papers by **[Florence Nightingale](kloom:e/florence-nightingale)** were read at Liverpool to the National Association for the Promotion of Social Science, on the defects in the construction of hospital wards. She printed them in 1859 as _Notes on Hospitals_, with her evidence to the Royal Commission on the army, and in 1863 brought out a much enlarged third edition, with plans of hospitals from Paris to India. Its preface opens with the sentence the book is remembered by: "It may seem a strange principle to enunciate as the very first requirement in a Hospital that it should do the sick no harm." Hospitals in crowded cities, she went on, killed more of their patients than the same diseases killed at home. The 1859 edition named four "radical defects in hospital construction": too many sick under one roof, and too little space, ventilation and light.

## A ward by the numbers

Her answer was the **[pavilion hospital](kloom:e/pavilion-hospital)**: separate blocks of two floors, joined by a low corridor, each floor one long ward open to the air on both sides. The plan was not new: the Royal Naval Hospital at Plymouth had been built in separate blocks from 1758, and she held up **[Lariboisière Hospital](kloom:e/lariboisiere-hospital)** in Paris, opened in 1854, as the model to improve on. What she added was arithmetic. The 1863 edition gives a ward's every dimension:

| Rule                     | Her figure                                                                                                                                         |
| ------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Beds a ward              | 20 to 32                                                                                                                                           |
| Floor a bed              | not less than 100 square feet; the bed itself takes 21 to 24½                                                                                      |
| Height                   | 15 feet, giving 1,500 cubic feet a bed; 2,500 for small wards of grave cases                                                                       |
| A ward for 20            | 80 feet long, 25 (or 26) wide, 16 (or 15) high: 1,600 (or 1,560) cubic feet a bed                                                                  |
| Foot to foot of beds     | 11 (or 12) feet                                                                                                                                    |
| Windows                  | one at least to every two beds, opposite each other, at least 4 feet 8 inches wide, the sill 2 to 3 feet from the floor so the patient can see out |
| Between beds by the wall | not less than 3 feet                                                                                                                               |

The figures check: 80 × 25 × 16 is 32,000 cubic feet, and 80 × 25 is 2,000 square feet, a twentieth of each to a bed. The plate draws that ward in plan and in section. Some reasons were sound and some were not. The limit of 32 beds was partly the nurse's: a ward should be no larger than the head nurse could "overlook the whole at a glance". The three feet between beds rested on miasma, the theory that disease spread by foul air, which "may be said, roughly speaking, to diminish as the square of the distance".

## Built to it

The army built first. The **[Royal Herbert Hospital](kloom:e/royal-herbert-hospital)** at Woolwich, built on Sidney Herbert's authority and designed by Captain Douglas Galton of the Royal Engineers with her advice, opened on 1 November 1865. In the 1863 edition, which prints its plan while it was going up, she called it "by far the finest hospital establishment in the united kingdom, or indeed in Europe": 650 beds in seven detached buildings, wards of 28 to 32 beds with a window to every two, pavilions set apart by twice their height. It fell short of her figures in one place: army regulations held its wards to 14 feet high, giving 1,200 to 1,400 cubic feet a bed. **[St Thomas' Hospital](kloom:e/st-thomas-hospital)**, moved off its old site for a railway, was rebuilt in Lambeth with six ward pavilions 125 feet apart, linked by low corridors, and opened in 1871, with her nursing school in it. She had wanted it moved to the country.

## Counting deaths by the bed

The 1863 edition also opened with a table from the Registrar-General's annual report, where **[William Farr](kloom:e/william-farr)** was the statistician: the patients in each hospital on census day, 8 April 1861, against the deaths it registered in the year.

| Hospitals           | Inmates, 8 April 1861 | Deaths, 1861 | "Mortality per cent on inmates" |
| ------------------- | --------------------: | -----------: | ------------------------------: |
| 24 in London        |                 4,214 |        3,828 |                           90.84 |
| 12 in large towns   |                 1,870 |        1,555 |                           83.16 |
| 25 county hospitals |                 2,248 |          886 |                           39.41 |

In London, she wrote, "very nearly every bed" yielded a death in the year. **[John Simon](kloom:e/john-simon-pathologist)**, the government's chief medical officer, answered in his report for 1863 that such rates were "utterly worthless as measures of hospital unsuccess". A year's deaths divided by one day's patients rises with how fast a hospital empties its beds and how grave the cases it takes. An invented example shows it. Two hospitals each hold 100 patients and lose one in twenty of them. If one keeps its patients 60 days and the other 20, the first treats about 600 a year and loses 30, "30 per cent"; the second treats about 1,800 and loses 90, "90 per cent". The great London hospitals took the acute and the dying; country hospitals, Simon's reporters John Bristowe and Timothy Holmes found, admitted chronic cases on subscribers' letters and kept them for the full term. Nightingale had herself warned that a hospital's health "ought not … to be inferred solely" from its mortality, and that recoveries and the length of stay must be counted too, the subject of the next frame.

## Why it lasted, and why it went

The pavilion was built on the miasma theory, and germ theory took its reason away: once infection was understood to travel by hands and instruments, the asepsis of the operating room mattered more than the cubic feet of the ward. Block hospitals of many floors rose in American cities from the 1890s and were the usual form by the 1930s, on land too dear for pavilions. Yet the long open ward lived on in British hospitals as the **[Nightingale ward](kloom:e/nightingale-ward)**, where one nurse could see every bed, and three of St Thomas' Victorian pavilions still stand. The trail goes on to her proposal for counting hospitals' results all on one plan.
