In the summer of 1921 an Oxford undergraduate named **[Charles Elton](kloom:e/charles-sutherland-elton)**, twenty-one years old, sailed north with the university's expedition to Spitsbergen as an assistant to **[Julian Huxley](kloom:e/julian-huxley)**. On the way the party stopped at **[Bear Island](kloom:e/bjornoya)**, a bare plateau of rock and moss in the Barents Sea, with no trees and only one native land mammal, the **[arctic fox](kloom:e/arctic-fox)**. Elton and the botanist **[Victor Summerhayes](kloom:e/v-s-summerhayes)** set out to list everything that lived there and, the new part, what each thing ate. In 1923 they published the result in the _Journal of Ecology_ as a diagram of boxes and arrows, the food relations of a whole island.

![A hand-lettered diagram of boxes joined by arrows: marine animals feed kittiwakes, guillemots, puffins and other seabirds and seals; seals feed the polar bear; the seabirds, ptarmigan, purple sandpiper and snow bunting feed the arctic fox; bird dung feeds bacteria, which make mineral salts for plants; plants feed springtails, flies, mites and the land birds](bear-island.jpg)

Elton reprinted it four years later in his first book, and told the reader to "start at 'marine animals' and follow the arrows." Bear Island was chosen, he wrote, because on so poor an island the whole web could be worked out "fairly completely." The arrows show how little of the island's life the island feeds. The sea feeds the seabirds, and they feed the fox. Their droppings, rotted by bacteria into mineral salts, feed the plants. Seals feed the polar bear, and the fox eats even the bear's dung.

## Chains, niches and a pyramid

**_Animal Ecology_**, published in 1927, was Elton's attempt to turn natural history into a science of animal communities, an **[ecology](kloom:e/ecology)** of animals. It set out four ideas that are still taught. The first was the **[food chain](kloom:e/food-chain)**: "There are, in fact, chains of animals linked together by food, and all dependent in the long run upon plants." Elton's own word for the whole web of chains in a community was the _food-cycle_; "food web" came later. The second was that a predator can eat only food within a certain range of sizes, so each link in a chain turns small food into larger, and a chain usually has fewer than five links.

The third was the **[niche](kloom:e/ecological-niche)**, which Elton defined by what an animal does rather than where it lives: "the animal's place in its community, its relations to food and enemies." The same niche, he pointed out, can be filled by quite different animals in different parts of the world. The fourth was the **[pyramid of numbers](kloom:e/ecological-pyramid)**. He opened it with a proverb, "One hill cannot shelter two tigers": in an oak wood there are vast numbers of aphids, many spiders, fewer warblers and one or two hawks. Elton explained the pyramid by size and breeding. Small animals multiply fast and so can feed larger ones, which multiply more slowly. He admitted that "good data are very scarce."

## Counting the calories

The data came from a Minnesota bog lake. Over four years **[Raymond Lindeman](kloom:e/raymond-lindeman)**, with his wife, Eleanor, sampled everything that lived in Cedar Bog Lake, a shallow, weedy lake slowly turning into bog, from the pondweeds and algae to the plankton, insect larvae and fish. He sorted its life into feeding levels, the **[trophic levels](kloom:e/trophic-level)**, and then did what Elton had not: he converted every level into energy, in gram-calories fixed per square centimeter of lake each year, and added back what each level lost before it could be measured.

| Feeding level       | Growth measured | Respiration | Eaten by the next level | Decay | Total productivity | Efficiency                     |
| ------------------- | --------------- | ----------- | ----------------------- | ----- | ------------------ | ------------------------------ |
| Producers (plants)  | 70.4            | 23.4        | 14.8                    | 2.8   | 111.3              | 0.10% of the sunlight          |
| Primary consumers   | 7.0             | 4.4         | 3.1                     | 0.3   | 14.8               | 13.3% of the producers' energy |
| Secondary consumers | 1.3             | 1.8         | 0.0                     | 0.0   | 3.1                | 22.3% of the herbivores'       |

The plate draws the three levels to scale. The lake's plants captured about a tenth of one percent of the 118,872 calories of sunlight that fell on each square centimeter in a year. Each level's total is its own growth plus what it burned, what was eaten from it and what decayed, so what the herbivores took, 14.8, is the whole of the next level's income. Lindeman read the rising efficiencies as a principle: consumers higher in the food cycle "are progressively more efficient in the use of their food supply," while losing more of it to respiration, and the two together limit how long a chain can be. By our arithmetic his own table gives 20.9 percent for the carnivores, not the 22.3 he printed.

The "ten percent law" now taught in his name is a later rounding. Lindeman never called it a law, and his own figures ran from a tenth of a percent to more than twenty.

The paper nearly did not appear. _Ecology_'s two referees, Chancey Juday and Paul Welch, the country's leading lake scientists, both said no. Juday complained that it rested on "belief, probability, possibility, assumption and imaginary lakes." Lindeman's adviser at Yale, **[G. Evelyn Hutchinson](kloom:e/g-evelyn-hutchinson)**, wrote in its defense that a testable hypothesis was worth more than "an unending number of marks on paper," and the paper was accepted in March 1942. Lindeman died that June, aged twenty-six, probably of hepatitis, four months before it was printed.

## The system and the invaders

Lindeman built on a word from England. In 1935 the botanist **[Arthur Tansley](kloom:e/arthur-tansley)**, arguing against treating a plant community as a single organism, proposed the **[ecosystem](kloom:e/ecosystem)**: "the whole system (in the sense of physics)," the organisms together with the physical environment they live in. Lindeman's lake, with its "ooze" of the dead feeding the living, was one, measured.

Elton stayed at Oxford all his career. In 1958 he published _The Ecology of Invasions by Animals and Plants_, on **[invasive species](kloom:e/invasive-species)** carried across the world by people and the damage they do in communities that never met them; it is often counted the founding book of invasion ecology. Thirty-five years after _Animal Ecology_, a book about pesticides would show what else a food chain can carry: Rachel Carson's _Silent Spring_.
