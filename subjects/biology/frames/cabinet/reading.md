Gessner gathered what had been written about animals. A century later, collectors gathered the things themselves. A **[cabinet of curiosities](kloom:e/cabinet-of-curiosities)** (in German a _Kunstkammer_ or _Wunderkammer_, a room of art or of wonders) was a room, not a cupboard, filled with whatever its owner could find that was rare: stones and shells, stuffed animals, horns and teeth, dried plants, weapons, coins and carvings from far away. A prince's cabinet showed off his reach. A physician's or an apothecary's was a working collection, kept to be studied and shown to visitors.

The earliest known picture of a natural history cabinet is a fold-out engraving in **[Ferrante Imperato](kloom:e/ferrante-imperato)**'s _Dell'historia naturale_, printed at Naples in 1599: an apothecary's vaulted room, its ceiling covered with fishes and shells around a hanging crocodile, its walls with pigeonholes of minerals and shelves of books. The best known is the one in the drawing below.

## Worm's museum

**[Ole Worm](kloom:e/ole-worm)** was a professor at the **[University of Copenhagen](kloom:e/university-of-copenhagen)** and physician to King Christian IV. His collection was in **[Copenhagen](kloom:e/copenhagen)**, and its catalog, the _Museum Wormianum_, was printed at Leiden by the Elzevier press in 1655, the year after he died of the plague. Its title calls it a "history of rarer things, natural as well as artificial, domestic as well as exotic," which are the kinds the cabinets were built from (translations from the Latin here are ours).

![An engraved view of a long room: stuffed fishes, birds, a bear and turtle shells hang from the ceiling beams, antlers and horns line the wall by the windows, and shelves of labeled boxes run down the right-hand wall and across the back, with a title tablet on the checkered floor](worm.jpg)

The frontispiece, signed by the engraver G. Wingendorp, shows how the room was arranged. Large animals hang from the beams. Antlers, horns and skulls are fixed to the wall between the windows. Weapons, oars and clothing hang at the back. Below them run open shelves of boxes, each labeled by kind; a long spiraled tusk lies on the top shelf at the back. Most of the labels match the four books of the catalog (a few, such as _Varia_, "various," do not):

| Labels on the boxes in the engraving                              | Book of the _Museum Wormianum_                                 |
| ----------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------- |
| _Terrae, Salia, Sulphura, Lapides, Metalla, Metallica, Mineralia_ | I. Things dug from the earth: earths, salts, stones and metals |
| _Radices, Herbae, Semina, Fructus, Ligna, Cortices_               | II. Plants: roots, herbs, seeds, fruits, woods and barks       |
| _Conchilia, Conchiliata, Turbinata, Cochleae, Animalium partes_   | III. Animals: shells, snails and the parts of animals          |
| Weapons, clothes, tools and vessels, unlabeled                    | IV. Things made by people                                      |

The order climbs: plants come after stones, and among the plants Worm began with fungi, because nature, he wrote, rises "from the more imperfect to the more perfect." Animals came next, and things made by hand last. Every object had a place, and every place a name.

## Not a horn, but a tooth

A collection let Worm test what books said. Cabinets prized the **[unicorn](kloom:e/unicorn)**'s horn, long, white and spiraled, and sold it as an antidote to poison. Worm tells in the _Museum_ how, in 1636, he reproached the Royal Chancellor, Christian Friis, because the merchants who brought these horns from the sea off Greenland never brought any other part of the animal. Friis smiled, took him into his own museum, and showed him a skull with the horn still in it. Worm measured and drew it. The skull was two Roman feet long. The horn sat in a socket in the left side of the upper jaw, a foot and four inches deep, set in as a tooth is set in its jaw, while the right side had no socket at all. It was the tooth of a whale, the **[narwhal](kloom:e/narwhal)**. A drawing of the whole animal came to him from Thorlákur Skúlason, bishop of Hólar in Iceland, who had seen one come ashore with the Greenland ice.

His own account dates the skull to 1636; Wikipedia's article on Worm dates his public refutation to 1638. Worm did not let go of the old belief. The same chapter reports that on 3 October 1636 he and an apothecary gave arsenic and sublimate to two pigeons and some kittens, and then scrapings of the tusk, "not entirely without success," and concludes that though it is a tooth and not a horn, it "does not seem to lack powers."

## The museum as an instrument

The **[Royal Society](kloom:e/royal-society)** in London kept a collection of its own, at Gresham College. Its catalog of 1681 was written by **[Nehemiah Grew](kloom:e/nehemiah-grew)**, who had followed Henry Oldenburg as the Society's secretary. The Society's tusk, given by its president, was eight feet long, with spiral furrows "making about seven Rounds from the bottom to the top," which the plate draws. Grew reported that the **[Inuit](kloom:e/inuit)** of Greenland tipped the thickest tusks with iron to hunt whales. He read Worm's poison tests too, and asked the question a control would answer: "Whether these Dogs might not have liv'd without the Horn."

That is what collections did for the study of life. A specimen on a shelf could be looked at again, measured, compared with the next one and shown to a doubter, long after the traveler who brought it had gone. After Worm's death his collection passed to King Frederick III. **[Hans Sloane](kloom:e/hans-sloane)**, a London physician whose fortune rested in part on his wife's Jamaican sugar estates, worked by enslaved people, gathered some 71,000 objects and left them to the nation for £20,000, a sum far below their worth. An act of Parliament of 1753 accepted them, and they became the founding collection of the **[British Museum](kloom:e/british-museum)**.

With the specimens on the shelves, the next question was how to sort them into kinds.
