By the 1960s biologists agreed that living things came in two kinds. In a **[eukaryote](kloom:e/eukaryote)** (an animal, a plant, a fungus or an amoeba) the cell keeps its DNA inside a nucleus; in a **[prokaryote](kloom:e/prokaryote)**, a bacterium, it does not. The French biologist Édouard Chatton had drawn the line in the 1930s, and Roger Stanier and C. B. van Niel made it the textbook's in 1962. Above it sat Robert Whittaker's five kingdoms of 1969: animals, plants, fungi, protists, and all the bacteria together as Monera. No one could place the bacteria on the **[tree of life](kloom:e/tree-of-life-biology)**. Their shapes were too simple to compare, and Stanier and van Niel doubted that their family tree could ever be drawn.

**[Carl Woese](kloom:e/carl-woese)**, a biophysicist at the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign, set out to draw it from a molecule instead. He wanted one that every living thing carries and that changes slowly enough to record its deepest branches. In the words of his 1977 paper with **[George E. Fox](kloom:e/george-e-fox)**, **[ribosomal RNA](kloom:e/ribosomal-rna)** fitted: "It is a component of all self-replicating systems; it is readily isolated; and its sequence changes but slowly with time." Every cell builds its proteins on ribosomes, and the smaller of their two parts is built around a strand of RNA, 16S in bacteria and 18S in eukaryotes, about 1,500 letters long in a bacterium.

## A fingerprint on film

There was no way in the 1970s to read the whole strand. What Woese's laboratory did, as Fox and the historian Jan Sapp describe it in 2013, was read it in pieces:

| Step        | What was done                                                                                                                                    |
| ----------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Label       | Cells grown in a medium with radioactive phosphorus-32; the lab received 50 millicuries of it every other Monday                                 |
| Cut         | The 16S RNA digested with ribonuclease T1, which cuts after every G                                                                              |
| Fingerprint | The fragments spread in two dimensions by electrophoresis on paper, and the paper laid on X-ray film: each fragment a dark spot                  |
| Catalog     | Each spot cut further with other enzymes until its sequence could be read; every fragment of six letters or more listed                          |
| Compare     | Two catalogs scored by an association coefficient, S: twice the letters in the fragments they share, divided by all the letters in both catalogs |

One organism took three to four weeks. Woese read the films at a light table eight hours a day, and the laboratory drew its trees from the catalogs by average linkage, joining first the pair most alike.

In 1976 **[Ralph Wolfe](kloom:e/ralph-stoner-wolfe)**, a microbiologist at Illinois, and his student William Balch found a way to grow **[methanogens](kloom:e/methanogen)**, microbes that live without oxygen and turn carbon dioxide and hydrogen into methane, in pressurized tubes that Woese's laboratory could feed phosphorus-32. The first methanogen fingerprint lacked two spots that had been on every fingerprint before, fragments carrying chemically modified letters, and lacked the sequences found in every bacterium so far. Woese told his laboratory a new form of life had been found, and then suggested that Fox had botched the RNA. A second methanogen gave the same result.

## Three lines of descent

The paper of November 1977 compared 13 catalogs: three eukaryotes (yeast, duckweed and a line of mouse cells), five bacteria and a chloroplast, and four methanogens. A few of its association coefficients:

| Pair                                            | S    |
| ----------------------------------------------- | ---- |
| Two methanogens of the genus _Methanobacterium_ | 0.51 |
| _Bacillus_ and _Corynebacterium_ (two bacteria) | 0.34 |
| Duckweed and mouse cells (two eukaryotes)       | 0.36 |
| A methanogen and _E. coli_                      | 0.11 |
| A methanogen and yeast                          | 0.11 |
| _E. coli_ and yeast                             | 0.05 |

The methanogens were no closer to the bacteria than to the eukaryotes. Woese and Fox concluded that living systems "represent one of three aboriginal lines of descent", and called the third the archaebacteria, a name Linda Magrum and David Nanney suggested. The plate is the tree their table gives by average linkage, by our arithmetic: the methanogens join the bacteria at 0.10 and the eukaryotes join the rest at 0.08, a difference too small to read as an order of branching, as the paper itself warned. A NASA press release put the find on the front page of _The New York Times_ on 3 November 1977.

The group soon grew. **[Otto Kandler](kloom:e/otto-kandler)**, in Munich, had found that methanogen cell walls lacked the peptidoglycan of bacterial ones, and the salt-loving halophiles and the hot-spring Sulfolobus and Thermoplasma joined them. **[_Halobacterium salinarum_](kloom:e/halobacterium-salinarum)** lives in the saltiest brines, the salt that keeps food from spoiling, and turns up in salted fish and hides; such microbes, with algae, color the evaporating ponds of salt works red.

## Domains and doubters

In 1990 Woese, Kandler and Mark Wheelis proposed a new rank above the kingdom, the domain, and three of them: Bacteria, **[Archaea](kloom:e/archaea)** and Eucarya. They asked that "archaebacteria" be dropped, since the word "incorrectly suggests a specific relationship between the Archaea and the Bacteria."

The elders of evolutionary biology objected. **[Salvador Luria](kloom:e/salvador-luria)** was among them. **[Ernst Mayr](kloom:e/ernst-mayr)**, in "Two empires or three?" (1998), called the archaebacteria "like the discovery of a new continent" but kept the two empires, prokaryote and eukaryote, because classification should weigh how alike organisms are, not genealogy alone. "Woese was not trained as a biologist", he added. **[Lynn Margulis](kloom:e/lynn-margulis)**, whose five-kingdom scheme the domains overturned, argued against them too. The archaea were accepted as a group by the mid-1980s, few biologists now treat the prokaryotes as one, and Woese, who died in 2012, was given the Crafoord Prize in 2003 "for his discovery of a third domain of life".

The tree has moved again since. In 2015 a team from Uppsala University found, in DNA from seafloor mud near a hydrothermal vent field called Loki's Castle, archaea whose genes branched beside the eukaryotes', and in 2020 a Japanese team reported growing one after a decade of trying: a sphere about 550 nanometers wide with long, branching arms. These **[Asgard archaea](kloom:e/asgard-archaea)** suggest that the eukaryotes arose from within the Archaea, so that there are two domains of life, not three. That view has become more widely accepted. As of October 2026, though, papers of 2023 and 2025 disagree about where in the Asgard tree the eukaryotes sit, and a 2017 analysis found that taking out a single protein, perhaps contaminated, broke the link altogether.

The next frame turns from life's oldest branches to a single animal, copied from one cell.
