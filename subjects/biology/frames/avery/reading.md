At the hospital of the **[Rockefeller Institute](kloom:e/rockefeller-university)** in New York, the [pneumococcus](kloom:e/streptococcus-pneumoniae) had been studied since 1913, when **[Oswald Avery](kloom:e/oswald-avery)** joined its pneumonia team. Avery and his colleagues sorted the bacterium into types, and showed that what made a type was the sugar of its capsule. So when Fred Griffith reported in 1928 that one type could be turned into another, the claim fell on Avery's own ground. By 1932 his laboratory could turn rough cells derived from Type II into smooth Type III in a test tube, with a filtered extract of Type III bacteria. What, in the extract, did it?

## Ten years of purifying

**[Colin MacLeod](kloom:e/colin-munro-macleod)** took up the question in 1934. It took him three years to make the test reliable. Not every rough strain would transform, and he found one that would, R36A, by passing a Type II culture 36 times through serum against Type II. Enzymes in the bacteria, and in the serum added to the broth, slowly destroyed the active principle, and heating to 60 or 65 °C stopped them. MacLeod set the work aside in 1937 and took it up again with Avery in 1940, and **[Maclyn McCarty](kloom:e/maclyn-mccarty)**, who arrived in 1941, carried the purification through. Their paper, received by the _Journal of Experimental Medicine_ on 1 November 1943 and printed on 1 February 1944, gives the method, drawn in the plate. The work is now called the **[Avery–MacLeod–McCarty experiment](kloom:e/avery-macleod-mccarty-experiment)**, and its purification ran:

| Step           | What was done                                                                                                                                     | What it removed                                       |
| -------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------- |
| Grow           | Type III pneumococci grown in 50 to 75 liters of broth, collected in a chilled centrifuge                                                         | The broth                                             |
| Heat           | 65 °C for 30 minutes                                                                                                                              | The bacteria's own enzyme that destroys the principle |
| Wash, dissolve | Three saline washes, then sodium deoxycholate, which dissolves the cells                                                                          | Much of the capsular sugar, protein and RNA           |
| Deproteinize   | Shaken with chloroform until no film of protein gel formed                                                                                        | Protein                                               |
| Digest         | A bacterial enzyme that breaks down the Type III capsular sugar                                                                                   | The remaining capsule                                 |
| Fractionate    | Alcohol added drop by drop; at about an equal volume the active material came out as fibers that wound round the stirring rod; four or five times | Everything else                                       |

From 75 liters of culture they recovered 10 to 25 milligrams.

## How they knew

The fibers gave no test for [protein](kloom:e/protein) and a strong one for **[DNA](kloom:e/dna)**. Four preparations held nitrogen and phosphorus in ratios from 1.58 to 1.75, averaging 1.67, against 1.69 calculated for DNA; protein, with much nitrogen and almost no phosphorus, would have pushed the ratio up. In the ultracentrifuge and in electrophoresis the material moved as one substance, and the activity moved with it. Then came the enzymes. Crystalline trypsin and chymotrypsin, which digest protein, left the activity alone, and so did crystalline ribonuclease, which digests RNA. No pure enzyme for DNA was to hand, so they tried crude ones, and measured each against DNA that their colleague **[Alfred Mirsky](kloom:e/alfred-mirsky)** had prepared from fish sperm:

| Crude preparation        | Breaks down known DNA | Destroys the principle |
| ------------------------ | --------------------- | ---------------------- |
| Dog intestinal mucosa    | Yes                   | Yes                    |
| Rabbit bone phosphatase  | No                    | No                     |
| Swine kidney phosphatase | No                    | No                     |
| Pneumococcal autolysate  | Yes                   | Yes                    |
| Dog and rabbit serum     | Yes                   | Yes                    |

The two powers went together. Dog serum lost both at 60 °C, rabbit serum only at 65 °C, and fluoride blocked both. Their best preparation still transformed at 0.003 micrograms, one part in 600 million of the culture.

## Within the limits of the methods

The paper's conclusion was guarded: "The evidence presented supports the belief that a nucleic acid of the desoxyribose type is the fundamental unit of the transforming principle of Pneumococcus Type III." In private Avery said more. In a long letter to his brother Roy, a bacteriologist in Nashville, begun on 13 May 1943 and finished on the 26th (the National Library of Medicine's record gives both dates), he wrote: "Sounds like a virus - may be a gene." And: "It is lots of fun to blow bubbles but it is wiser to prick them yourself before some one else tries to."

Doubt came from close by. Mirsky, who had supplied the DNA, argued for years that a trace of protein too small to detect could carry the activity, and **[Phoebus Levene](kloom:e/phoebus-levene)**'s **[tetranucleotide hypothesis](kloom:e/tetranucleotide-hypothesis)**, also from the Rockefeller Institute, made DNA too monotonous to carry genes. McCarty and Avery answered in 1946 with a purified **[deoxyribonuclease](kloom:e/deoxyribonuclease)** from ox pancreas, which destroyed the activity in minute amounts. The paper was read, too: it had over three hundred citations by 1954, and **[Erwin Chargaff](kloom:e/erwin-chargaff)** turned his laboratory to DNA after reading it. That chemistry is told in the chemistry of DNA before 1953.

Avery received the Royal Society's **[Copley Medal](kloom:e/copley-medal)**. He would not travel to London to collect it, so the Society's president, Henry Dale, brought it to his laboratory in New York; in an address to the Society Dale called the substance "the gene in solution, one is tempted to call it". The Nobel committee was slower. Peter Reichard, who read its files, found that from 1946 its expert Einar Hammarsten judged the DNA contaminated with protein; in 1952 the bacteriologist Malmgren judged the final proof missing, and in 1954 Hammarsten accepted DNA but thought the mechanism unknown. Avery died in 1955 without the prize. Wikipedia's article on Mirsky says he lobbied Stockholm against Avery; Reichard's account of the files names only the committee's own experts. A different experiment, at Cold Spring Harbor, convinced more biologists faster.
