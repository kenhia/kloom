On 28 February 1908 **[Reginald Punnett](kloom:e/reginald-punnett)**, William Bateson's collaborator at Cambridge, lectured on "Mendelism in relation to disease" to the **[Royal Society of Medicine](kloom:e/royal-society-of-medicine)** in London. In the discussion the statistician **[Udny Yule](kloom:e/udny-yule)**, a colleague of Karl Pearson's, raised **[brachydactyly](kloom:e/brachydactyly)**, shortened fingers, which was taken to be inherited as a dominant. If it were Mendelian, Yule said, one would expect "in the course of time" to get "three brachydactylous persons to one normal." There were nowhere near that many. Punnett heard the objection as a puzzle he could not answer: why does a dominant trait not spread through a population?

## The multiplication table

Back in Cambridge, Punnett put the question to a friend he played cricket with, the mathematician **[G. H. Hardy](kloom:e/g-h-hardy)** of **[Trinity College](kloom:e/trinity-college-cambridge)**. Hardy answered at once, and on 5 April wrote a short letter that the American journal _Science_ printed on 10 July 1908. Punnett said later that _Nature_ was then too hostile to Mendelism to take it. Hardy began:

> I am reluctant to intrude in a discussion concerning matters of which I have no expert knowledge, and I should have expected the very simple point which I wish to make to have been familiar to biologists. … Suppose that Aa is a pair of Mendelian characters, A being dominant, and that in any given generation the numbers of pure dominants (AA), heterozygotes (Aa), and pure recessives (aa) are as p:2q:r. Finally, suppose that the numbers are fairly large, so that the mating may be regarded as random, that the sexes are evenly distributed among the three varieties, and that all are equally fertile. A little mathematics of the multiplication-table type is enough to show that in the next generation the numbers will be as (p + q)²:2(p + q)(q + r):(q + r)², or as p₁:2q₁:r₁, say.

The new proportions are stable when _q_² = _pr_, and after one generation of random mating they always are. "In a word, there is not the slightest foundation for the idea that a dominant character should show a tendency to spread over a whole population, or that a recessive should tend to die out." Today the rule is written with the frequencies of the two forms of the [gene](kloom:e/gene), its **[alleles](kloom:e/allele)**: if _p_ is the share of A and _q_ the share of a, the genotypes come in proportions _p_², 2*pq* and _q_². The plate draws the three curves. In a worked example with invented numbers, mix two pure stocks, 300 people AA and 700 aa, so that _p_ = 0.3 and _q_ = 0.7:

| Generation             |  AA |  Aa |  aa | Show the dominant trait |
| ---------------------- | --: | --: | --: | ----------------------: |
| 0, two stocks mixed    | 300 |   0 | 700 |                     30% |
| 1, after random mating |  90 | 420 | 490 |                     51% |
| 2                      |  90 | 420 | 490 |                     51% |

The dominant trait rises once, as the hybrids appear, and then stands at 51 percent forever. Yule's three to one belongs only to a population in which the two alleles are equally common, as they were in Mendel's crosses of hybrid by hybrid.

## The doctor in Stuttgart

Six weeks before Punnett's lecture, on 13 January 1908, **[Wilhelm Weinberg](kloom:e/wilhelm-weinberg)**, a physician in **[Stuttgart](kloom:e/stuttgart)** who delivered some 3,500 babies in his career, had read a paper on proving heredity in humans to the natural history society of Württemberg. Mendel's ratios, he pointed out, show what happens under the strictest inbreeding, which people do not practice. Starting from proportions _m_ and _n_ of two pure types mating at random, he found the offspring in proportions _m_² AA + 2*mn* AB + _n_² BB, and the next generation the same: "We thus obtain under the influence of panmixia the same distribution of pure types and hybrids for each generation," in our translation. He used it to argue that a tendency to bear twins is inherited as a Mendelian recessive. He had got there first, but he wrote in German, and for thirty-five years the rule was Hardy's law. The geneticist Curt Stern drew attention to Weinberg in 1943, and it has been the **[Hardy–Weinberg principle](kloom:e/hardy-weinberg-principle)** since. Others had glimpsed the special case of two equally common alleles: Yule himself in 1902, Pearson in 1904.

## A null model

Hardy had named his conditions: a large population, random mating, equal fertility, and a trait that does not depend on sex. Add no migration and no new mutation, and the proportions never change. That is the principle's use. It says what a population does when nothing acts on it, so any departure measures something that does act: selection, inbreeding, a population mixed from two. In 1924 Felix Bernstein used it on census counts of the blood groups, which showed that ABO is one gene with three alleles and not two genes. Hardy himself, asked by Punnett in 1917, worked out how slowly selection removes a rare recessive. From such sums grew **[population genetics](kloom:e/population-genetics)**, the mathematics of genes in whole populations, which the modern synthesis would turn on evolution.

Hardy was thirty-one, and that year published _A Course of Pure Mathematics_. He thought little of applications, and wrote in **[_A Mathematician's Apology_](kloom:e/a-mathematicians-apology)** in 1940: "I have never done anything 'useful'." His one note on genetics became what the geneticist James Crow called "the cornerstone of diploid population genetics." Meanwhile at Columbia, Thomas Hunt Morgan's flies were about to show where the genes lie.
