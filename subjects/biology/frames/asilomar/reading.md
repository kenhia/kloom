In July 1974 eleven scientists published a page and a half in three journals at once, asking their colleagues to stop. The letter, "Potential Biohazards of Recombinant DNA Molecules," came from a National Academy of Sciences committee chaired by **[Paul Berg](kloom:e/paul-berg)**, with **[David Baltimore](kloom:e/david-baltimore)**, **[Herbert Boyer](kloom:e/herbert-boyer)**, **[Stanley Cohen](kloom:e/stanley-norman-cohen)**, **[Daniel Nathans](kloom:e/daniel-nathans)**, **[James Watson](kloom:e/james-watson)** and five others. It asked scientists everywhere to "join with the members of this committee in voluntarily deferring" two kinds of work: putting genes for antibiotic resistance or bacterial toxins into bacteria that lacked them, and joining the DNA of tumor viruses to **[plasmids](kloom:e/plasmid)** or other DNA that copies itself. It asked the **[National Institutes of Health](kloom:e/national-institutes-of-health)** for an advisory committee, and called for "an international meeting of involved scientists from all over the world" early the following year.

That meeting was the **[Asilomar Conference on Recombinant DNA](kloom:e/asilomar-conference-on-recombinant-dna)**, held among the dunes at Pacific Grove, on California's Monterey peninsula, from 24 to 27 February 1975. It is the most cited example of a science stopping itself to decide how it would go on, and has been praised and attacked ever since for the same reasons.

## Who came, and what they argued about

The organizing committee's own report counts 155 participants: 83 from institutions in the United States, 51 from other countries, and 21 from the lay and scientific press, more than a tenth of the room. (Accounts since, Wikipedia's among them, say "about 140" professionals, which is the committee's count less the press.) Invitations went to those doing the work, or planning it, and to people who could speak to the risks. Nothing was published as proceedings: the sessions were taped, and the seven reels were to be kept for the archive and not copied.

The program ran through the ecology of plasmids and gut bacteria, recombinants made with animal viruses, recombinants made with the DNA of higher organisms, and, on the third evening, the legal and ethical questions. A provisional statement drafted that night was argued over clause by clause on the last morning, the participants voting by show of hands on each amendment.

## Containment as the answer

What the conference agreed was a principle rather than a rule: "that containment be made an essential consideration in the experimental design," and "that the effectiveness of the containment should match, as closely as possible, the estimated risk." Experiments were sorted into bands of risk, each with its containment, and a few left alone:

| Level    | What it meant                                                                                                    |
| -------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Minimal  | The practice of an ordinary clinical microbiology laboratory: no eating or drinking, coats, mechanical pipetting |
| Low      | The above, with access limited and a safety cabinet for anything likely to make an aerosol                       |
| Moderate | Transfers in a safety cabinet, gloves, filtered vacuum lines, negative pressure in a limited-access laboratory   |
| High     | Air locks, a change of clothes and a shower on leaving, and exhaust air and wastes treated before they leave     |
| Deferred | DNA from the most dangerous pathogens, DNA carrying toxin genes, and anything above ten liters of culture        |

Beside physical containment the conference put a second kind, which it thought the more important: _biological_ containment, hosts and carriers crippled on purpose, "fastidious bacterial hosts unable to survive in natural environments." Making such strains was the task the meeting set, and the participants agreed that improved ones "will be made freely available to all interested investigators."

The NIH turned the scheme into rules. Its guidelines, issued in June 1976 and printed in the _Federal Register_ that July, named four levels of physical containment, P1 to P4, and three of biological containment, EK1 to EK3 — the letters for [_E. coli_](kloom:e/escherichia-coli) K-12, the laboratory strain — with a measurable bar for the middle one: no more than one cell in a hundred million should keep the carrier or its insert alive outside the laboratory. Those four levels are the ancestor of today's [biosafety levels](kloom:e/biosafety-level), BSL-1 to BSL-4, set out under that name by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention and the NIH in 1984.

## Cambridge says no, then yes

Not everyone accepted that scientists should write their own rules. In [Cambridge, Massachusetts](kloom:e/cambridge-massachusetts), where [Harvard](kloom:e/harvard-university) was building a laboratory, the mayor **[Alfred Vellucci](kloom:e/alfred-vellucci)** called public hearings in the summer of 1976, and the city council voted a three-month halt, later extended. The city manager appointed a review board of eight residents — a health commissioner, a nurse, a structural engineer, two former city councillors, a physician, a nun and a businessman — and no scientist from either university, on the ground that "it would be extremely difficult to find knowledgeable scientists who did not have preconceived views on the subject." It reported in December that the work could go on under the NIH rules with extra conditions. Ordinance 874 passed on 7 February 1977, nine votes to none: it bound the universities to the NIH guidelines, required the strictest available biological containment at the moderate physical level, barred anything needing more, demanded a community member on each institution's biohazards committee, and set a fine of $200 a day.

The fears faded. Berg, writing thirty years later, said no hazard to public health had ever been traced to the technology, and that the conference's achievement was trust: scientists raised the alarm themselves, in front of the press, "rather than having it raised against them." The criticisms have not. The participants were almost all from rich countries and nearly all stood to gain from a permissive answer; Senator Edward Kennedy said they "were making public policy. And they were making it in private"; and the agenda was confined to laboratory safety, leaving the technology's use, ownership and application to people for later. Berg accepted the last charge and defended it: those questions "were still far in the future."

Asilomar has been invoked at every alarm since: by the scientists who met at Napa in 2015 over editing the human germ line, by an AI meeting at the same grounds in 2017, and by the AI laboratories whose safety levels are modeled, they say, on the biosafety levels this argument produced. The next frames follow the technology itself.
