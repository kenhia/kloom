**[Frederick Sanger](kloom:e/frederick-sanger)** spent his working life asking what order the units of a long molecule come in. He read the amino acid sequence of **[insulin](kloom:e/insulin)** by 1955, the first protein whose order was known, and won the [Nobel Prize in Chemistry](kloom:e/nobel-prize-in-chemistry) for it in 1958. Then he turned to the nucleic acids, "following my work on amino acid sequences in proteins," as his second Nobel lecture puts it. In December 1977, from the **[MRC Laboratory of Molecular Biology](kloom:e/mrc-laboratory-of-molecular-biology)** in Cambridge, he and his colleagues S. Nicklen and A. R. Coulson published three and a half pages in the _Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences_ titled "DNA sequencing with chain-terminating inhibitors." The method it describes, **[Sanger sequencing](kloom:e/sanger-sequencing)**, read most of the DNA sequenced anywhere in the world for the next thirty years, and all of the first human genome.

## Stopping the copy on purpose

The idea rests on a flaw deliberately introduced into a building block. **DNA** is copied by a polymerase, which adds one unit at a time to a growing strand, each pairing with a unit of the template being copied. Every unit added must offer a hydroxyl group at its 3′ position for the next one to attach to. A **[dideoxynucleotide](kloom:e/dideoxynucleotide)** lacks it. A polymerase will take one up as though it were ordinary, and the strand then stops there, for good.

Sanger's four reactions each held an ordinary supply of all four units, a radioactive label, and a trace of one dideoxy version — about one part in a hundred, the paper says. The copies made in that reaction therefore stopped, at random, at one or another of the places where that one base belongs, and the reaction ended as a mixture of strands of every such length. Run side by side in four lanes down a gel, the strands sort by length, the shortest fastest. An X-ray film laid on the gel shows them as a ladder of bands, and the sequence is read from the bottom upward, taking each rung from the lane it appears in. An invented example, to show the reading:

| Position from the bottom | 1   | 2   | 3   | 4   | 5   | 6   | 7   | 8   |
| ------------------------ | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Band in lane             | G   | A   | T   | T   | A   | C   | A   | G   |

The sequence of the new strand is GATTACAG, and the template is its complement. The plate draws such a gel, with the four lanes, the ladder of bands and the reading arrow.

Sanger's earlier "plus and minus" method of 1975 had given him most of the sequence of a bacterial virus. The dideoxy method was faster and more accurate, and left fewer artifacts; where the old one made the reader guess how many units a run of identical bases held, the new one showed them as separate bands. Its weaknesses were honest ones: 15 to about 200 units could be read from one starting primer, sometimes 300; the first bases after the primer were unreadable; and bands "piled up" where the strand folded back on itself. "One should not rely completely on single results obtained by this method alone," the paper says; the sequence of each stretch was read on both strands.

## The first genome, and the rival

That same year Sanger's group published the whole genome of **[ΦX174](kloom:e/phi-x-174)**, a virus that infects [_E. coli_](kloom:e/escherichia-coli): 5,386 units, the first DNA genome ever read, done with the older method and finished with the new one. Reading it brought a surprise nobody had predicted. Some stretches code for two different proteins at once, read in different frames — the gene for one protein sitting wholly inside the gene for another. In a related virus a four-unit stretch was found doing duty in all three of its possible frames.

A second method arrived in the same year, from **[Allan Maxam](kloom:e/allan-maxam)** and **[Walter Gilbert](kloom:e/walter-gilbert)** at Harvard: chemical reactions that break a labeled strand preferentially at each kind of base, read on a gel the same way. It had the advantage of working on double-stranded DNA directly. It was also more laborious, and it used hydrazine; within a few years almost everyone used Sanger's. The 1980 Nobel Prize in Chemistry went half to **[Paul Berg](kloom:e/paul-berg)** "for his fundamental studies of the biochemistry of nucleic acids, with particular regard to recombinant-DNA," and half jointly to Gilbert and Sanger "for their contributions concerning the determination of base sequences in nucleic acids." It was Sanger's second; he remains one of very few people to have won two.

Sanger's laboratory went on to read human [mitochondrial DNA](kloom:e/mitochondrial-dna), 16,569 units, and the genome of a larger virus, 48,502. He retired in 1983, and described himself as "just a chap who messed about in a lab."

## Four colors

What made the method industrial was automation. In 1986 Lloyd Smith, [Leroy Hood](kloom:e/leroy-hood) and their colleagues at Caltech reported attaching a fluorescent dye to the primer, "a different coloured fluorophore for each of the reactions specific for the bases A, C, G and T," so that all four could run down one lane and be read as they passed a detector, "acquired directly by computer." Applied Biosystems sold the first such machine in 1987. The dyes later moved onto the chain terminators themselves, so one reaction sufficed, and the gel slab gave way to fine capillaries. The ladder and the X-ray film became a trace of four colored peaks, but the chemistry under them was still Sanger's.

The genomes that followed, and the machines that overtook this one, are later frames'.
