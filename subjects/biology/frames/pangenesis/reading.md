Natural selection works on differences that are passed from parent to child, and in 1859 **[Charles Darwin](kloom:e/charles-darwin)** could not say how they were passed. Nobody could. In January 1868, in the second volume of **[_The Variation of Animals and Plants under Domestication_](kloom:e/the-variation-of-animals-and-plants-under-domestication)**, he offered what he called a "provisional hypothesis or speculation", and gave it a name: **[pangenesis](kloom:e/pangenesis)**, from the Greek for "origin from all". He had held it back for years. In 1865 he sent a draft to **[Thomas Henry Huxley](kloom:e/thomas-henry-huxley)**, who doubted it but told him to publish all the same: "Somebody rummaging among your papers half a century hence will find Pangenesis & say 'See this wonderful anticipation of our modern Theories—and that stupid ass, Huxley, prevented his publishing them'".

## Swamped

The usual answer to heredity was that a child is a blend of its parents, as paint is of two colors. In June 1867 the engineer **[Fleeming Jenkin](kloom:e/fleeming-jenkin)** showed in the _North British Review_ what **[blending inheritance](kloom:e/blending-inheritance)** would do to Darwin's theory. A single favorable "sport" would breed with ordinary mates, its advantage halved in its children and halved again in theirs, so that "the sport will be swamped by numbers, and after a few generations its peculiarity will be obliterated." He drove the point home with a parable that took white superiority for granted: a white castaway made king of an island of black people, who has many children and still, Jenkin argued, cannot make its people white. **[Gregor Mendel](kloom:e/gregor-mendel)** had published in 1866 the experiments that answered Jenkin, but neither man read them.

## Gemmules

Darwin's answer was a particle. Every cell of the body, he supposed, throws off "minute granules or atoms, which circulate freely throughout the system", multiply, and gather in the buds and the sexual elements:

> These granules for the sake of distinctness may be called cell-gemmules, or, as the cellular theory is not fully established, simply gemmules. They are supposed to be transmitted from the parents to the offspring, and are generally developed in the generation which immediately succeeds, but are often transmitted in a dormant state during many generations and are then developed.

Dormant gemmules explained a grandparent's feature reappearing. A part changed by use or by the climate would throw off changed gemmules, so its changes were inherited, as **[Jean-Baptiste Lamarck](kloom:e/jean-baptiste-lamarck)** had held. "Each living creature," Darwin wrote, "must be looked at as a microcosm—a little universe, formed of a host of self-propagating organisms, inconceivably minute and as numerous as the stars in heaven." Unlike the preformed germs of Charles Bonnet's emboîtement, which he set aside as "exploded", gemmules were made afresh in every generation.

## Galton's rabbits

If gemmules swarmed in the blood, the blood of one breed put into another should show in its young. Darwin's half-cousin **[Francis Galton](kloom:e/francis-galton)** set out to test it at the end of 1869, with the operations done by the prosector of the **[Zoological Society of London](kloom:e/zoological-society-of-london)** in its gardens. Transfusion, he noted, had been used with success "in midwifery practice". Under anesthetic, silver-gray rabbits, a breed that bred true, were bled and given the blood of yellow, gray and black-and-white rabbits; in the boldest series the carotid arteries of a silver-gray and another rabbit were joined crosswise, for up to 37½ minutes, so that each poured its blood into the other. Most pairs given the largest transfusions failed to breed, and one doe died after the cross-circulation. Then he bred them:

| Operation on the silver-grays            | Galton's estimate of foreign blood in the young | Young born | True silver-grays                    |
| ---------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------- | ---------- | ------------------------------------ |
| Moderate transfusion of both parents     | about ⅛                                         | 30         | 30, one perhaps not: a white foreleg |
| A second, larger transfusion of the buck | about ¼                                         | 6          | 6                                    |
| Cross-circulation of the buck only       | ¼ to ⅓                                          | 13         | 13                                   |
| Cross-circulation of both parents        | fully ½                                         | 37         | 36, and one the breed throws anyway  |
| Common rabbits joined to silver-grays    | a little under ½ silver-gray                    | 38         | none                                 |

"In no single case has there been any evidence of alteration of breed," he told the Royal Society on 30 March 1871: pangenesis, as he understood it, was "incorrect". Darwin answered in _Nature_ on 27 April that he had "not said one word about the blood", and that Galton's conclusion seemed "a little hasty", though pangenesis, "from presenting so many vulnerable points", had a life "always in jeopardy".

## Tails and the germ line

**[August Weismann](kloom:e/august-weismann)**, at Freiburg, struck at the root. Heredity, he argued from 1883, runs through "the continuity of the substance of the germ-cells, or germ-plasm", which the body grows from but never sends back to. From October 1887 he cut off the tails of white mice and bred from them, cutting the tails of each new generation's breeders in turn, and counted the young by 16 January 1889:

| Generation with tails cut | Breeders  | Young born | Tails shorter than normal |
| ------------------------- | --------- | ---------- | ------------------------- |
| 1                         | 12        | 333        | 0                         |
| 2                         | 15        | 237        | 0                         |
| 3                         | 14        | 152        | 0                         |
| 4                         | not given | 138        | 0                         |
| 5                         | 13        | 41         | 0                         |

All 901 had tails of 10.5 millimeters or more. Weismann admitted that mice alone proved little, since an effect might take a thousand generations; and critics have noted since that cutting a tail is an injury, not the use or disuse Lamarck meant. His **[germ plasm](kloom:e/germ-plasm)**, the hereditary substance kept apart from the body, was the stronger blow.

The word outlived the theory. In 1889 **[Hugo de Vries](kloom:e/hugo-de-vries)** kept Darwin's particles inside the cell and called them pangenes, and in 1909 the Danish botanist Wilhelm Johannsen cut that to its last syllable: the **[gene](kloom:e/gene)**. The next wrong turn on this trail was not an honest one.
