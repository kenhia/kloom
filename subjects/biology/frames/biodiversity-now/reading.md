The word is younger than most of its readers. In September 1986 the National Academy of Sciences and the Smithsonian Institution held a National Forum on BioDiversity in Washington, and **[E. O. Wilson](kloom:e/e-o-wilson)**, editing its papers, credited the word to the forum's organizer, Walter G. Rosen, who "introduced the term." It meant the variety of life at every level, from genes to ecosystems. As of October 6, 2026, **[biodiversity](kloom:e/biodiversity)** is measured more closely than ever, and there is still no agreed answer to the first question, how much of it there is.

## How many species?

The **[Catalogue of Life](kloom:e/catalogue-of-life)**, the shared checklist of the world's taxonomists, listed 2,269,433 accepted species in its release of September 11, 2026, of which 171,616 are known only as fossils: by our arithmetic, about 2.1 million living species have been described. It is a fraction. In 2011 Camilo Mora and four colleagues at Dalhousie University estimated the rest from the shape of classification itself. The numbers of phyla, classes, orders, families and genera are far better known than the number of species, and in well-studied groups they rise from rank to rank in a regular curve. Fitted for each kingdom and run on to the species rank, the curve gave "≈8.7 million (±1.3 million SE) eukaryotic species globally," of which some 86 percent on land and 91 percent in the sea "still await description." They opened with a question from the ecologist **[Robert May](kloom:e/robert-may-baron-may-of-oxford)**: if aliens visited, they would ask how many species Earth has, and we would be "embarrassed" by the answer. Bacteria and archaea are another matter: one estimate from the scaling of commonness and rarity, by Kenneth Locey and Jay Lennon in 2016, gave "upward of 1 trillion."

## Measuring a loss

[Extinctions](kloom:e/extinction) are counted in extinctions per million species-years (E/MSY): among a million species, one extinction a year is a rate of 1. From fossils and family trees of living groups, Jurriaan de Vos, **[Stuart Pimm](kloom:e/stuart-pimm)** and colleagues put the natural background rate at about 0.1 in 2014, and the present rate at about a thousand times that. In 2015 **[Gerardo Ceballos](kloom:e/gerardo-ceballos)** and colleagues took a background of 2, twice the old benchmark, to be safe, and still found the vertebrate extinctions of the last century would have taken 800 to 10,000 years at that pace.

Most species cannot be watched until they vanish, so the **[IUCN Red List](kloom:e/iucn-red-list)**, kept since 1964, judges the risk instead, against fixed criteria:

| Category              | One of its tests (criterion E, or the smallest population) |
| --------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------- |
| Critically Endangered | At least a 50% chance of extinction in 10 years            |
| Endangered            | At least a 20% chance of extinction in 20 years            |
| Vulnerable            | At least a 10% chance in 100 years, or under 1,000 adults  |

The three together are "threatened." Version 2026-1, released on July 9, 2026, has assessed 175,909 species, by our arithmetic 8 percent of the 2,121,262 described species it counts, and 49,505 of them are threatened. Only some groups have been assessed nearly whole:

Insects, perhaps most animal species, are 1.4 percent assessed. Abundance is tracked separately: WWF and the Zoological Society of London's **[Living Planet Index](kloom:e/living-planet-index)** found in 2024 that 34,836 monitored vertebrate populations had fallen by 73 percent on average since 1970. Critics question the average, not the decline: Brian Leung and colleagues found in 2020 that less than 3 percent of populations drove it, and Falko Buschke's group that random fluctuations alone exaggerate it by 9.6 percent.

## Why, and what was promised

In 2019 the global assessment of **[IPBES](kloom:e/intergovernmental-science-policy-platform-on-biodiversity-and-ecosystem-services)**, the intergovernmental panel for biodiversity, ranked the direct drivers "starting with those with most impact: changes in land and sea use; direct exploitation of organisms; climate change; pollution; and invasion of alien species." Three-quarters of the land surface had been "significantly altered." It used the **[species–area relationship](kloom:e/species-area-relationship)** too, as the plate does: habitat 30 percent degraded leaves "around 9 per cent" of 5.9 million land species without enough to survive. By our arithmetic that is a curve of slope 0.26. **[Climate change](kloom:e/climate-change)**, third on the list, is driven by the carbon dioxide the Mauna Loa record traces.

In December 2022 the parties to the **[Convention on Biological Diversity](kloom:e/convention-on-biological-diversity)** adopted the **[Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework](kloom:e/kunming-montreal-global-biodiversity-framework)**: 23 targets for 2030, among them protecting 30 percent of land and sea, and a goal of cutting extinction rates tenfold by 2050. In October 2026 protected areas cover 17.6 percent of land and 9.8 percent of the ocean. The talks in Cali in 2024 ran so late that delegates left for their flights and the meeting lost its quorum, so it finished in Rome in February 2025; the first review of progress opens in Yerevan on October 19, 2026.

## A sixth mass extinction?

The fossil record's five **[mass extinctions](kloom:e/extinction-event)** each took more than three-quarters of species in a geologically short time. In 2011 Anthony Barnosky and colleagues concluded that today's losses were not yet on that scale, though losing the species now critically endangered would bring it close. Ceballos's group held in 2015 that a sixth "is already under way," and Robert Cowie and colleagues estimated in 2022 that 7.5 to 13 percent of known species, mostly unrecorded invertebrates, may already be gone since 1500. In 2025 John Wiens and Kristen Saban answered that recorded extinctions were mostly of island species, most often to invasive species, and have slowed over the last century, and warned against projecting them forward. Neither side doubts that species are being lost faster than the background rate. They disagree about the name, and about what the count Aristotle began on Lesbos will look like at its end.
