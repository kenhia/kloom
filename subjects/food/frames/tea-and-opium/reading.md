China had drunk **[tea](kloom:e/tea)** for well over a thousand years; in the eighth century Lu Yu's _Classic of Tea_ set out how the leaves were grown, cured and brewed. The English came to it late. It was known in London by 1660, and in 1670, by one count, England imported 79 pounds of it. By 1839 the missionary monthly _The Chinese Repository_, published in Canton, reckoned that England drank nearly two pounds a head a year. Its table of tea used in England is the chart; the steep rise after 1784 is the Commutation Act, which cut the duty from 119 percent to 12.5 and killed the smuggling trade.

Almost all of it came from one port. Under the **[Canton System](kloom:e/canton-system)** of 1757 the **[Qing dynasty](kloom:e/qing-dynasty)** confined Western ships to Canton (Guangzhou), and from 1760 foreigners could buy only from a guild of licensed Chinese merchants, the **[Cohong](kloom:e/cohong)**. On the British side the **[East India Company](kloom:e/east-india-company)** held the monopoly of the China trade until 1834. Its warehouses filled with tea, and in 1773 Parliament's Tea Act let it ship its surplus straight to the American colonies, where the patriots of Boston threw it into the harbor.

## The triangle

China wanted little that Britain made, so for a century British merchants paid for tea in silver. Then they found something China would buy. The Company ruled Bengal, and there it made **[opium](kloom:e/opium)** a monopoly. Farmers grew the poppy under its license, some of them compelled to; its agents lanced the pods, its factories, such as the one at Patna, dried the gum into balls and packed them in chests, and it sold the chests at auction in **[Calcutta](kloom:e/kolkata)**. The Qing had banned the drug in 1796, so the Company carried none to China itself. Private merchants bought at the auction and shipped the chests to hulks moored off the Pearl River, where Chinese dealers in fast, armed boats took them inland and paid in silver. The silver bought tea. British factory cloth, meanwhile, went to India. The plate draws the circuit.

Imports rose from about 4,000 chests a year in 1800–18 to nearly 20,000 by 1831, and doubled again after the Company's monopoly ended. In July 1839 _The Chinese Repository_ set the two trades side by side:

| Season | Tea bought, British and American ships | Opium sold to the Chinese |
| ------ | -------------------------------------- | ------------------------- |
| 1837   | $19,928,052 for 56.8 million pounds    | $13,554,030               |
| 1838   | $13,535,026 for 52.2 million pounds    | $19,727,259               |

By 1838 opium paid for the tea and more. Silver flowed out of China, and its price in the copper coin of everyday trade rose. Historians have long blamed opium for that drain and the depression that followed; the economic historian Alejandra Irigoin argues the reverse, that a flight of silver driven by a shortage of trusted Spanish dollars came first, and opium followed.

## Lin Zexu at Humen

The **[Daoguang Emperor](kloom:e/daoguang-emperor)** rejected proposals to legalize the drug, and in 1839 sent the scholar-official **[Lin Zexu](kloom:e/lin-zexu)** to Canton to end the trade. Lin besieged the foreign merchants in their factories until the British superintendent, Charles Elliot, had them surrender 20,283 chests. From 3 June Lin destroyed them at Humen, near the mouth of the river, in the open, so that, as the emperor commanded, natives and foreigners "both alike might hear of it, and see it". The American missionary Elijah Bridgman watched and described the method:

| Step  | At Humen, June 1839                                                                            |
| ----- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Dig   | Three trenches about 150 feet long, 75 broad and 7 deep, floored with stone, lined with timber |
| Flood | Two feet of fresh water let in from the hill (later accounts say sea water)                    |
| Break | Coolies on planks stamp each ball of opium to pieces with the heel and kick it in              |
| Kill  | Salt and lime spread over the surface while others hoe and stir from the bottom                |
| Flush | The vat let out through a screened sluice into the creek, to the sea                           |

Up to 1,300 chests went in a day; one man caught carrying off a few pieces was executed. Lin also wrote to **[Queen Victoria](kloom:e/queen-victoria)**. In the translation printed at Canton, he asked her to "suppose that foreigners came from another country, and brought opium into England, and seduced the people of your country to smoke it", and whether she would not then "look upon such a procedure with anger". The letter never reached her.

## War

In July 1839 British sailors killed a villager, Lin Weixi, at Kowloon, and Elliot would not hand them over. Fighting began that autumn, and in June 1840 a British expedition arrived, with Indian troops and the Company's steamship _Nemesis_. The **[First Opium War](kloom:e/first-opium-war)** ended under the guns of Nanking. The **[Treaty of Nanking](kloom:e/treaty-of-nanking)**, signed on 29 August 1842, opened five ports, abolished the Cohong, ceded **[Hong Kong](kloom:e/hong-kong)** Island "in perpetuity", and charged China 21 million dollars, six million of it "as the value of the opium". About 3,100 Chinese soldiers were killed, by one estimate, and 87 British in battle, though many more British died of disease. The treaty neither legalized the opium trade nor forbade it, and the trade went on.

## Tea without China

Britain set out to grow its own tea. The Scottish botanist **[Robert Fortune](kloom:e/robert-fortune)**, sent by the Company in 1848, traveled inland in Chinese dress, bought plants and seed, and shipped them in sealed glass **Wardian cases**, sowing seeds among the plants so that they sprouted on the voyage. By 1851 he had sent "upwards of twenty thousand tea-plants, eight first-rate manufacturers, and a large supply of implements" to the government's tea plantations in the Himalayas. The larger harvest came from a native plant: **[Assam tea](kloom:e/assam-tea)**, first sold in London in January 1839, the year of Humen, and grown by companies whose laborers, indentured and recruited from famine districts, died of disease in large numbers.
