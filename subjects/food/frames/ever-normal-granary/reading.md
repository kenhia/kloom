Under **[Emperor Xuan](kloom:e/emperor-xuan-of-han)** of the **[Han dynasty](kloom:e/han-dynasty)**, in the 50s BC, the harvests were so good that grain fell to five cash a _shi_ and farmers could not live on what it fetched. An official of the Grand Ministry of Agriculture named Geng Shouchang, whom the **[Book of Han](kloom:e/book-of-han)** describes as good at reckoning, made two proposals. (In the telling of [Liu Hui](kloom:e/liu-hui), the third-century commentator, Geng also restored the text of the [_Nine Chapters on the Mathematical Art_](kloom:e/the-nine-chapters-on-the-mathematical-art), a book full of problems about grain and fields.) The first, to buy the capital's grain nearby instead of shipping four million _shi_ a year from the east with 60,000 conscripts, worked. The second, in 54 BC, is the subject of this frame. In our translation of the Book of Han:

> Shouchang then proposed that the border commanderies all build granaries. When grain was cheap, they were to raise the price and buy, to benefit the farmers; when grain was dear, they were to lower the price and sell. They were called ever-normal granaries. The people found them convenient.

The emperor made Geng a marquis. The **[ever-normal granary](kloom:e/buffer-stock-scheme)**, _changpingcang_, is the oldest working example of what economists call a buffer stock scheme. The plate draws the rule as a band of prices, with the store rising and falling in turn.

## Older than the granary

The rule itself was centuries old. The Book of Han credits it to **[Li Kui](kloom:e/li-kui-legalist)**, a minister of the state of Wei in the fifth century BC, who argued that grain too dear harms those who eat it and grain too cheap harms those who grow it: "the bad results of a high price and a low price are the same". He worked it out for a household of five farming 100 _mu_, which in an ordinary year reaped 150 _shi_. In Chen Huan-chang's translation of 1911, with his acres and bushels given as _mu_ and _shi_:

| The year               | Harvest of 100 _mu_ | What the state does                         |
| ---------------------- | ------------------: | ------------------------------------------- |
| Best of the good years |           600 _shi_ | buys 300                                    |
| Middling good year     |           450 _shi_ | buys 200                                    |
| Least good year        |           300 _shi_ | buys 50                                     |
| Ordinary year          |           150 _shi_ | nothing                                     |
| Small famine           |           100 _shi_ | sells what it bought in the least good year |
| Middling famine        |            70 _shi_ | sells what it bought in the middling year   |
| Great famine           |            30 _shi_ | sells what it bought in the best year       |

"Therefore," Li Kui concluded, "even if famine, flood and drought should occur, the price of grain would not be high." The **[Guanzi](kloom:e/guanzi-text)**, a Han compilation of older economic writing, made the same case for state buying and selling.

## Abolished and revived

Geng had critics. The imperial counselor Xiao Wangzhi warned that building granaries and boats would cost more than 200 million cash, and that Geng, in our translation, was "practiced in reckoning labor and fractions, but his far-reaching plans cannot be trusted". After **[Emperor Yuan of Han](kloom:e/emperor-yuan-of-han)** came to the throne in 48 BC, floods and famine struck the east, and grain rose above 300 cash a _shi_; the Confucian scholars at court argued that the salt and iron monopolies and the ever-normal granaries should go, "so as not to compete with the people for profit", and the emperor abolished them all. Historians differ on what Geng had built. Chen read it as the first true price-stabilizing granary. R. Bin Wong, in _Nourish the People_ (1991), notes that the Han granaries of that name supplied troops on the frontier, and that granaries balancing harvests in the later manner first appear under the Jin dynasty (265–420). The name returned under the Jin, Sui, Tang and Song.

## The Qing system

The **[Qing dynasty](kloom:e/qing-dynasty)** built the largest. In _Nourish the People_, Pierre-Étienne Will and Wong reconstruct it from the archives: ever-normal granaries in every county, holding a national total of some thirty million _shi_ by the mid-eighteenth century and a recorded peak of more than forty-five million in the 1790s, though the authors doubt the stocks ever truly reached that. Each spring, when prices were highest before the harvest, a granary typically sold 30 percent of its grain below the market price, lent more to farmers, and bought back after the harvest, so the stock was turned over before it spoiled. The system frayed from the 1780s: the prices the Board of Revenue let magistrates pay to restock fell behind the market, and some officials forced farmers to sell to them cheaply. An investigation of 1835 found eighteen million _shi_ missing, against some twenty-four million in the granaries.

## Wallace's granary

In the 1920s an Iowa farm editor, **[Henry A. Wallace](kloom:e/henry-a-wallace)**, read Chen's book, his doctoral dissertation at **[Columbia University](kloom:e/columbia-university)**, and took from it what he called the "ever-normal granary". As Secretary of Agriculture from 1933, at the head of the **[United States Department of Agriculture](kloom:e/united-states-department-of-agriculture)**, he made it his program after the drought of 1934, and the **[Agricultural Adjustment Act of 1938](kloom:e/agricultural-adjustment-act-of-1938)** enacted it: "storage of reserve supplies, loans, marketing quotas", and "assisting consumers to obtain an adequate and steady supply of such commodities at fair prices". The **[Commodity Credit Corporation](kloom:e/commodity-credit-corporation)** lent farmers money on corn stored under seal, at 75 percent of the "parity" price in an ordinary year, and on a sliding scale down to 52 percent in a crop more than a quarter above normal, so the state held back a glut as Li Kui's had. The historian Derk Bodde wrote to Wallace in 1945 to ask where the idea came from, and published the answer in 1946.

A granary steadies prices only while it holds grain and its keepers keep the rules. When either failed, in the famine of Emperor Yuan's reign or the audit of 1835, the band broke.
