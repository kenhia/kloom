Around the year 800 **[Charlemagne](kloom:e/charlemagne)** wanted to know what his farms produced. A decree on the running of the royal estates, the **[Capitulare de villis](kloom:e/capitulare-de-villis)**, told each steward to send in, every Christmas, "an annual statement of all our income, giving an account of our lands cultivated by the oxen which our own plowmen drive and of our lands which the tenants of farms ought to plow," then of mills, vineyards, honey and eggs, "so that we may know what and how much of each thing we have" (in James Harvey Robinson's translation of 1904). This was the manor: the lord's own land, the demesne, worked partly by his tenants' labor.

The monks of Saint-Germain-des-Prés, near Paris, counted the same way. The **[Polyptych of Irminon](kloom:e/polyptych-of-irminon)**, a survey made around 823 under their abbot, names more than 10,000 people on 25 of the abbey's estates. At Villeneuve-Saint-Georges it lists a tenant family, Arctardus and his wife Eligildis, with six children:

| They held                               | They owed                                                         |
| --------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 5 bonniers and 1 arpent of plowland     | 4 solidi in silver one year, 2 the next, a ewe and lamb the third |
| 4 arpents of vineyard                   | 2 muids of wine for grazing, 50 shingles, wood dues               |
| 4½ arpents of meadow                    | Plowing 4 perches of the abbey's land at Martinmas, and 2 more    |
| A free holding, one of 60 on the estate | Labor services "as many as are imposed," 3 chickens and 15 eggs   |

## Turning the furrow

The older plow of the Mediterranean, the **[ard](kloom:e/ard-plough)**, drags a point through the soil and leaves the earth between its furrows untouched, so fields were plowed twice, the second time across the first, and tended to be square. The heavy plow of the north, the **[carruca](kloom:e/carruca)**, did more. A knife-like coulter cut down through the sod, an iron share cut it free underneath, and a slanting board, the moldboard, laid the slice over on its back, burying weeds and opening wet clay to drain. Wheels in front carried the beam and set the depth. It took a big team: the **[Domesday Book](kloom:e/domesday-book)** of 1086 reckoned a plow team at eight oxen. In China iron moldboards were in use from the Han, drawn by two animals or one; Europe's moldboards were mostly wooden and straight, and heavy to pull.

## Strips, furlongs and ridges

A team that long was slow to turn, so the land was laid out to turn it seldom. Under the **[open-field system](kloom:e/open-field-system)** each village had two or three great unfenced fields, divided into furlongs and those into long, narrow strips. A family's twenty acres might lie in seventy strips scattered across the fields, a share of good and bad land that also spread the risk of a bad year. The furlong was a "furrow-long", 220 yards. **[Walter of Henley](kloom:e/walter-of-henley)**, writing his _Husbandry_ around 1280, worked out an acre as 40 perches long and 4 wide, plowed in 36 rounds, or 72 furlongs of walking. By our arithmetic each slice was about 11 inches wide.

The plow threw its soil one way, so a plowman worked round each strip clockwise, and year after year the earth moved toward its middle. The strips rose into the **[ridge and furrow](kloom:e/ridge-and-furrow)** still seen in English pasture, ridges that shed water into the furrows between them.

Rotation kept the land in heart. In a two-field village half lay fallow each year. In the **[three-field system](kloom:e/three-field-system)**, one field was sown with wheat or rye in autumn, one with barley, oats, peas or beans in spring, and one rested and was grazed and manured by the village's beasts. Walter's sums for an acre of wheat show what was at stake:

| Cost of an acre of wheat                     | Pence |
| -------------------------------------------- | ----- |
| Three plowings at 6d                         | 18    |
| Harrowing                                    | 1     |
| Seed, two bushels                            | 12    |
| Weeding, reaping, carrying                   | 6½    |
| Total                                        | 37½   |
| Harvest at three times the seed, six bushels | 36    |

"If the return of your grange only yields three times the seed sown," he warned, "you will gain nothing unless corn sells well." An anonymous _Husbandry_ of the same age expected wheat to return five times its seed, barley eight and oats four. P. F. Brandon's study of Battle Abbey's accounts in the later fourteenth century found an average of 4.34 for wheat, 4.01 for barley and 2.87 for oats.

The grain went to the lord's mill, and the miller kept a share. Domesday counted 5,624 water mills in England, and later counts run past 6,000. The earliest certain record of a **[windmill](kloom:e/windmill)** in northern Europe comes from Weedley, in Yorkshire, in 1185; four centuries later the Dutch geared one to a crankshaft to saw timber.

## A plow that made Europe?

In _Medieval Technology and Social Change_ (1962) the historian **[Lynn White](kloom:e/lynn-townsend-white-jr)** argued that the heavy plow, which few peasants could team alone, pushed villages to pool their oxen and farm in common, and that with the three-field rotation it moved Europe's center of wealth north. Many reviewers were hostile: P. H. Sawyer and R. H. Hilton called it "a chain of obscure and dubious deductions from scanty evidence."

The weeds have since been asked. Weeds that flower for a long season or spread by their roots survive light tillage, and a plow that turns the soil kills them, so the seeds of weeds charred with the grain show how deeply a field was worked. The scale is set by modern fields, among them the open fields still farmed at **[Laxton](kloom:e/laxton-nottinghamshire)**, in Nottinghamshire. In 2023 Helena Hamerow and her colleagues found that around Cologne the ard prevailed in the fifth and sixth centuries, a plow that turned the soil was in use by the seventh and eighth, and the full three-field system came only in the tenth or eleventh. Their study of English sites finds that the new farming, between about 800 and 1200, let lords grow rich by spreading the plow over more land with the labor of others.

The **[Black Death](kloom:e/black-death)** of 1348 left the land short of hands: wages rose, richer farmers gathered strips, and grain gave way to sheep. The spine crosses the ocean to a city that farmed its lake.
