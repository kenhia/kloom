In July 1944 a plant pathologist from Iowa, **[Norman Borlaug](kloom:e/norman-borlaug)**, flew to Mexico City to breed **[wheat](kloom:e/wheat)** for a program run jointly by the Mexican government and the **[Rockefeller Foundation](kloom:e/rockefeller-foundation)**. Mexico then imported more than half the wheat it ate, and its farms averaged 750 kilograms a hectare. To breed faster, Borlaug grew two generations a year: one sown in the autumn in Sonora, near sea level at 28 degrees north, as the days shortened; the next in summer near Toluca, 2,500 meters up, as the days lengthened. His chief vetoed it, believing seed needed a rest after harvest; Borlaug resigned, and was talked into staying. The shuttle bred wheats indifferent to day length, resistant to rust and at home across a wide range of places, which mattered twenty years later.

## Why the wheat fell over

Feeding the new wheats nitrogen **[fertilizer](kloom:e/fertilizer)** raised yields to about 4,500 kilograms a hectare, and there they stopped. In Borlaug's words, "lodging (falling over of the plant) began to limit further increases." Nitrogen makes a tall wheat taller and its ear heavier, and the stem is a lever: wind and rain push on the head, and the bending moment at the base is that push times the height. Take, as an invented example, the same push on an ear 120 centimeters up and on one 80 centimeters up: by our arithmetic the shorter stem carries two-thirds of the load at its base, and it is thicker besides. A lodged crop is hard to harvest and loses much of its yield. The plate draws the two plants.

The short stem came from Japan. **[Norin 10 wheat](kloom:e/norin-10-wheat)**, registered in 1935, descended from a short native variety, Daruma, crossed with two American wheats. An American adviser in occupied Japan, S. C. Salmon, collected it, and from 1949 the wheat breeder **[Orville Vogel](kloom:e/orville-vogel)** in Washington State crossed it with American varieties. Borlaug obtained Vogel's crosses in 1953 and began crossing them into his Mexican wheats in 1954. In 1999 its dwarfing genes, _Rht-B1b_ and _Rht-D1b_, were shown to make a mutant form of a protein that holds back growth. The hormone gibberellin normally lifts that brake; the mutant ignores the signal, so the stem stays short and the plant spends less on straw and more on grain. Borlaug set out what the dwarf Mexican wheats did in his Nobel lecture:

## Seed for India

In 1962 **[M. S. Swaminathan](kloom:e/m-s-swaminathan)**, at the Indian Agricultural Research Institute in New Delhi, asked for Borlaug and for seed carrying the Norin 10 genes. Then the monsoon failed in 1965 and again in 1966, and India leaned on American grain shipped under Public Law 480. In Rome in November 1965 the American agriculture secretary, Orville Freeman, and India's minister of food and agriculture, C. Subramaniam, signed a plan: 32 million acres of the best irrigated land, farmed by "the more efficient farmers," would get the new seed and "from 100 to 150 pounds of fertilizer per acre as compared with a national average of 3 to 5 pounds." Freeman advised President Johnson that the food shipments could be "conditioned to the performance of the Indian Government."

In 1965, by Borlaug's figures, 350 tons of Mexican seed went to Pakistan and 250 to India; other accounts give less. In 1966 India bought 18,000 tons, the largest seed purchase anywhere until then. India's wheat harvest rose from 12.3 million tons in 1964–65 to 20 million in 1970, led by **[Punjab](kloom:e/punjab-india)**, where yields went from about 1,200 kilograms a hectare in 1960–61 to 5,080 in 2018–19. In 1968 an American aid official, William Gaud, named the change the **[Green Revolution](kloom:e/green-revolution)**.

At the **[International Rice Research Institute](kloom:e/international-rice-research-institute)** in the Philippines, a cross of a tall Indonesian rice, Peta, with a dwarf from Taiwan, Dee-geo-woo-gen, gave **[IR8](kloom:e/ir8)**, released in November 1966. Its short stem comes from a different gene, _sd1_, which makes a faulty enzyme in the pathway that builds gibberellin. Borlaug received the **[Nobel Peace Prize](kloom:e/nobel-peace-prize)** in 1970, and said the revolution had given "a breathing space" against hunger, not an end to it.

## What it cost

Borlaug's lecture counted 70,000 private tube wells sunk in India in one season, 1969–70. Satellites measured the bill: between 2002 and 2008 groundwater under Rajasthan, Punjab and Haryana fell by 4 centimeters of water a year, 109 cubic kilometers in all, most likely pumped for irrigation, and Punjab's water table fell almost 9 meters between 2000 and 2019. **[Vandana Shiva](kloom:e/vandana-shiva)** argued in 1991 that the revolution had left Punjab with "diseased soils, pest-infested crops" and violence. Economists such as Prabhu Pingali reply that without the new varieties the developing world would have grown about a fifth less food, at prices 35 to 65 percent higher, while agreeing that the plan's bias toward the best land and the larger farmer left the rain-fed poor behind and that subsidized fertilizer, pesticide and water were overused. Historians reviewed by Glenn Davis Stone go further, and question whether the seeds themselves raised India's food output as much as the story says. The popular claim that Borlaug saved a billion lives comes from a magazine article of 1997, and is an estimate, not a count. The next frame turns to the crops that genetic engineering made.
