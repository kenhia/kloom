**[Ganj Dareh](kloom:e/ganj-dareh)** is a small mound in the **[Zagros Mountains](kloom:e/zagros-mountains)** of western Iran, where people lived in mud-walled houses about 10,000 years ago, among the bones of a great many **[goats](kloom:e/goat)**. For decades archaeologists judged whether such goats were domestic by their size, on the idea that herded animals soon grow smaller than wild ones. The Ganj Dareh goats were as big as wild goats. In 2000 the zooarchaeologist **[Melinda Zeder](kloom:e/melinda-a-zeder)** and Brian Hesse looked at them another way. Male goats have bigger bones than females, so the bones can be sorted by sex; the ends of long bones fuse at known ages, so they can be sorted by age too. What they found was a herd.

## A hunt or a herd?

Hunters and herders kill different animals, and leave different bones.

| What is killed | By hunters                   | By herders                                    |
| -------------- | ---------------------------- | --------------------------------------------- |
| Males          | Large adults, in their prime | Most of the young males, surplus to breeding  |
| Females        | Fewer, and adults too        | Kept to breed, killed after their prime years |
| The bones left | Dominated by big adults      | Dominated by smaller, older females           |
| Body size      | Wild                         | Wild at first; smaller only centuries later   |

At Ganj Dareh young males were culled and females lived long: the signature of a managed herd, directly dated to about 7900 BC. The smaller bodies and changed horns of domestic goats appear only 500 to 1,000 years later, when herds were moved down into the hotter lowlands. The plate sketches the pattern.

Evidence of this kind, gathered across the hills from the Zagros to the upper Euphrates, now puts the taming of all four of the region's great livestock animals between about 9000 and 8000 BC: **[sheep](kloom:e/sheep)** and goats in the Zagros and southeastern Anatolia, the **[pig](kloom:e/pig)** in southeastern Anatolia, and **[cattle](kloom:e/cattle)** in the upper Euphrates valley. Taming the wild ox, the **[aurochs](kloom:e/aurochs)**, was hard. Modeling the genes of ancient and modern cattle, Ruth Bollongino and her colleagues estimated in 2012 that the world's humpless cattle descend from only about 80 tamed females.

## Milk, wool and the plow

In 1981 the archaeologist **[Andrew Sherratt](kloom:e/andrew-sherratt)** proposed a **[secondary products revolution](kloom:e/secondary-products-revolution)**: for their first few thousand years herds were kept for meat, and only in the fourth and third millennia BC did people turn to the products of living animals, milk, wool, and the strength to pull a plow. Studies of bones, of the ages at which animals died and of joints worn by pulling, bore much of it out. But in 2008 **[Richard Evershed](kloom:e/richard-evershed)** and his colleagues tested the fat soaked into more than 2,200 pots from the Near East and southeastern Europe, and found milk in use by the seventh millennium BC, most of all in northwestern Anatolia, where cattle were common.

The test depends on the two commonest fatty acids in animal fat. The heavier one, stearic acid, is made differently in a ruminant's udder than in its body fat, and carries less carbon-13. The difference between the two acids' carbon-13, Δ¹³C, sorts a pot's residue:

| Fat in the pot        | Δ¹³C, parts per thousand |
| --------------------- | ------------------------ |
| Milk of a ruminant    | below −3.1               |
| Meat of a ruminant    | −3.1 to −0.3             |
| Pig and other animals | above −0.3               |

The two lines of evidence can both be right: people milked from early on, and on a far larger scale later.

## Cheese before milk

Milk had a problem. Most adults cannot digest its sugar, lactose: the gene for lactase, the enzyme that breaks it down, is switched off after weaning in about two-thirds of people alive today, and was in nearly every early farmer. **[Lactase persistence](kloom:e/lactase-persistence)**, the ability to keep digesting lactose, spread only later, through several different mutations in Europe, Africa and the Middle East. Ancient DNA shows the main European variant reaching appreciable frequencies only in the Bronze and Iron Ages.

So the first dairy farmers ate milk transformed. Souring it into yogurt lets bacteria consume much of the lactose. Curdling it into **[cheese](kloom:e/cheese)** keeps the fat and protein in the curds and drains most of the lactose away in the whey. In 2013 Mélanie Salque, Evershed and others found milk fat in pottery sieves from Kuyavia, in Poland, made by farmers of the **[Linear Pottery culture](kloom:e/linear-pottery-culture)** in the sixth millennium BC: the oldest direct evidence of cheese making in northern Europe. A woman buried at Ganj Dareh, whose genome was read in 2016, could not have digested milk as an adult.

Why lactase persistence spread so fast is disputed. The textbook answer was the calories and calcium of fresh milk. In 2022 Evershed and more than a hundred colleagues mapped 6,899 fat residues from 554 sites and found milk in wide use from the Neolithic onward, yet how much milk people used did not explain how the gene spread. In half a million living Britons the gene barely changed how much milk people drank. They proposed instead that drinking milk was harmless to most people most of the time, but deadly in famines and epidemics, when diarrhea could kill the starving and the sick, and that those crises drove the selection. Others still argue for calcium, vitamin D in the sunless north, or clean fluid in dry lands.

## Living with animals

Herding brought people close to animals and their infections. Some links turned out later than once supposed: the measles virus split from rinderpest, a cattle plague, only in the sixth century BC, and the bacillus of human **[tuberculosis](kloom:e/tuberculosis)** is not descended from the cattle form: the cattle bacillus branched off a lineage whose common ancestor may already have infected people. Herds also changed the land. Nitrogen isotopes in charred grain from thirteen European sites, from about 5900 BC onward, show that farmers spread manure on their fields, binding herding and crops together.
