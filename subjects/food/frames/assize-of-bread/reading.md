For five hundred years an English baker could not choose what to charge for a loaf. The **[Assize of Bread and Ale](kloom:e/assize-of-bread-and-ale)**, _Assisa panis et cervisie_, fixed the price of [bread](kloom:e/bread) at a **[farthing](kloom:e/farthing-english-coin)**, a quarter of a penny, and let its weight move instead, with the price of [wheat](kloom:e/wheat). Its date is uncertain: the printed statute books gave it to the fifty-first year of **[Henry III](kloom:e/henry-iii-of-england)**, 1266–67, and **[Adam Smith](kloom:e/adam-smith)** put it in 1262. A committee of the House of Commons found in 1815 that it was issued at the petition of the bakers of **[Coventry](kloom:e/coventry)**, restating ordinances of King John's reign.

## The sliding table

The statute opens, in the translation printed in the _Statutes of the Realm_ in 1810:

> When a Quarter of Wheat is sold for xii d. then Wastel Bread of a Farthing shall weigh vi l. and xvi s.

A quarter was a measure of grain, eight bushels. Wastel was the best white bread. The weight is written as money was, in pounds, shillings and pence, because the coins were the weights: the text that goes with the assize in some copies defines a penny as 32 grains of wheat "in the midst of the Ear", and a pound as 240 pennies. The table then steps up sixpence at a time, and the farthing loaf shrinks as wheat grows dear:

| Wheat, a quarter | Wastel loaf of a farthing | By our arithmetic |
| ---------------- | ------------------------- | ----------------- |
| 12d.             | 6 l. 16 s.                | 2.4 kg            |
| 2s.              | 3 l. 8 s.                 | 1.2 kg            |
| 5s.              | 27 s. 2½d.                | 476 g             |
| 8s.              | 17 s.                     | 298 g             |
| 12s.             | 11 s. 4d.                 | 198 g             |
| 20s.             | 6 s. 9¾d.                 | 119 g             |

The grams take the statute's pound as the Tower pound of about 350 grams, which is what the 1815 committee found it to be. By our arithmetic, every row of these is one rule: the loaf's weight in shillings, times the price of wheat in pence, comes to 1,632. Double the price of wheat and the loaf halves, so a quarter of wheat always made the same quantity of bread for the same money. The plate draws that curve with the statute's steps on it.

At twelve pence a quarter, the committee reasoned, the baker sold 48 farthing loaves for the price of his wheat, about 418 pounds of the coarser wheaten bread; whatever more a quarter made was his. The statute says what that was, "as it is proved by the King's Bakers": he "may gain iv d. and the Bran, and Two Loaves [for Advantage], for Three Servants i d. ob., for Two Lads ob., in Salt ob., for Kneading ob., for Candle q., for Wood [ii d.], for his Bultel ob." (ob. is a halfpenny, q. a farthing, and the bultel the cloth he sifted his flour through).

## The pillory

A baker caught selling short weight was fined, the first, second and third time, if his farthing loaf was no more than two shillings' weight light. If it was more, he was to be set upon the **[pillory](kloom:e/pillory)** "without any Redemption of Money", and a woman brewer who would not amend went to the tumbrel. The same statute priced ale by the grain: with wheat at three shillings a quarter, brewers in cities "may well afford to sell Two Gallons of Beer or Ale for a Penny".

Fear of short weight may be why a baker's dozen is thirteen. That is the popular story; the _Oxford English Dictionary_ finds the phrase only from the late sixteenth century, and explains the thirteenth loaf as the profit of a retailer who bought a dozen to resell.

## Fair price or high price?

Magistrates set it from market prices for centuries, under tables revised in the eighteenth century, though by 1815 many large towns set none and **[London](kloom:e/london)**'s had an act of its own. Smith called it "the only remnant" of the old regulation of prices: "where there is none" of a bakers' monopoly, he wrote in **[_The Wealth of Nations_](kloom:e/the-wealth-of-nations)**, "the competition will regulate it much better than any assize." The Commons committee of 1815 agreed. London's bakers, it found, reported their flour at inflated prices, which the assize then turned into dear bread; in Manchester, Birmingham and Newcastle, with no assize, bread was cheaper and the streets quiet. It concluded that the assize price of bread in London was "higher than if no Assize had ever existed", and Parliament repealed it there that year. Acts of 1822 and 1836 had bread sold by the pound, and the old statute was struck off the books in 1863.

Historians still argue over what it did. It was long thought ill-calculated, its allowance to the baker never as stable as it promised; James Davis argued in 2004 that its makers' logic fitted the trade of their day, with one flaw in its construction that made it hard to enforce. The historian **[E. P. Thompson](kloom:e/e-p-thompson)** called the crowd's side of it the **[moral economy](kloom:e/moral-economy)**: the belief of eighteenth-century crowds that bread had a fair price, and that millers, dealers and bakers who broke it did wrong. Prices, malpractice and hunger set off their **[food riots](kloom:e/food-riot)**, he granted, but the rioters were defending the old rules of marketing, milling and baking that the new free market was discarding. Critics such as John Stevenson doubted so deep-rooted a pattern of belief. The 1815 committee had heard the same belief from the other side: that the assize, by making the magistrate answer for the price of bread, kept the capital calm.

The trail goes on to Chillicothe, Missouri, in 1928, where a machine first sliced the loaf.
