In the winter and spring of 1933 millions of people starved to death in the countryside of Soviet Ukraine, the richest grain land of the **[Soviet Union](kloom:e/soviet-union)**. Ukrainians call it the **[Holodomor](kloom:e/holodomor)**, "killing by hunger". It was not a crop failure that the state failed to relieve. The harvests of 1931 and 1932 were poor, but the people who died were left without food because the state took it: the grain procurement of 1932 was run by **[Joseph Stalin](kloom:e/joseph-stalin)**'s government in Moscow and its officials in the **[Ukrainian SSR](kloom:e/ukrainian-soviet-socialist-republic)**, and it went on taking grain, seed and finally any food at all from villages that were already starving.

## How the procurement worked

From 1929 the **[collectivization](kloom:e/collectivization-in-the-soviet-union)** of farming forced peasants into collective farms, the _kolkhozy_, whose harvest belonged first to the state. Each year the Politburo set a national plan for grain, which was divided among the republics, then the oblasts, the districts and each farm. A farm was paid only after the plan was met. The plan for Ukraine's peasant farms from the 1932 harvest was 5.83 million tonnes, cut three times to 3.77 million; 3.53 million were taken, against 6.47 million from the year before, the historians **[R. W. Davies](kloom:e/r-w-davies)** and **[Stephen Wheatcroft](kloom:e/stephen-g-wheatcroft)** found in the archives. The cuts came too late and were too small, and three measures closed the village while they were being made:

| Date            | Measure                                                                                                              |
| --------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 7 August 1932   | Collective-farm property, "the harvest in the fields" included, made state property; theft of it punishable by death |
| November 1932   | Farms behind on the plan to return grain advanced to their members; villages put on the "black board"                |
| 22 January 1933 | Peasants forbidden to leave Ukraine or the Kuban "for bread"; those who reached the north arrested and sent back     |

The first is remembered as the **[Law of Spikelets](kloom:e/law-of-spikelets)**, because peasants were prosecuted under it for gleaning ears of wheat. Its second section ordered, in our translation of the Russian, "as a measure of judicial repression for theft of collective-farm and cooperative property, the supreme measure of social defense, shooting with confiscation of all property", or under mitigating circumstances at least ten years, with no amnesty. A blacklisted village had its loans called in, its shops closed and its goods confiscated; a 1988 report to the United States Congress counted 82 districts blacklisted in December 1932, while later archival counts give 37 of Ukraine's 392 districts and at least 400 farms. The directive of 22 January, signed by Stalin and Molotov, said that the flight of peasants "is organized by enemies of Soviet power", and Ukraine stopped selling railway tickets to peasants without a permit.

The plate draws the machine: the quota coming down the chain, the grain going out to the state's reserves, the towns and export, and the border drawn around the village. Exports fell from 4.8 million tonnes in 1931/32 to about 1.8 million in 1932/33, but did not stop. In mid-1933 the state still held over a million tonnes in reserve; Davies, Mark Tauger and Wheatcroft judged that releasing more of it could have saved "hundreds of thousands – perhaps millions" of lives.

## The dead

No one counted the dead at the time, and Soviet officials hid what was counted. The demographers' estimates for Soviet Ukraine run from about 2.6 to 5 million; one of the most careful, by Nataliia Levchuk and her colleagues in 2020, finds 3.9 million excess deaths in 1932 to 1934. The famine was wider than Ukraine. Some 3.3 million died in the Russian republic, many in the Kuban, where most people were Ukrainian, and on the Volga. In the **[Kazakh famine of 1930–1933](kloom:e/kazakh-famine-of-1930-1933)**, which came with the forced settlement of nomadic herders, 1.3 to 2.3 million died, the highest share of any people.

## Was it genocide?

Historians agree that the famine was made by policy. They disagree about intent. **[Robert Conquest](kloom:e/robert-conquest)**, in 1986, called it a "terror-famine" aimed at the Ukrainian peasantry; **[Anne Applebaum](kloom:e/anne-applebaum)** argues that it was used to undermine Ukrainian national identity. Davies and Wheatcroft found "no evidence, either direct or indirect, that Stalin sought deliberately to starve the peasants", though they call his policy "ruthless and brutal"; Conquest wrote to them in 2003 that Stalin could have prevented the famine but "put 'Soviet interest' other than feeding the starving first". **[Raphael Lemkin](kloom:e/raphael-lemkin)**, who coined the word genocide, called Ukraine in 1953 "perhaps the classic example of Soviet genocide", and the question turns on the **[Genocide Convention](kloom:e/genocide-convention)**'s phrase, conditions "calculated to bring about" a group's destruction. Ukraine's parliament declared the Holodomor a genocide in 2006, the European Parliament in 2022.

## Who said so

Soviet newspapers did not mention the famine. In March 1933 the Welsh journalist **[Gareth Jones](kloom:e/gareth-jones-journalist)** walked through villages and reported from Berlin: "Everywhere was the cry, 'There is no bread; we are dying!'" **[Walter Duranty](kloom:e/walter-duranty)**, Moscow correspondent of _The New York Times_, answered in print that there was no "actual starvation". Jones was murdered in Japanese-occupied Inner Mongolia in 1935. Inside the Soviet Union the famine could not be named until the late 1980s.

The trail goes on to Bengal in 1943, where a famine came in a market rather than a quota.
