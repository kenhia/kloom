Who first cut a potato into sticks and fried them is disputed. In Belgium a story told by the journalist Jo Gérard has villagers of the Meuse valley frying potatoes before 1680, when the river froze and they could not catch fish; but Gérard never produced the manuscript he cited, and potatoes were not grown there until about 1735. The Belgian historian Pierre Leclercq concludes that **[french fries](kloom:e/french-fries)** are French: a Paris street food by the 1840s, taken to Belgium in 1844 by a Bavarian musician, Frédéric Krieger, who sold "fried potatoes in the Paris style". "French fried potatoes" appear in an English cookbook of 1856, though as thin slices. What made the fry the commonest way of eating a potato in America came a century later, in the American West, and depended on a particular potato and a freezer.

## The potato

**[Luther Burbank](kloom:e/luther-burbank)**, a young farmer in Massachusetts, raised the Burbank potato in the 1870s from a seedling of the Early Rose and sold the rights for $150 in 1875. A russet-skinned sport of it, a natural mutation in a growing tuber, was long credited to a Colorado grower, Lou Sweet, in 1914. In 2014 Paul Bethke and colleagues traced it instead to May's Netted Gem, released in 1902 by a seed firm in St. Paul, and confirmed with DNA markers that it is a mutant of Burbank's potato. The **[Russet Burbank](kloom:e/russet-burbank)** is long, dry and floury, which is what a good fry needs. In 1930 it made up only 4 percent of American potatoes; irrigation on **[Idaho](kloom:e/idaho)**'s Snake River Plain, and then the frozen fry, made it the continent's leading potato.

## How a frozen fry is made

Frying a potato from frozen took some solving: sugar browns too fast in hot fat, and ice damages the tissue as it forms. A patent filed in September 1949 by Henry Chase of Nampa, Idaho, set out a whole plant, step by step:

| Step      | In the 1949 patent                                                      | Why                                                                                        |
| --------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Condition | Store warm, about 70 °F, for 10 to 20 days                              | Turns sugar back to starch; above 1 percent sugar "renders a potato unfit for deep frying" |
| Peel      | Steam under pressure for half a minute or more, then an ice-water spray | Shocks the skin off                                                                        |
| Cut       | Into French fries, shoestrings, cottage fries                           |                                                                                            |
| Wash      | 3 to 4 minutes in agitated water                                        | Removes surface starch so pieces do not stick                                              |
| Blanch    | Live steam at 210 to 250 °F for about 3 minutes                         | Kills the enzymes that discolor the potato                                                 |
| Dry       | Fans                                                                    | Takes off 10 to 15 percent of the water                                                    |
| Freeze    | Tunnels averaging −25 °F; storage at 0 °F                               |                                                                                            |
| Fry       | From frozen, in fat at 375 °F, about 3 minutes                          |                                                                                            |

Industrial fries today follow nearly the same line with one addition: after blanching and a soak in glucose and sodium acid pyrophosphate, the strips are par-fried, partly cooked in oil, before they are frozen, and the restaurant only finishes them. The plate draws the line and a fry in section.

The fry that made the industry came from the company of **[J. R. Simplot](kloom:e/j-r-simplot)**, an Idaho farmer and shipper whose plants had dried onions and potatoes for the army in the Second World War. In **[Eric Schlosser](kloom:e/eric-schlosser)**'s account in **[_Fast Food Nation_](kloom:e/fast-food-nation)** (2001), a team of chemists led by Ray Dunlap worked through years of failures, finding among other things that fries sink to the bottom of a potato-chip fryer and burn, before Dunlap brought Simplot a reheated fry that worked. Simplot began selling them in 1953; the company's own history says the late 1940s. Sales to households were slow.

## McDonald's

The customer was **[McDonald's](kloom:e/mcdonalds)**. **[Ray Kroc](kloom:e/ray-kroc)**, who franchised the chain from 1955, treated its fries as near-sacred, and its restaurants cut fresh Russet Burbanks bought from about 175 local suppliers. According to Schlosser, Simplot met Kroc in 1965, offered to build a plant only for McDonald's fries, and the deal was sealed with a handshake; the chain began selling his frozen fries the next year. McDonald's then had about 725 restaurants; within a decade it had more than 3,000. Other accounts date the handshake to 1967 and say all its fries were frozen by 1972. Frozen fries were cheaper and the same in every restaurant, in every season, and fries became one of the most profitable things on the menu.

Schlosser gives the change in the American diet: in 1960 the typical American ate 81 pounds of fresh potatoes a year and about 4 pounds of frozen fries; by the end of the century, 49 pounds of fresh and more than 30 of frozen fries, nine-tenths of them bought at fast food restaurants. The Department of Agriculture's count for 2025 shows where the crop goes now. Of 417.6 million hundredweight of potatoes grown in the United States, 159.7 million went into frozen fries, about 38 percent by our arithmetic, more than went to the fresh market. Idaho grew 140 million hundredweight, a third of the nation's crop and more than any other state.

The trail began with chuño, a potato frozen by the Andean night to keep it through a hard year. It ends with one frozen in a tunnel to make it the same everywhere. The trail returns to its anchor, the Columbian Exchange.
