Between 1959 and 1961 China suffered the deadliest famine in history. Demographers' estimates of the **[Great Chinese Famine](kloom:e/great-chinese-famine)** run from 15 million excess deaths to more than 45 million; most fall between 20 and 40 million. It came in the second year of the **[Great Leap Forward](kloom:e/great-leap-forward)**, the campaign by which **[Mao Zedong](kloom:e/mao-zedong)** and the **[Chinese Communist Party](kloom:e/chinese-communist-party)** meant to overtake Britain's industry within fifteen years, and it was made worse at every step by a state that believed its own reports.

## The Great Leap

In 1958 every rural household was forced into one of the new **[people's communes](kloom:e/peoples-commune)**. Private plots were abolished and markets closed; the harvest went from the field to the commune's store, and families ate in communal kitchens, where in the autumn of 1958 they were told to eat as much as they liked. Millions of farmers were sent to dig irrigation works and to make iron in **[backyard furnaces](kloom:e/backyard-furnace)**: of the 11 million tonnes of steel the Party claimed for 1958, it admitted that 3 million had been made "by indigenous methods". On the fields, cadres ordered close planting, seed sown several times as densely as usual, and deep plowing, to 33 to 66 centimeters instead of the usual 15 to 20, ideas taken from the Soviet agronomist **[Trofim Lysenko](kloom:e/trofim-lysenko)** and his followers. Both lowered yields.

## Grain that was never grown

Procurement ran from the top. Beijing set production targets, the provinces passed them down, and after the autumn harvest the state took a share fixed from what each collective was expected to grow. Officials were rewarded for big numbers and punished as "rightists" for small ones, so they reported harvests that did not exist. Shandong reported 50 million tonnes for 1958 against a real 12. In April 1959 the State Statistical Bureau announced a national harvest of 375 million tonnes, "doubling that of the previous year". The plate sets the claims against the counts:

Taken on claimed harvests, procurement rose to about 38 percent of the real output of 1959, against 26 percent the year before. Grain exports reached their highest level of the decade in 1959.

In July 1959, at the **[Lushan Conference](kloom:e/lushan-conference)**, the defense minister **[Peng Dehuai](kloom:e/peng-dehuai)** wrote privately to Mao of a "wind of exaggeration" and "petty-bourgeois fanaticism". Mao circulated the letter and had Peng condemned. The Central Committee's communiqué cut the 1958 harvest to 250 million tonnes, but called on the Party to fight "right opportunist ideas" among cadres who "slander as 'petty-bourgeois fanaticism' the great leap forward". In the purge that followed, over three million Party members were punished, and no official dared report a shortfall. The worst deaths came that winter, two or three months after the harvest of 1959 had been procured.

## Counting the dead

| Estimate, excess deaths | By whom                         |
| ----------------------- | ------------------------------- |
| 15 million              | Cormac Ó Gráda, 2007            |
| 23 million              | Peng Xizhe, 1987                |
| 30 million              | Basil Ashton and others, 1984   |
| 36 million              | Yang Jisheng, _Tombstone_, 2008 |
| 45 million or more      | Frank Dikötter, 2010            |

The estimates differ in what a normal death rate would have been and in how many deaths went unrecorded. **[Yang Jisheng](kloom:e/yang-jisheng-journalist)**, a journalist of the state news agency whose foster father starved in 1959, spent years in provincial archives; his _Tombstone_ was published in Hong Kong and is banned in mainland China. **[Frank Dikötter](kloom:e/frank-dikotter)**'s **[_Mao's Great Famine_](kloom:e/maos-great-famine)** adds deaths from beatings and overwork; critics, Ó Gráda among them, found its numbers poorly sourced. Scholars disagree, too, about the weather. Dikötter and Yang find nothing unusual in it; the economist Y. Y. Kueh found that a simple index of the weather explained much of the fall in yields in 1960. Xin Meng, Nancy Qian and Pierre Yared showed that in 1959 China grew enough grain to feed everyone, and that more people died where more food had been grown, which they explain by a procurement system too rigid to respond.

## What the Party said

For two decades the famine was "the three years of natural disasters". In 1961 **[Liu Shaoqi](kloom:e/liu-shaoqi)**, the head of state, concluded after 44 days in Hunan villages that the disaster was three parts nature and seven parts human error, and he said so at the **[Seven Thousand Cadres Conference](kloom:e/seven-thousand-cadres-conference)** of January 1962. The Party's resolution on its history, adopted in June 1981, says that "it was mainly due to the errors of the Great Leap Forward and of the struggle against 'Right opportunism' together with a succession of natural calamities and the perfidious scrapping of contracts by the Soviet Government that our economy encountered serious difficulties between 1959 and 1961". It gives no number of the dead.

The retreat began in January 1961. Procurement was cut, millions of workers were sent back to the villages, and China, which had exported grain through the famine, imported 5.8 million tonnes in 1961, from Australia and Canada among others.

The trail ends here. Its anchor, the Great Famine in Ireland, is where the argument about what makes a famine began.
