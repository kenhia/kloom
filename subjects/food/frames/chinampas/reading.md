The Mexica built their city on an island in a lake. **[Tenochtitlan](kloom:e/tenochtitlan)** rose in **[Lake Texcoco](kloom:e/lake-texcoco)**, in the Valley of Mexico, more than 2,200 meters up; its founding is dated by tradition to 1325, the date chosen for its six-hundredth anniversary in 1925. Around it lay one of the most productive ways of farming ever devised. The farmers of the southern lakes had been building the raised fields called **[chinampas](kloom:e/chinampa)** for two centuries or more: the earliest securely dated are from about 1150 to 1350, and a large system around Xaltocan, in the north of the valley, was abandoned when its neighbors conquered the city in the fourteenth century. In the 1430s **[Itzcoatl](kloom:e/itzcoatl)**, ruler of Tenochtitlan, conquered the chinampa towns of the southern lakes, among them **[Xochimilco](kloom:e/xochimilco)** and Culhuacan, and under the **[Aztec Empire](kloom:e/aztec-empire)** the fields spread across the fresh water, behind dikes that kept out the salt of Lake Texcoco.

## A field built in a lake

Chinampas did not float, though they are often called floating gardens. Each was built up from the lake bed:

| Step            | What was done                                                                               |
| --------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Stake out       | A rectangle in shallow water over a firm bottom, marked by posts at the corners             |
| Fence           | Reeds and branches woven between stakes, a wattle wall under the surface                    |
| Fill            | Alternate layers of mud from the lake bottom and water plants, until 50–70 cm above water   |
| Dry and dress   | The top left to dry for some weeks, then more mud, compost and plant matter added           |
| Plant the edges | Ahuejote willows set along the sides, their roots binding the bank                          |
| Keep it up      | Mud dredged from the canals spread on the beds each season, renewing soil and keeping depth |

Between the beds ran ditches a meter or two wide, opening into canals wide enough for a canoe. The beds were kept narrow so that water from the canals rose through the soil to the roots: in Tenochtitlan they ran about 90 meters long and 5 to 10 wide. The fertility came from the lake, its mud full of rotting plants, and the willow, the **[ahuejote](kloom:e/salix-bonplandiana)**, kept it in place. Farmers raised seedlings in beds of mud and set them out, so one plot could carry several crops a year: up to seven, by the usual account, and up to five by the UN Food and Agriculture Organization's.

## How many did they feed?

No one measured the harvest. Every estimate starts from modern chinampa yields and an area. One, from 1974, holds that a hectare fed 20 people and that the valley's 9,000 hectares of chinampas could feed 180,000. That is close to Tenochtitlan's own size, most often put above 200,000, but the valley as a whole held one and a half million people or more, so by our arithmetic the chinampas could have fed about one in eight of them. Scholars draw opposite lessons. Edward Calnek, reconstructing the city's own chinampa districts from colonial records in 1972, found that they fed far less of it than had been supposed, and that it depended on farmland outside. Others stress that maize and beans came in as tribute from conquered provinces. Christopher Morehart, reconstructing the fields around Xaltocan, found that they could produce a large surplus above the needs of everyone who lived there.

## Maize, tribute and the market

**[Maize](kloom:e/maize)** came first, with beans, squash, chilies, amaranth, tomatoes and flowers. The **[Codex Mendoza](kloom:e/codex-mendoza)**, painted by Nahua artists around 1541 for the Spanish viceroy, lists the provinces of the empire and what each paid, and shows how a child was fed. Its glosses, in Spanish, give a child's ration of maize tortillas by age:

| Age      | Tortillas      |
| -------- | -------------- |
| 3        | Half           |
| 4 and 5  | One            |
| 6 to 12  | One and a half |
| 13 to 14 | Two            |

![A page of the Codex Mendoza in four bands of painted figures with Spanish glosses: parents punishing children of eleven over chili smoke, a boy of twelve made to lie bound on damp ground and a girl of twelve sweeping at night, a boy of thirteen poling a canoe of reeds and a girl grinding maize and making tortillas on a griddle, and at fourteen a boy fishing from a canoe and a girl weaving at a loom, each with its count of tortillas drawn as small ovals](mendoza-60r.jpg)

Food that was not tribute was traded. **[Hernán Cortés](kloom:e/hernan-cortes)** wrote to Charles V in 1520 of the market at **[Tlatelolco](kloom:e/tlatelolco-altepetl)**, the city's twin on the same island, "twice as large as that of Salamanca, all surrounded by arcades, where there are daily more than sixty thousand souls, buying and selling", where "they sell maize, both in the grain and made into bread" (in Francis MacNutt's translation). **[Bernal Díaz del Castillo](kloom:e/bernal-diaz-del-castillo)**, in Alfred Maudslay's translation, remembered beans, fowls and the women "who sold cooked food, dough and tripe"; little cakes made from an ooze skimmed off the lake, "having a flavour something like cheese"; and men and women sold as slaves, brought in "tied to long poles, with collars round their necks."

## What the conquest did

**[Smallpox](kloom:e/smallpox)** reached Tenochtitlan in October 1520. The city surrendered to Cortés and his Indigenous allies on August 13, 1521, after a siege that cut its causeways and its water. The dams and sluice gates were broken and never rebuilt. As the people who knew the fields died, many were abandoned; others, in the lakeshore towns of the south, were kept through the colonial era. The **[cocoliztli](kloom:e/cocoliztli-epidemics)** epidemics of 1545 and 1576 killed millions more across New Spain, and by one reconstruction the valley's population had fallen below 100,000 a century after the conquest. The canals of the city were filled for carts. In 1605, after a flood, the royal cosmographer Enrico Martínez began a canal to drain the lakes out of the valley. Over the following centuries they were emptied, and Mexico City, pumping the water from beneath it, now sinks into the dried bed.

## Xochimilco

The chinampas lasted where the Spanish took little interest. The canals of Xochimilco became a World Heritage Site in 1987, and the chinampas of Mexico City a **[Globally Important Agricultural Heritage System](kloom:e/globally-important-agricultural-heritage-systems)** in 2017. They are still shrinking. Between 1989 and 2006 chinampa land fell from 7.4 to 2.5 percent of Xochimilco, while built-up land rose from 46.7 to 57.2 percent; since 1950 many canals have been fed with treated sewage, and most beds now grow flowers and vegetables, not maize. From here the spine follows the crops of both Americas across the ocean, in the Columbian Exchange.
