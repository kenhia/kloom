In the 1990s the Brazilian epidemiologist **[Carlos Augusto Monteiro](kloom:e/carlos-augusto-monteiro)** noticed obesity rising among Brazil's poor while it fell among the better-off. Studying what Brazilians ate, he found two patterns: one built on traditional foods such as rice and beans, the other on highly processed products. In 2009 he set out the idea in a short commentary in **[_Public Health Nutrition_](kloom:e/public-health-nutrition)**, titled "The issue is not food, nor nutrients, so much as processing." In 2010 he and his colleagues at the **[University of São Paulo](kloom:e/university-of-sao-paulo)** published "a new classification of foods" by the extent and purpose of their industrial processing; from its Portuguese title it took the name **[Nova](kloom:e/nova-classification)**. In Brazil's household budget survey of 2002–03, a fifth of the calories families bought came from the most processed group, and almost a third in the richest households. They called that group **[ultra-processed food](kloom:e/ultra-processed-food)**.

## Four groups

In its later form, set out by Monteiro and eleven colleagues in 2019, Nova sorts every food into four groups, which the plate draws as a flow:

| Group                                 | What it is                                                        | How it is made                                                                                                                | Examples                                                                           |
| ------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1. Unprocessed or minimally processed | The edible parts of plants and animals                            | Cleaned, dried, ground, pasteurized, chilled or frozen; nothing added                                                         | Grains, fresh or frozen vegetables, milk, eggs, meat                               |
| 2. Processed culinary ingredients     | Substances taken from group 1 foods or from nature                | Pressed, refined, milled or mined                                                                                             | Oils, butter, sugar, salt                                                          |
| 3. Processed foods                    | Group 1 foods with group 2 added                                  | Canned, bottled, or fermented as bread and cheese are                                                                         | Canned vegetables, cheese, bread of flour, water, salt and yeast                   |
| 4. Ultra-processed foods              | "Formulations of ingredients, mostly of exclusive industrial use" | Foods fractioned into substances, some chemically modified, then assembled with additives by extrusion, molding or pre-frying | Soft drinks, packaged snacks, flavored breakfast cereals, nuggets, instant noodles |

The test is the list of ingredients. A product is ultra-processed if it holds even one substance a kitchen never or rarely uses, such as high-fructose corn syrup, hydrogenated oil, maltodextrin or soy protein isolate, or a "cosmetic" additive: a flavor, color, emulsifier, sweetener or thickener. A loaf of flour, water, salt and yeast is processed; the same loaf with an emulsifier is ultra-processed. The definition says nothing about sugar, fat or salt. Brazil's dietary guidelines of 2014 made avoiding these products their "golden rule".

## Hall's trial

The obvious objection is that ultra-processed foods might harm only because they carry more sugar, fat and salt. In 2019 **Kevin Hall** and his colleagues at the **[National Institutes of Health](kloom:e/national-institutes-of-health)** tested that in a **[randomized controlled trial](kloom:e/randomized-controlled-trial)**. Twenty adults lived for 28 days in its Clinical Center. Each ate an ultra-processed diet for two weeks and an unprocessed one for two, the order assigned at random. The two sets of meals were matched for the calories, energy density, protein, carbohydrate, fat, sugar, sodium and fiber put in front of them, at twice what each person needed, and they could eat as much or as little as they liked.

![Bar chart of mean daily energy intake in kilocalories: ultra-processed diet 3,099 in week 1 and 2,865 in week 2; unprocessed diet 2,555 in week 1 and 2,486 in week 2](hall-intake.svg)

| Energy intake, kcal a day, 19 people, measured weeks | Week 1 | Week 2 |
| ---------------------------------------------------- | -----: | -----: |
| Ultra-processed diet                                 |  3,099 |  2,865 |
| Unprocessed diet                                     |  2,555 |  2,486 |

On the ultra-processed diet they ate about 508 kilocalories a day more, and gained 0.9 kilograms; on the unprocessed diet they lost 0.9. They rated the meals equally pleasant, but ate the ultra-processed ones faster. The authors listed the limits themselves: a ward is not a home, there was no run-in or washout period, and the trial was not designed to find the cause. A larger trial in London, published in 2025, gave 55 adults eight weeks of each diet, both following Britain's healthy-eating guide. They lost weight on both, about 2.1 percent on the minimally processed diet and 1.1 on the ultra-processed one.

## What the cohorts show

Most of the evidence comes from **[cohort studies](kloom:e/cohort-study)**, which follow people and record what they eat and what illnesses follow. In 2024 an umbrella review in **[_The BMJ_](kloom:e/the-bmj)** gathered 45 pooled analyses covering almost ten million people and found greater exposure to ultra-processed food associated with 32 kinds of harm, the most convincing for death from cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes, anxiety and common mental disorders. Its own grading rated most of that evidence low or very low in quality. People who eat a great deal of it differ from those who eat little: in the United States, where it supplied 55 percent of calories in 2021–23, adults in the highest income group ate the smallest share.

Critics press harder. Michael Gibney and colleagues argued in 2017 that the group is too broad to base dietary advice on, and that nutrients remain the better guide. In 2022 a French team asked 159 nutrition specialists to place 120 marketed foods in Nova's groups, and found their agreement low; their study was partly funded, as they declared, by a fund the food industry helps sponsor. Monteiro and his colleagues answer that the products are made by transnational corporations to be cheap, durable and hyper-palatable, and that this, not any one nutrient, is the problem. Hall's own meals cost $106 a week for the ultra-processed diet against $151 for the other.

## The law, as of October 2026

Chile's food labeling law, in force since 2016, put warning labels on packaged products high in sugar, sodium, saturated fat or calories, and barred them from schools and children's advertising; purchases of such drinks fell about 24 percent below their earlier trend. Mexico approved warning labels of its own in 2019. Both are set by nutrients, not by Nova. Britain's ban on advertising "less healthy" food on television before 9 p.m. and online, in force since January 5, 2026, is set by a nutrient profile too. In the United States the **[Food and Drug Administration](kloom:e/food-and-drug-administration)** and the **[United States Department of Agriculture](kloom:e/united-states-department-of-agriculture)** asked in July 2025 for information to write a federal definition, and the **[Dietary Guidelines for Americans](kloom:e/dietary-guidelines-for-americans)** of January 2026 told readers to "avoid highly processed" foods without defining them. In August 2026 the agencies sent a proposed definition for review to the White House's **[Office of Management and Budget](kloom:e/office-of-management-and-budget)**; its terms have not been published.

The subject began with fire, the first processing of all. It ends with an argument over how much a factory may do to food before it stops being the food it started as.
