About a million years ago, someone kept a fire some 30 meters inside **[Wonderwerk Cave](kloom:e/wonderwerk-cave)**, in the Kuruman Hills of South Africa's Northern Cape. The evidence is small. In 2012 a team led by **Francesco Berna** cut blocks of intact sediment from the cave's early Acheulean layers, the layers of the hand axe, and read thin slices of them under the microscope and with infrared spectroscopy. They found bone that had been burned and the ash of plants, mostly grasses, brush and leaves, lying where they had burned. Burning ran through the whole thickness of the layer, which made repeated wildfires an unlikely source. It was, they wrote, "the earliest secure evidence for burning in an archaeological context." A study reported in 2026 argues that the cave held fire much earlier still, perhaps 1.8 million years ago, kept going from natural blazes rather than made.

Burning is not cooking. The oldest evidence that a human ancestor cooked food comes from **Gesher Benot Ya'aqov**, on the Jordan River north of the **[Sea of Galilee](kloom:e/sea-of-galilee)**, about 780,000 years ago. Its old lake shore preserved more than 40,000 fish remains. In the layers people lived on they are almost all the throat teeth of two large species of carp, clustered around "phantom" hearths marked only by burned flint, while the bones are gone. X-ray diffraction of the teeth by **Irit Zohar** and her colleagues, published in 2022, showed they had been heated, but below 500 °C: the fish had been cooked, not thrown on the fire.

## What fire does to food

Cooking does work that a body would otherwise have to do. Heat breaks the bonds that fold proteins, so enzymes can reach them. It swells and bursts starch granules, so they can be digested. It softens fiber and tough meat, and it kills parasites and some toxins. The gain has been measured.

| Study                  | What was compared                                                      | What it found                                                                                                       |
| ---------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Evenepoel et al., 1998 | 25 g of egg protein, raw or cooked, in five volunteers                 | 51 percent of the raw protein was absorbed; 91 of the cooked                                                        |
| Carmody et al., 2011   | Mice fed sweet potato or beef, raw or cooked, whole or pounded         | Mice kept their weight on cooked food and lost it on raw, whether or not it was pounded                             |
| Koebnick et al., 1999  | 513 people eating 70 to 100 percent raw food, for 3.7 years on average | Men lost 9.9 kg on average, women 12 kg; about 30 percent of women under 45 stopped menstruating, in part or wholly |

The last result is the one that matters for history. These were modern raw-food eaters, with farmed fruit and vegetables and oils to hand, and many still could not hold their weight. Wrangham argues from such evidence that people cannot stay healthy and fertile on raw food for long.

## The cooking hypothesis

The primatologist **[Richard Wrangham](kloom:e/richard-wrangham)** turned this into a theory of human origins, the **[cooking hypothesis](kloom:e/control-of-fire-by-early-humans)**, first in a 1999 paper and then in his book **[_Catching Fire: How Cooking Made Us Human_](kloom:e/catching-fire-how-cooking-made-us-human)** (2009). About 1.9 million years ago, **[_Homo erectus_](kloom:e/homo-erectus)** appeared with smaller teeth and jaws, a smaller gut and a larger brain than the species before it, and a body built for walking and sleeping on the ground. Wrangham argues that cooked food explains all of these at once: soft food needs small teeth, digestible food needs a short gut, and the energy saved could feed a costly brain.

One test of the idea measured time. A chimpanzee spends about 37 percent of its active day feeding. In 2011 Wrangham, **Chris Organ** and their colleagues compared feeding time across primates and found that a primate of our size would be expected to spend 48 percent of its day eating, while people spend 4.7. They put the change in the human line before or at the appearance of _Homo erectus_, which is what the hypothesis predicts.

## The case against

The difficulty is the fire. If cooking shaped _Homo erectus_ 1.9 million years ago, hearths should be common from then on, and they are not. Wonderwerk and Gesher Benot Ya'aqov are rare and much later. Reviewing the European sites in 2011, **[Wil Roebroeks](kloom:e/wil-roebroeks)** and **Paola Villa** found that people reached cold northern latitudes without leaving signs of regular fire, which became a habitual part of life only 300,000 to 400,000 years ago. Others point out that brains grew through long stretches with no evidence of cooking at all, and that meat, scavenged or hunted and cut with stone tools, could explain the bigger brain without fire.

So the question is open. What is not in doubt is the end of the story: we are the cooking animal, and today we cannot live well without it. The next frame looks at how people fed themselves for most of our history, as foragers.
