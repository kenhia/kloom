In June 1647 a royalist ruined by the English Civil War, **[Richard Ligon](kloom:e/richard-ligon)**, sailed for **[Barbados](kloom:e/barbados)** to make his fortune. He found an island in the middle of a change. Its settlers had grown tobacco, cotton and indigo, much of it worked by English and Irish servants bound for five years. A few years before, planters had brought **[sugarcane](kloom:e/sugarcane)** and the knowledge of how to work it from Dutch Brazil. Ligon lived on a plantation of 500 acres, more than 200 of them in cane, worked by 96 enslaved Africans, three enslaved Indigenous women and their children, and 28 servants. He went home in 1650, and in 1657 he published _A True & Exact History of the Island of Barbados_, with plans of the buildings where **[sugar](kloom:e/sugar)** was made.

## From New Guinea to the Caribbean

Sugarcane was domesticated in New Guinea, from about 4000 BC. People in India learned by about AD 350 to boil its juice down to crystals that kept and traveled, _khanda_, the root of the word "candy". Sugar-making reached Persia by the sixth century, and the Arab conquests carried cane and the skill of boiling it to Egypt, Sicily and Cyprus. In the fifteenth century the Portuguese planted it on **[Madeira](kloom:e/madeira)**, which by 1492 made over 1,400 tonnes a year, and on **[São Tomé](kloom:e/sao-tome-island)**, in the Gulf of Guinea, settled from 1493, where it was worked by Africans enslaved on the mainland: the pattern of what followed. Columbus carried cane to Hispaniola in 1493. Brazil became the great producer in the sixteenth century, and the Dutch, who held its northeast from 1630, brought the method to the English and French islands.

## How a sugar works ran

Ligon set down "the whole processe" as it stood when he left, step by step:

| Step               | What was done                                                                                                                                                 |
| ------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Cutting            | The cane, ripe at fifteen months, cut with bills six inches above the ground and stripped of its leaves                                                       |
| Carrying           | Bundles brought on donkeys to a railed platform beside the mill                                                                                               |
| Grinding           | Horses or oxen turned sweeps that turned three upright rollers; an enslaved African fed the cane through, and another passed it back                          |
| The cistern        | The juice ran in a lead pipe to a cistern, and could stand no more than a day before it soured                                                                |
| Five coppers       | Boiled and skimmed in the largest copper, then ladled to each smaller one, with a "temper" of water and a vine, the withe, without which it would never grain |
| The tache          | In the last copper it was tested by drops that should "candy" and harden, then ladled out to cool                                                             |
| Filling and curing | Set in clay pots stopped with plantain leaves for two days, then a month in the curing house while molasses dripped out                                       |

The plate draws the boiling house in section. Cut cane spoils in a day or two, so the mill and the coppers could not wait. As Ligon puts it, "the work goes on, from Monday morning at one a clock, till Saturday night, (at which time the fire in the Furnaces are put out) all houres of the day and night, with fresh supplies of Men, Horses, and Cattle."

Ligon says plainly why planters chose slaves: servants were theirs "but for five yeers", while "the slaves and their posterity" were "subject to their Masters for ever". He also recorded their food: a bunch of plantains a week, "To this, no bread nor drink, but water. Their lodging at night a board". He added that they were "happy people, whom so little contents". That was a visitor's view from the planter's house. The historian **[Russell Menard](kloom:e/russell-menard)** has argued that slavery took hold in Barbados before sugar did, under cotton and tobacco, so that sugar spread a system already there rather than creating it.

Across the Americas the **[Atlantic slave trade](kloom:e/atlantic-slave-trade)** carried about 12.5 million people out of Africa between 1501 and 1866, and about 10.7 million survived the crossing. Brazil took about 40 percent of them. Of some 8.5 million taken between 1640 and 1807, the historian Kenneth Morgan reckons one in three was bound for the British Caribbean. On the sugar islands the enslaved died faster than they were born, from overwork, disease and a diet that historians such as Kenneth Kiple blame for much of the infant death. Planters bought more. By the estimate of a French lawyer who lived there, **[Saint-Domingue](kloom:e/saint-domingue)** took in more than 800,000 Africans between 1680 and 1776 and held about 290,000 at the end of it. **[Olaudah Equiano](kloom:e/olaudah-equiano)**, who by his own account was carried to Bridgetown on a slave ship as a boy, wrote in 1789, citing the Quaker Anthony Benezet, that even Barbados, said to treat its slaves best, needed 1,000 new Africans a year to keep its 80,000 from falling: "So that the whole term of a negro's life may be said to be there but sixteen years!"

## Sweetness and power

In England, sugar consumption per head rose about twentyfold between 1663 and 1775. In _Sweetness and Power_ (1985) the anthropologist **[Sidney Mintz](kloom:e/sidney-mintz)** traced sugar's path from a luxury and a medicine to a staple of the English poor, and argued that sugar, "by provisioning, sating—and, indeed, drugging—farm and factory workers, sharply reduced the overall cost of creating and reproducing the metropolitan proletariat." Whether the profits of sugar and slaves built industrial Britain is older and fiercer ground. **[Eric Williams](kloom:e/eric-williams)** argued in **[_Capitalism and Slavery_](kloom:e/capitalism-and-slavery)** (1944) that they helped finance the **[Industrial Revolution](kloom:e/industrial-revolution)**. Stanley Engerman reckoned the profits of the slave trade and the West Indian plantations at under 5 percent of the British economy in any year, and David Richardson the profits of the trade at under 1 percent of domestic investment. Joseph Inikori has answered that the whole Atlantic trade, not the profits alone, drove British manufacturing.
