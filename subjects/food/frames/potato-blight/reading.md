In the late summer of 1845 the potato fields of western Europe turned black. The leaves showed dark spots, the stems collapsed, and the tubers dug from under them were brown and rotting, or rotted in the store after they had seemed sound. The disease had been spreading in the northeastern United States and Canada since 1843; in Europe it appeared first in Belgium and spread quickly across the mainland and then to Britain and Ireland. "The whole of Western Europe, from Norway to Bordeaux, seems almost equally to have suffered," the Reverend **[Miles Joseph Berkeley](kloom:e/miles-joseph-berkeley)** wrote in 1846, and on the speed of it: "in a week or so the greater portion of a field is laid waste." What it did to Ireland belongs to the **[Great Famine](kloom:e/great-famine-ireland)**; this frame is about the disease, and how its cause was found.

## Cause or consequence?

Almost everyone agreed that a mold appeared on the dying leaves. The question was which came first. **[John Lindley](kloom:e/john-lindley)**, the botanist who edited **[_The Gardeners' Chronicle_](kloom:e/the-gardeners-chronicle)**, wrote on 23 August 1845: "The cause of this calamity is, we think, clearly traceable to the season." The weeks before had been cold, wet and sunless, and he reasoned that the plants had taken up more water than they could digest or breathe out, so that it "stagnated in the interior" and they rotted; the mold, on this view, fed on tissue already dying. Others blamed electricity in the air, or a degeneration of potatoes from centuries of growing them from cuttings.

Berkeley, a clergyman and the leading English student of fungi, argued the opposite in the first volume of the _Journal of the Horticultural Society of London_ in 1846. Under the microscope the white bloom on the underside of the leaf was a mold whose threads grew out through the stomata, the breathing pores, from a mycelium that ran through the tissue inside; in a few hours it bore its spores and the patch withered. "The decay is the consequence of the presence of the mould, and not the mould of the decay," he wrote. The weather was a cause only "as they stimulate into action the latent pest", in his words about the atmosphere. But he could not make the spores sprout, and without that he could not prove it.

## De Bary's experiments

The proof came in 1861 from **[Heinrich Anton de Bary](kloom:e/heinrich-anton-de-bary)**, professor of botany at Freiburg, in a short book written for farmers too. He watched the spore cases, the sporangia, in drops of water: in a few hours each split into six to ten spores that swam, settled and sent out a tube that pierced a healthy leaf or stem. He then turned to the tubers, which many still thought rotted for reasons of their own.

| Experiment | What he did                                                                                                                       | What happened                                                                      |
| ---------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Sowing     | Spread sporangia on sound tubers and buried them in damp earth                                                                    | After about 8 days on average, the sown spot was diseased; failures were very rare |
| Control    | Buried matching tubers, treated alike but kept from the spores                                                                    | They stayed sound                                                                  |
| Soil       | Laid halved tubers 1 to 2 and 7 to 8 centimeters under clean white sand from a porcelain works, and spread diseased leaves on top | The spores washed down and the tubers fell ill                                     |

"It is therefore proved by these investigations, which are easy to repeat," he concluded, in our translation, "that the disease of the potato tuber is produced directly by the entry of the fungus, and spread by its further growth, in exactly the same way as the browning of the foliage." He showed too that the spores of related molds from other plants would not infect the potato, nor its spores theirs. A microbe had been shown by experiment to cause a disease of a crop while bad air was still the usual explanation of disease in people; the **[germ theory of disease](kloom:e/germ-theory-of-disease)** took hold in medicine only towards the end of the century, and Robert Koch set out his rules for proving such a cause in 1884. In 1876 he gave the organism the name it still bears, **[_Phytophthora infestans_](kloom:e/phytophthora-infestans)**, "the infesting plant-destroyer".

## What it is, and what stopped it

It is not, after all, a fungus. _Phytophthora_ is an **[oomycete](kloom:e/oomycete)**, a water mold, with walls of cellulose rather than chitin and swimming spores, closer in its evolution to algae and diatoms than to mushrooms. The plate draws its cycle, which on a potato crop can turn in about five days when the air is mild and humid. Its sexual spores, the oospores, form only where its two mating types meet, and the second was not found until the 1950s, in the Toluca Valley of central Mexico.

Chemistry came first from the vineyards. Near Bordeaux, the botanist **[Pierre-Marie-Alexis Millardet](kloom:e/pierre-marie-alexis-millardet)** noticed that vines along the roads kept their leaves through an attack of downy mildew, another oomycete; they had been sprayed with copper sulfate and lime to deter passers-by from picking the grapes. He published the **[Bordeaux mixture](kloom:e/bordeaux-mixture)** in 1885. It works only as a preventive, its copper stopping the spores from sprouting, and potato growers took it up as well: it was in use in northern Maine by 1921.

The famine strain itself was found in 2013. Kentaro Yoshida and his colleagues sequenced the genomes of the mold in eleven dried leaves kept in the herbaria of **[Kew](kloom:e/royal-botanic-gardens-kew)** and Munich, the oldest gathered in England in 1845 and Ireland in 1846. All belonged to one lineage, which they named HERB-1. It persisted for over fifty years and then gave way, in the twentieth century, to a related strain, US-1. Where the mold first came from is still argued. A study of 2014 found that it arose in central Mexico, where its closest relatives live; a larger genomic study of 2016 from Jean Ristaino's laboratory, and later work, point instead to the Andes, the potato's own home.

The trail ends with the potato's most industrial form, the frozen fry.
