In the autumn of 1493 **[Christopher Columbus](kloom:e/christopher-columbus)** sailed west a second time, with seventeen ships, and among the things he carried from the Canary Islands were cuttings of **[sugarcane](kloom:e/sugarcane)**. They were planted on Hispaniola. Ships going the other way carried seeds and tubers that no one in Europe, Africa or Asia had seen. In 1972 the historian **[Alfred W. Crosby](kloom:e/alfred-w-crosby)** gave the whole traffic a name, the **[Columbian exchange](kloom:e/columbian-exchange)**: the crops, animals, weeds and germs that crossed the Atlantic once the two halves of the world were in steady contact. Looking at the two lists of crops, he wrote, "the difference … was, with the possible exception of cotton, absolute." Each hemisphere had domesticated its own plants, and none of the staples was shared.

## What crossed

| Crop or animal       | Domesticated in           | Where it took hold after 1492                      |
| -------------------- | ------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------- |
| Maize                | Mexico                    | Southern and eastern Africa, China, northern Italy |
| Potato               | The Andes                 | Northern and eastern Europe, Ireland               |
| Cassava (manioc)     | South America             | Central and West Africa, Southeast Asia            |
| Sweet potato         | Central and South America | China, East Africa, the Pacific                    |
| Chiles, tomatoes     | Mexico and South America  | India, Hungary, Korea, Sichuan, Italy              |
| Cacao                | South and Central America | West Africa, which now grows most of it            |
| Wheat, barley, oats  | Southwest Asia            | The Argentine pampas, the North American plains    |
| Sugarcane            | New Guinea                | Brazil and the Caribbean                           |
| Rice                 | Asia and West Africa      | The Carolinas, Cuba, Brazil                        |
| Coffee               | Ethiopia                  | Brazil and Colombia                                |
| Cattle, pigs, horses | Eurasia                   | Every American grassland                           |

New World foods now feed much of the Old. In the figures for 2000 that the economists **[Nathan Nunn](kloom:e/nathan-nunn)** and **[Nancy Qian](kloom:e/nancy-qian)** gathered from the Food and Agriculture Organization, the average person in Lesotho ate 1,508 calories a day of **[maize](kloom:e/maize)**, and in the Democratic Republic of the Congo 925 of **[cassava](kloom:e/cassava)**. The ten countries that ate most cassava were all in Africa. [Wheat](kloom:e/wheat) went the other way, and so did the great grazing animals, which the Americas had almost none of: of the animals on Crosby's American list, only the llama was large, and it could not be ridden.

## A crop crossed without its know-how

A plant can cross an ocean in a sack. What the people who bred it knew about eating it often stayed behind. Maize and bitter cassava both need work before they are safe to live on:

| Crop           | The danger                                             | How the Americas handled it                                          | What happened without it                                   |
| -------------- | ------------------------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------- |
| Maize          | Its niacin is bound up where the body cannot use it    | _Nixtamalization_: the grain is cooked in water with lime, an alkali | Pellagra, described in Spain in 1735 and epidemic in Italy |
| Bitter cassava | Its root holds compounds that release hydrogen cyanide | Peel, grate, soak and ferment the pulp, press it, then dry or roast  | Cyanide poisoning, and the paralysis called konzo          |

West Africans learned to soak grated cassava for two or three days, which removes 85 to 99 percent of its cyanide, and to roast it into _garri_, much as Brazilians make _farofa_. Maize mostly crossed without the lime. Where poor farmers ate little else, in Asturias, northern Italy and later the American South, [pellagra](kloom:e/pellagra) followed, and its cause was not understood until 1937.

The exchange also carried disease. In the Americas there had been no **[smallpox](kloom:e/smallpox)**, measles, influenza, typhus or malaria. Smallpox was first recorded there in 1518. Estimates of how many people lived in the Americas in 1492 run from about 8 million to over 110 million. A review of 119 regional estimates in 2019 put it at about 60.5 million, and the loss at 90 percent within a century. Hunger followed the sickness. A Nahua account collected by the friar Bernardino de Sahagún describes the smallpox that struck the capital of the **[Aztec Empire](kloom:e/aztec-empire)** in 1520, in James Lockhart's translation: "very many people died of them, and many just starved to death; starvation reigned, and no one took care of others any longer." The fields of the dead went untended, and the Spanish and Portuguese turned to Africa for labor, the story of the next frame.

## How many more mouths?

Crosby argued that the meeting of the continents was "a prerequisite for the population explosion of the past two centuries". Nunn and Qian tried to measure it for the **[potato](kloom:e/potato)**. They compared regions of the Old World whose soils and climate suited the potato with regions that did not, before and after it was adopted. In 2010 they put the potato's share of the growth in average population at 12 percent. Their full study, published in 2011, puts it at about a quarter of the growth in Old World population and urbanization between 1700 and 1900, by its most conservative estimates.

Others who used the same method found that more food did not always mean better lives. Shuo Chen and James Kung found that maize accounted for an increase of nearly 19 percent in China's population between 1776 and 1910, but did not raise income per head, and did little for wages or the growth of cities: more people, living no better. In Africa, Jevan Cherniwchan and Juan Moreno-Cruz found that maize raised population density and, with it, the number of people sold into the [Atlantic slave trade](kloom:e/atlantic-slave-trade). Whether the new crops enriched the Old World or only filled it is still argued.

The potato's own story, from the high Andes to the Irish famine, is told by the trail that branches from here. The main spine goes on to the crop that, more than any other, drove the Atlantic slave trade: sugar.
