For a quarter of a century after Landsteiner found the blood groups, nobody knew how they were inherited, though they were, in the words of the geneticist James Crow, the only good genetic marker in people. The answer came from a mathematician who never needed to type a family. In 1924 **[Felix Bernstein](kloom:e/felix-bernstein-mathematician)**, director of the Institute of Mathematical Statistics at the **[University of Göttingen](kloom:e/university-of-gottingen)**, showed from the counts of the four groups in whole populations that ABO is one gene with three forms, and not two genes, as everyone had believed since 1910. Crow called it the first human marker locus.

## The wrong model

In 1910 **[Emil von Dungern](kloom:e/emil-von-dungern)** and **[Ludwik Hirszfeld](kloom:e/ludwik-hirszfeld)**, at Heidelberg, typed 72 families and found that a child never carried A or B unless a parent did. They explained it with two independent genes, each in two forms: a dominant _A_ that makes the A antigen and a recessive _a_ that does not, and likewise _B_ and _b_. Group O was _aabb_; AB needed at least one _A_ and one _B_. The model fitted their families, and it was widely accepted until 1924.

## A census as an experiment

Bernstein's tool was a rule that **[G. H. Hardy](kloom:e/g-h-hardy)**, the Cambridge number theorist, had set out in a letter to _Science_ in 1908, calling it mathematics "of the multiplication-table type", and that the Stuttgart physician **[Wilhelm Weinberg](kloom:e/wilhelm-weinberg)** had published independently the same year. In a large population mating at random, the shares of the forms of a gene, its _alleles_, fix the shares of the genotypes: if a gene has forms with frequencies _p_ and _q_, the three genotypes come in proportions _p_², 2*pq* and _q_², generation after generation. This is the **[Hardy–Weinberg principle](kloom:e/hardy-weinberg-principle)**. Bernstein used it to make each model predict a relation among the four group frequencies that any census could test, without a single family.

- **Two genes.** Carrying A and carrying B are independent, so the share of people who carry both must be the product of the shares who carry each: AB = (A + AB) × (B + AB).
- **One gene, three alleles**, with frequencies _p_ for A, _q_ for B and _r_ for O. Group O is _r_², so _r_ = √O; people without the A allele, groups O and B, make up (_q_ + _r_)², so _p_ = 1 − √(O + B); likewise _q_ = 1 − √(O + A). The three must add to one: [1 − √(O + B)] + [1 − √(O + A)] + √O = 1.

Crow's account works it with one population from Bernstein's 1925 paper, 502 Japanese living in Korea: O 0.294, A 0.422, B 0.206 and AB 0.078. For two genes, (0.422 + 0.078) × (0.206 + 0.078) = 0.500 × 0.284 = 0.142, nearly twice the AB actually seen. For three alleles, 0.293 + 0.154 + 0.542 = 0.989, close to one. We turned both models into expected counts, by our arithmetic, scaling the three allele frequencies to add to exactly one:

Crow, redoing the comparison in 1993 by modern likelihood, found the three-allele model favored by odds of some hundreds of millions to one. Population after population in Bernstein's 1925 survey of the world's counts, which had been gathering since the Hirszfelds' wartime survey of soldiers in 1919, fitted three alleles.

## What it changed

The two models disagree about families. Under two genes an AB parent who carries _a_ and _b_ as well can have an O child; under three alleles an AB parent gives every child an A or a B, so never an O, and an O parent never has an AB child. In America Laurence Snyder had been puzzled that his O × AB families gave only A and B children; his 200 families, published in 1926, upheld Bernstein, and the rules became exclusions in paternity cases. Some families seemed to break them: in Alexander Wiener's summary of the published data for 1924 to 1932 there were 10 such exceptions among 3,205 children. Supporters of two genes cited them; they are now put down to typing errors and children whose father was not the man named.

Today the **[ABO blood group system](kloom:e/abo-blood-group-system)** is known as a gene on chromosome 9 whose alleles make different forms of an enzyme that adds sugars to the red cell's surface, and sequencing has found many more alleles, each of which still types as A, B or O. Crow, who wondered why no one had done this sooner when both the rule and multiple alleles had been known for a decade, suspected the First World War.

Bernstein was a mathematician first. Born in Halle in 1878, he attended Georg Cantor's seminar as a schoolboy, proved the theorem on equivalent sets now named for Cantor and him while still a teenager (at 18 in Crow's telling, 19 by another account), and took his doctorate under David Hilbert in 1901. In 1933, while on a lecture tour in the United States, he was dismissed from Göttingen under the Nazi civil-service law; he taught at Columbia and New York University, returned to Europe after the war, and died in Zurich in 1956.
