In April 1935, at a meeting of biochemists in Detroit, **[Armand Quick](kloom:e/armand-j-quick)**, an assistant professor of pharmacology at **[Marquette University](kloom:e/marquette-university)** in Milwaukee, described a test that took a few drops of plasma, a pinch of dried rabbit brain and a stopwatch. With two surgeons of New York's Fifth Avenue Hospital, Margaret Stanley-Brown and Frederic Bancroft, he published it that October. It measured, he said, how much _prothrombin_ the blood holds, and he called it a new prothrombin test. It became the one-stage **[prothrombin time](kloom:e/prothrombin-time)**, and nine decades on it is still the test by which **[warfarin](kloom:e/warfarin)** is dosed.

## Hold everything else still

Quick began from the textbook theory of the day, in two steps. Prothrombin, **[thromboplastin](kloom:e/thromboplastin)** and calcium make **[thrombin](kloom:e/thrombin)**; thrombin turns the soluble fibrinogen of the plasma into a fibrin clot. Fibrinogen, he showed, barely changes the clotting time, and calcium can be added in a fixed, ample dose. Thromboplastin, the clotting substance of the tissues, he supplied in excess, and showed that past a point more of it did nothing: a plasma that clotted in 110 seconds alone clotted in 40 with 0.002 cc of his rabbit-brain emulsion and in 22 seconds with anything from 0.01 to 0.2 cc. With calcium fixed and thromboplastin in excess, prothrombin was the only thing left to vary, and the clotting time measured it.

## By hand

The test as Quick wrote it, with the details the Army's 1951 laboratory manual added for its technicians:

| Step | What is done                                                                                            | Why                                             |
| ---- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------- |
| 1    | Draw 9 cc of blood quickly, "with special precaution to avoid trauma", into 1 cc of M/10 sodium oxalate | the oxalate binds calcium, so nothing clots yet |
| 2    | Spin at low speed for 5 minutes                                                                         | to take off the plasma                          |
| 3    | Put 0.1 cc of plasma and 0.1 cc of thromboplastin in a dry, clean tube, 13 by 100 mm                    | thromboplastin in excess                        |
| 4    | Add M/40 calcium chloride, start the stopwatch, shake, and stand the tube in a water bath at 37 °C      | calcium restores clotting; body heat            |
| 5    | Tip the tube every few seconds until the plasma no longer flows: a firm clot                            | the end point                                   |
| 6    | Report the time with the time of a normal plasma beside it                                              | reagents differ                                 |

Quick's paper adds 0.1 cc of calcium chloride; the Army manual of 1951 adds 0.25 cc. His thromboplastin was the brain of a freshly killed rabbit, ground, dried at 37 °C, and made into an emulsion in salt solution; any batch that did not clot normal plasma in 22 to 25 seconds was thrown away. Today the blood goes into sodium citrate, one part to nine of blood, and a laboratory may refuse a tube that is underfilled, because the extra citrate lengthens the time. Spinning had mattered to the older test, Howell's recalcification time, whose result Quick found could triple when a hard spin took out the platelets; his excess of thromboplastin was meant to swamp theirs.

The test answered to dilution as a measuring instrument should. Plasma diluted with salt water or with plasma stripped of its prothrombin clotted more slowly in step, where the older test barely moved:

![Bar chart of Quick's 1935 prothrombin times as normal plasma was diluted: undiluted 23 seconds, four-fifths 30, three-fifths 35, two-fifths 45, one-fifth 65](dilution.svg)

| Plasma in the mixture | 100% | 80% | 60% | 40% | 20% |
| --------------------- | ---: | --: | --: | --: | --: |
| Quick's test, seconds |   23 |  30 |  35 |  45 |  65 |
| Older test, seconds   |  120 | 110 | 115 | 115 | 150 |

It also drew a line between two bleeding disorders. The plasma of six patients with **[hemophilia](kloom:e/haemophilia)**, which took 205 to 405 seconds to clot on its own, clotted in 22 to 24 seconds with excess thromboplastin: their prothrombin was normal, and the prothrombin time cannot see hemophilia. In jaundice, prothrombin was low, and one patient's plasma took 90 seconds. Within a few years the test was measuring the new anticoagulants that work by lowering prothrombin.

## Why the INR

Quick's own paper held the warning. His normal plasma clotted in 22 to 25 seconds with brain from New York rabbits; brain from Milwaukee rabbits clotted it in 16 or 17. A clotting time depends on its thromboplastin, and so does the ratio of a patient's time to a normal one, so the same blood gave different answers in different laboratories. In 1983 Tom Kirkwood of Britain's National Institute for Biological Standards and Control proposed the fix the **[World Health Organization](kloom:e/world-health-organization)** adopted: calibrate each batch of thromboplastin against an international reference, give it an _International Sensitivity Index_ (ISI), and report

INR = (patient's time ÷ mean normal time) ^ ISI.

A worked example, with invented numbers. One patient's plasma goes to two laboratories:

| Laboratory | Reagent's ISI | Mean normal time | Patient's time | Ratio |  INR |
| ---------- | ------------: | ---------------: | -------------: | ----: | ---: |
| A          |           1.0 |           12.0 s |         30.0 s |  2.50 | 2.50 |
| B          |           2.3 |           13.0 s |         19.4 s |  1.49 | 2.50 |

The blood is the same and so is the INR, but laboratory B's insensitive reagent makes the ratio look mild. A physician dosing to a ratio of 2.0 to 2.5 at laboratory B would push the patient, by our arithmetic, to an INR of 4.9 to 8.2. A 1992 survey of American hospital laboratories found reagents with ISIs from 1.2 to 2.8 and only one laboratory in five reporting an INR, and concluded that anticoagulation in the United States was being managed inappropriately.

As of October 2026 the routine test is done by machines, perhaps 800 million times a year, but Quick's tube has not gone. The sixth international standard thromboplastin, reported in 2026, was given its ISI of 1.08 by eight people in four laboratories timing clots with the manual tilt-tube method, kept as the reference procedure precisely because it belongs to no machine. The next frame follows the drug the test was made to steer, warfarin, from the sweet clover that killed cattle.
