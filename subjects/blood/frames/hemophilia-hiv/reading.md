On 16 July 1982 the **[Centers for Disease Control](kloom:e/centers-for-disease-control-and-prevention)** reported three men with severe **[hemophilia](kloom:e/haemophilia)** A who had come down with _Pneumocystis_ pneumonia, an infection of people whose immune defenses have failed. Two had died. None had another disease or a treatment that suppresses immunity; what they shared was years of frequent injections of **[factor VIII](kloom:e/factor-viii)** concentrate. The report's editorial note set out what that meant. The concentrate was made "from plasma pools collected from as many as a thousand or more donors", and the three cases suggested "the possible transmission of an agent through blood products". The agent was the virus later named **[HIV](kloom:e/hiv)**, and by then it was already in the pools.

## A treatment that worked

Freeze-dried factor VIII concentrate was the best thing that had happened to people with hemophilia. Unlike **[cryoprecipitate](kloom:e/cryoprecipitate)**, which came from one donor or a small pool of ten to fifteen, it was a powder of known strength that kept in a refrigerator and could be injected at home at the first sign of a bleed. Home infusion, said the US Institute of Medicine's 1995 report, nearly eliminated the crippling joint damage the disease had caused. Life expectancy with hemophilia, about 40 years in 1941–60, had reached about 60 by 1971–80.

The price was the pool. Concentrate was made from batches of up to 10,000 liters of plasma from 1,000 to 20,000 or more donors, much of it bought from paid donors. The UK's **[Infected Blood Inquiry](kloom:e/infected-blood-scandal-in-the-united-kingdom)** found commercial pools of from about 1,500 to about 30,000 donors, and the National Health Service's own at 2,000 donations in 1977, 5,000 by 1980 and 10,000 in March 1985. The danger was known before AIDS. Hepatitis came with the product: by 1976, 80 percent of people with hemophilia in the United States carried antibody to hepatitis B. In 1975, reporting an outbreak of hepatitis at Bournemouth, the British doctor John Craske warned that "the risk is greatly increased with factor-VIII concentrates prepared from pools of more than a thousand donations."

## Why the pool multiplied the risk

Suppose, inventing the number, that one donation in 10,000 carries a virus that no test can yet find. A pool of _N_ donations is clean only if every one of them is, so by our arithmetic the chance that it holds at least one infected donation is 1 − (1 − 1/10,000)ᴺ.

One bad donation spreads through the whole lot, every vial of it. A person with severe hemophilia might use vials from many lots in a year; four lots from pools of 20,000, at this invented rate, would make the chance of at least one exposure better than 99.9 percent. ## What was known, and when

| When          | What                                                                                          |
| ------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| May 1981      | Behringwerke's pasteurized factor VIII, heated against hepatitis, licensed in West Germany    |
| July 1982     | the CDC's three cases                                                                         |
| March 1983    | US Public Health Service asks people at high risk of AIDS not to give blood or plasma         |
| by April 1983 | the first heat-treated concentrate licensed in the United States, also made against hepatitis |
| October 1984  | the CDC reports that heat treatment inactivates the AIDS virus                                |
| by March 1985 | two antibody tests for the virus licensed for screening donations                             |
| 1989          | the FDA requires the recall of all untreated concentrate                                      |

Heat-treated concentrate was dearer, Behringwerke's about ten times the price, and doctors feared it might provoke inhibitors, antibodies against the factor; many doctors, the Institute of Medicine found, were slow to urge their patients to switch. It judged that large numbers of people with hemophilia were infected between July 1983 and 1985, and that heat treatment against hepatitis might have been developed before 1980. Wikipedia, drawing on the _New York Times_ in 2003, reports that Cutter sold unheated concentrate in Asia and Latin America into 1985 after it had a heated product for the West.

## The toll

About half of the 16,000 people with hemophilia in the United States were infected with HIV. In the United Kingdom about 1,250 people with bleeding disorders were, 380 of them children; almost all also caught **[hepatitis C](kloom:e/hepatitis-c)**, and three quarters of the 1,250 have died. Before there were effective drugs for HIV, most of those infected died. At Treloar's, a school at Alton with a hemophilia center of its own, a great majority of the pupils of the early 1980s were infected with HIV; the inquiry found they had often been regarded "as objects for research", and that only about 30 of the 122 pupils with hemophilia there between 1970 and 1987 survive. Its report of May 2024 concluded that the disaster "could largely, though not entirely, have been avoided". In France the director of the national transfusion center, Michel Garretta, was sentenced to four years in prison, and in 1999 a court found a former health minister guilty without penalty. In 1997 four American makers agreed to pay $660 million to more than 6,000 people with hemophilia. The trail's last frame follows the factor made with no donor at all.
