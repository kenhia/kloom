In 1915 a medical student who had paid his own way across the country by mining gold and drilling oil wells walked into the physiology laboratory at [Johns Hopkins](kloom:e/johns-hopkins-university) in Baltimore and asked for a problem he could finish alone in one year. He was **[Jay McLean](kloom:e/jay-mclean)**, and the professor was **[William Henry Howell](kloom:e/william-henry-howell)**, whose laboratory had studied blood clotting for years. Howell set him to find what in the tissues makes blood clot. What McLean found, in extracts of liver, was something that stopped it: the first form of **[heparin](kloom:e/heparin)**, the anticoagulant still given in hospitals around the world.

## Eight drops of plasma

Howell believed the clotting agent of the tissues was _cephalin_, a fatty substance extracted from brain, and he wanted to know whether it worked itself or through an impurity. McLean made cephalin and its relatives from brain, heart and liver by the laboratory's recipe: grind the organ, dry it in thin layers on glass plates, extract it with ether, and precipitate the extract with acetone and alcohol. He then tested each one by the method Howell's laboratory used. Fresh serum and fresh plasma were drawn from the same dog or cat, the plasma kept from clotting with oxalate and spun clear. A few drops of serum would clot the plasma slowly, and anything that hurried or hindered clotting showed in the time. His paper of 1916 sets out the tubes:

| Tube    | Oxalated plasma | Serum   | Added                    | Result in his paper           |
| ------- | --------------- | ------- | ------------------------ | ----------------------------- |
| Control | 8 drops         | 3 drops | water, 3 drops           | clot in 30 minutes to 2 hours |
| Test    | 8 drops         | 3 drops | cephalin (0.1%), 3 drops | solid clot in about a minute  |
| Heart   | 8 drops         | 3 drops | heart cephalin, 3 drops  | solid clot in 3 minutes       |
| Heart   | 8 drops         | 3 drops | cuorin, 3 drops          | not clotted in 6 hours        |

The last line, with the control beside it clotting in nine minutes, is the discovery in its first published form. _Cuorin_ from heart and _heparphosphatid_ from liver, two fatty substances named by German chemists, did not speed clotting at all. Purified, they slowed it. McLean's paper, about cephalin, gave this a few sentences and promised that the liver substance's "anticoagulating action" would be "reported upon later".

## His telling

McLean's own account was written in the 1950s and printed in _Circulation_ in 1959, after his death; it breaks off mid-story. In his telling he kept old batches of each extract to see whether they decayed as brain cephalin did, and two of the earliest, tested months later, not only failed to clot plasma but held it back, liver more than heart. He went to Howell's door and said, "Dr. Howell, I have discovered antithrombin." Howell suspected salt. So the laboratory's attendant bled a cat, McLean stirred a batch of liver extract into a beaker of its blood and left it on Howell's table, and asked to be told when it clotted. "It never did clot."

Howell and Emmett Holt published in 1918 a method for making the substance from dog liver, which they named heparin, from the Greek for liver, and they described it as a phosphatid, the same family as cephalin. Their paper says that attention was first called to it by McLean's work. By the early 1920s Howell was making a different, water-soluble heparin. Howell's unit of the drug was the amount that kept a millilitre of cat's blood fluid for 24 hours at 0 °C.

Who discovered heparin is still argued. In the 1940s McLean began to say in lectures and letters that the discovery was his, and in 1963 a plaque at Johns Hopkins said so. The Canadian biochemist Louis Jaques replied that McLean's anticoagulant was a fatty phospholipid and the drug a sugar-like polysaccharide, so he had found something else; the historian James Marcum's study of the papers sets the 1916 work back inside Howell's laboratory and its theories. A 1994 review in _Circulation_ splits the difference: McLean's impure material "differed somewhat from today's heparin".

## From dog liver to the ward

Dog liver gave too little, too impure, to inject into people. From 1928 **[Charles Best](kloom:e/charles-best-medical-scientist)**, the co-discoverer of insulin, set a team at the University of Toronto's **[Connaught Laboratories](kloom:e/connaught-laboratories)** to change that. Arthur Charles and David Scott moved from beef liver to beef lung and intestine, which they let spoil before extraction, and between 1933 and 1936 purified heparin into a standard dry powder given in salt solution. Trials in patients, led by the surgeon Gordon Murray, began in May 1935. In Stockholm Erik Jorpes published on its chemistry in 1935, and a Swedish company launched a heparin for injection into a vein in 1936. Heparin works by speeding up antithrombin, a natural brake on clotting in the plasma, much as Howell had guessed in 1918 when he said it acted through a substance already in the blood.

It is still made from animals, and in the United States only from pigs, and in 2008 that caught up with it. In January 2008 the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention began tracing a cluster of allergic-type reactions; it found 152 in 113 patients in 13 states between November 2007 and January 2008, most during dialysis, and traced them to heparin made by **[Baxter](kloom:e/baxter-international)** from raw material processed in China. The vials held _oversulfated chondroitin sulfate_, a cheap chemical cousin that passed for heparin in the tests then in use. The FDA linked 81 deaths to the contamination; the CDC's authors noted 238 deaths reported after heparin by May 2008 and that none had been shown to be caused by it.
