Nearly every unit of blood transfused today still begins in a donor's arm. In its fact sheet of June 2026, drawn from 168 countries' reports, mostly for 2023, the **[World Health Organization](kloom:e/world-health-organization)** counts about 120.4 million donations of blood a year. They are not shared evenly. High-income countries, home to 15 per cent of the world's people, collect 36 per cent of them, and 55 countries, all of low or middle income and 36 of them in Africa, collect fewer than 10 donations per 1,000 people. In high-income countries blood mostly supports heart and transplant surgery, major trauma and cancer care; in poorer ones it is used more often for bleeding in pregnancy and for children with severe anemia.

A donation goes furthest when it is split. Spun hard, a bag of donated blood settles into packed red cells below, a thin pale layer of white cells and **[platelets](kloom:e/platelet)**, and plasma above, and each part can go to a different patient. The plate draws one donation after the spin, with the tubing that carries its plasma and platelets off to satellite bags.

## Using less

In the United States the government's National Blood Collection and Utilization Survey counted 11,586,000 units of red cells collected in 2023 and 10,328,000 transfused. In 2015 the count transfused had been 11,349,000, itself 13.9 per cent below the count of 2013: by our arithmetic a further fall of 9 per cent in eight years.

Much of the fall was chosen. A trial published in 1999 assigned 838 critically ill patients either to a restrictive rule, transfusing red cells when hemoglobin fell below 7 grams per deciliter, or to a liberal one at 10. Deaths within 30 days were 18.7 per cent with the restrictive rule and 23.3 per cent with the liberal, a difference that could have been chance: less blood did no harm. Trials like it, 45 of them with 20,599 adults by 2023, led the AABB's international guideline of that year to recommend considering transfusion for a stable adult in hospital when hemoglobin falls below 7. **[Patient blood management](kloom:e/patient-blood-management)**, a term first used in 2005, builds a practice around it: find and treat a patient's anemia before surgery, lose less blood during it, salvage and return what is lost, and transfuse by a rule rather than by habit.

## Treating the blood

Testing finds only what is tested for. Pathogen reduction treats the component instead: most methods add a chemical that light turns against nucleic acids, so that a virus, bacterium or white cell in the bag can no longer copy its DNA or RNA. Amotosalen with ultraviolet A light is licensed for platelets, plasma and cryoprecipitate, and riboflavin with ultraviolet light is applied to plasma, platelets and unseparated blood in some countries. In 2023 the United States transfused 1,258,000 pathogen-reduced platelet units, by our arithmetic 57 per cent of all platelets transfused. Red cells, the component transfused most, are still in trials.

## Making blood

The old hope is to do without a donor. Solutions of hemoglobin, free of red cells, needed no matching or refrigeration, and several reached large trials. In 2008 a meta-analysis of 16 trials of five such **[blood substitutes](kloom:e/blood-substitute)**, with 3,711 patients, found that they raised the risk of death by 30 per cent and of heart attack nearly threefold, whatever the product. Hemoglobin wrapped in tiny fat vesicles, made in Japan from expired donated blood, was first given to healthy volunteers in 2022, in doses far below a transfusion.

The other road is to grow red cells. On 7 November 2022 **[NHS Blood and Transplant](kloom:e/nhs-blood-and-transplant)** announced that two volunteers in its RESTORE trial had received red cells grown in its Bristol laboratory from donors' stem cells, about 5 to 10 milliliters each, one or two teaspoons, labeled so that their survival could be compared with the same donor's ordinary red cells. A review published in 2026 reports that the grown cells survived about as well as native ones, with no adverse reactions, and that the trial's last batch was made in February 2026, with its full results still to come.

## What is still unsolved

Scale. The same review puts a unit of red cells at about 2 × 10¹² cells and the best feeder-free bioreactor cultures at up to 2 × 10⁸, ten thousand times fewer by our arithmetic, at a high cost in growth factors. Grown cells may first serve patients with rare blood groups, for whom matching donors are scarce. For everyone else, as RESTORE's own investigators said, the need for ordinary donations will remain. Military medicine, meanwhile, has brought **[whole blood](kloom:e/whole-blood)** back to trauma care, the last frame of the _Blood at war_ trail.
