Until the spring of 1965 one American hospital in Vietnam, the 8th Field Hospital, gave every transfusion the American forces needed, and every ten days ten units of blood arrived for it from Japan. By February 1969 the forces in Vietnam were using 38,000 units a month. The history of the **[Vietnam War](kloom:e/vietnam-war)** blood programme, written for the Army by **[Spurgeon Neel](kloom:e/spurgeon-neel)**, in 1965 the surgeon of the American command in Vietnam, called the system that grew to meet that demand the largest blood distribution system ever run by a single organisation. Almost all of what follows is from his account, published in 1973.

## From a donor to a field hospital

Liquid whole blood, kept in the anticoagulant ACD, was then good for 21 days from the donor's arm, and the planners' first principle was that it had to be watched by blood bank people the whole way, never handled as an ordinary supply item. Until July 1966 most of it came from the 406th Medical Laboratory at **[Camp Zama](kloom:e/camp-zama)** in Japan, whose bleeding teams drew donors in Japan, Korea, Okinawa and Taiwan and at the naval base at Yokosuka when the fleet was in. In June 1966 the Pacific's donors ran out, and the request went to the Military Blood Program Agency for 1,000 units of low-titre group O blood a week. The first shipment from the United States, 2,036 units, reached Japan that July. From then on, blood drawn across the United States was gathered at **[McGuire Air Force Base](kloom:e/mcguire-air-force-base)** in New Jersey, and the chain ran like this:

The central blood bank had moved to **[Cam Ranh Bay](kloom:e/cam-ranh-bay)** because, after the **[Tet Offensive](kloom:e/tet-offensive)** of 1968, the command feared that another attack might close the airfield at Tan Son Nhut and cut Saigon off. The new building had a walk-in refrigerator of 1,800 cubic feet. The blood travelled in a box that Major William Collins of the 406th designed in late 1965: a styrofoam insert in a cardboard carton, holding 18 units in wet ice for 48 hours and weighing 40 pounds full. It replaced a returnable Hollinger box that weighed 115 pounds with 24 units and cost $100; the Collins box cost $1.40 and was thrown away, or kept by soldiers and Vietnamese civilians as an icebox. The plate draws the route on a map of the Pacific, and below it the 21 days a unit had to make the journey.

## Universal blood, then type-specific blood

At first only universal-donor low-titre group O blood was sent to Vietnam, because with it a patient could not be given the wrong group. But fewer than 45 percent of donors had it. Group A blood arrived in December 1965, and blood of every group in January 1966. Clearing companies and forward surgical hospitals, which could not crossmatch, kept to group O; evacuation hospitals gave type-specific blood almost exclusively. Neel gives the rule for a casualty who had already been given group O: after four or more units he stayed on group O, and with fewer he was matched at the hospital. Whether more could do harm was not settled; a study at Walter Reed of men given 21 to 44 units of group O was inconclusive. Between March 1967 and June 1969 about 364,900 transfusions were recorded, with 38 hemolytic reactions, about one in 9,600, and 979 others, one in 370.

## Twenty-one days

Blood was usually four to seven days old when it reached Vietnam, and requisitions from distant subdepots could never be filled at once, so much of it expired. On average about 29 percent went out of date unused, and in lulls in the fighting as much as half. Most units 21 to 31 days old went to South Vietnamese hospitals, and older blood, destroyed at first, was later made into freeze-dried plasma. "Some waste of blood was the price that had to be paid," Neel wrote, so that no soldier died for want of it. From April 1968 **[fresh frozen plasma](kloom:e/fresh-frozen-plasma)**, drawn by plasmapheresis from group AB donors at Camp Zama, went to Vietnam for the bleeding that followed massive transfusion, and in 1969 blood kept 40 days with adenine was tried at Long Binh.

The Navy tried a way around the calendar. In 1966 its Bureau of Medicine and Surgery opened the first frozen blood bank in a combat zone, at the Naval Station Hospital in Da Nang, and over seven months it transfused 465 units. Group O, Rh-negative red cells were mixed with **[glycerol](kloom:e/glycerol)** to protect them from ice, frozen in Boston, flown out and kept at −80 °C for four to eight months, then thawed and washed free of the glycerol. Reporting on 36 casualties who were given them, Gerald Moss, Robert Valeri and Charles Brodine judged a frozen blood bank workable in a combat zone and "a satisfactory alternative to walking donors". Those donors, called to the hospital when the stored blood ran short, are the next frame's subject.
