An antibody test can only find an infection that the body has already answered. In the weeks before a newly infected donor makes antibody, the **[window period](kloom:e/window-period)**, the donation passes every serological test and can still infect. By the mid-1990s, with the antibody tests improved, nearly all the **[HIV](kloom:e/hiv)** still passed on by screened blood came from donations given in that window, and **[hepatitis C](kloom:e/hepatitis-c)**, whose window was longer, was a larger risk. A study of more than two million donations in 1991–93 put the chance that a screened unit carried HIV at about 1 in 493,000, and hepatitis C at about 1 in 103,000. The way to close the window was to look for the virus itself.

## Looking for the virus's own RNA

A **[nucleic acid test](kloom:e/nucleic-acid-test)** looks for a stretch of the virus's genetic material, which is in the blood days or weeks before antibody is. There is far too little of it to see, so the test first copies it, many millions of times. In the middle of 1999 the main blood programs of the United States began testing donations for the RNA of HIV and hepatitis C as an investigational test, under protocols of the **[Food and Drug Administration](kloom:e/food-and-drug-administration)**, before any such test had been licensed. Testing every donation alone was not yet feasible, so they tested pools.

| Step       | What is done                                                                                                                                                |
| ---------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1. Pool    | a portion of plasma from each of 16 to 24 donations is mixed into one minipool                                                                              |
| 2. Extract | the virus particles in the pool are concentrated and broken open, and their RNA is captured; Roche's test adds a known control RNA to prove each run worked |
| 3. Amplify | the chosen stretch of RNA is copied: by PCR in Roche's test, by _transcription-mediated amplification_ in Gen-Probe's                                       |
| 4. Detect  | a labeled probe that binds only the copies gives a signal, read against a threshold                                                                         |
| 5. Resolve | a reactive pool is taken apart: its sub-pools or its single samples are tested again until the one donation that carries the virus is found                 |
| 6. Release | donations in pools that stayed clean go to hospitals; the reactive one is discarded and its donor deferred                                                  |

The plate draws the pool and its resolution. The two amplifications differ. The **[polymerase chain reaction](kloom:e/polymerase-chain-reaction)**, for RNA first copied into DNA, doubles its target with every cycle of heating and cooling. **[Transcription-mediated amplification](kloom:e/transcription-mediated-amplification)** works at one temperature: a reverse transcriptase makes DNA from the RNA, and an RNA polymerase makes 100 to 1,000 RNA copies from each DNA, which go round again. The FDA requires that a reactive pool be taken apart down to the single donation. In its descriptions of the companies' later tests, Gen-Probe's pooled up to 16 samples for transfusion and Roche's up to 24.

Pooling costs sensitivity, because it dilutes. Roche's HIV test of 2001 detected 25 copies of HIV RNA per milliliter of pooled plasma in at least 95 per cent of runs, and its makers wrote that it would catch one infected donation carrying 600 copies per milliliter in a pool with 23 clean ones. By our arithmetic that is the same number: 600 divided by 24 is 25. A donation with fewer copies, early in an infection, can read clean in its pool and positive alone. The virus multiplies fast in those first days, so the cost is counted in days.

## What the pools found

Susan Stramer and her colleagues counted the first three years. Among 37,164,054 donations that had passed every antibody test, 12 carried HIV RNA, 1 in 3.1 million, and only 2 of those would have been caught by the p24 antigen test then used. Among 39,721,404 donations, 170 carried hepatitis C RNA, 1 in 230,000; donors traced afterwards made antibody a median of 35 days after the donation. The authors estimated that minipool testing was preventing about 5 HIV and 56 hepatitis C infections a year, and that it had brought the risk from each to about 1 in 2 million units.

That is now the order of the risk that is left. For 2007–08 a later study put it at about 1 in 1.5 million donations for HIV and 1 in 1.1 million for hepatitis C, and a 2019 review put the risk per unit below 1 in a million in the United States and other high-income countries. The pools have shrunk as machines have grown: one test licensed later pools only six donations, and screening has added hepatitis B, West Nile and Zika viruses. The window has not closed. A donation in the first days of an infection, before any test can find it, can still infect. What remains between it and a patient is the donor's answers to the questions and, more and more, treating the blood itself to kill what no test has found, which the frame on blood today describes.
