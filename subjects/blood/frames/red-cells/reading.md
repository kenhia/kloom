On 7 April 1674 a draper and town official of **[Delft](kloom:e/delft)** wrote to **[Henry Oldenburg](kloom:e/henry-oldenburg)**, the secretary of the **[Royal Society](kloom:e/royal-society)** in London: "I have divers times endeavoured to see and to know, what parts the Blood consists of; and at length I have observ'd, taking some Blood out of my own hand, that it consists of small round globuls driven thorough a Crystalline humidity or water". The writer was **[Antonie van Leeuwenhoek](kloom:e/antonie-van-leeuwenhoek)**. He wrote only in Dutch, and Oldenburg translated and printed his letters in the Society's _Philosophical Transactions_. The globules are what we call **[red blood cells](kloom:e/red-blood-cell)**, and his letters of 1674 describe them, say how he looked at them, and estimate their size.

## A lens on a plate

Leeuwenhoek made his own microscopes, each a single tiny lens. In 1753 the naturalist Henry Baker described the twenty-six that Leeuwenhoek had left to the Royal Society, all alike. Two thin silver plates, riveted together, hold a very small double-convex lens, with a hole through each plate to look through. The specimen is glued to the point of a pin on a little stage behind the lens; a long screw raises or lowers the stage, a small one moves it nearer the lens to focus, and the whole instrument, a few centimeters long, is held up close to the eye. Baker found that they magnified the diameter of an object between 40 and 160 times:

The surviving instruments reach about 275 times. How he made the lenses he kept to himself. Baker wrote that they were ground. A neutron study of the originals in 2021 found a short glass stem on one lens, the mark of a bead melted from a drawn glass thread in a flame, a method **[Robert Hooke](kloom:e/robert-hooke)** had described.

## Blood in a hair of glass

His letter of 1 June 1674 says how he mounted blood, so that the Society's members could do it too, and he sent them the glass tubes to try:

| Step | What Leeuwenhoek did                                                                                              |
| ---- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1    | Drew out thin hollow glass tubes, some "not thicker than a mans-hair"                                             |
| 2    | Tied a string round the top joint of his thumb, "as is usual in opening a Vein of an Arm"                         |
| 3    | Pricked the thumb with a needle, wiped the first blood away, and squeezed out more                                |
| 4    | Touched the tube to the drop: the blood ran up into it by itself                                                  |
| 5    | Laid the tube on white paper, broke off a piece with his nail, and stuck it to the pin with spittle or turpentine |

The thinner the tube, he found, the clearer the globules. A thicker one showed the clear liquid they swam in, and how they sank: they were heavier than it, and left standing they stuck together into shapeless lumps. An observer in Paris who was sent tubes saw no globules at all, and that, Leeuwenhoek explained, was why. A single globule stuck to the glass looked "white and colourless", and blood spread four or five globules thick had little color. **[Robert Boyle](kloom:e/robert-boyle)** had asked, through Oldenburg, why blood turns bright red in air. Leeuwenhoek's answer, that air could not move freely round globules packed together, was wrong. He said he had seen the figures in the books of Boyle and Hooke, but regretted that he could not read them.

## A grain of sand

He had no scale in his microscope. He measured by comparison, against small things everyone knew: a coarse grain of sand, a fine one, a hair from his beard. "The red Globuls of the Blood I reckon to be 25000 times smaller than a fine grain of sand", he wrote, and explained the geometry: of two globes whose axes are as 1 to 20, the volumes are as 1 to 8,000. His 25,000 was a ratio of volumes. By our arithmetic the cube root of 25,000 is about 29, so a globule's diameter was a twenty-ninth of the sand grain's. He later put a fine grain at an eightieth to a hundredth of an inch, and the editors of his collected letters work his figure out as about 8.5 micrometers. In 1684 he wrote that a hundred globules side by side would not span a coarse grain, which by the editors' reckoning puts each under about 8.7 micrometers. Today's figure for a human red cell is about 6 to 8.

He may not have been the first to see them. **[Jan Swammerdam](kloom:e/jan-swammerdam)** of Amsterdam described the blood of a frog as "a vast number of orbicular particles, of a flat oval but regular figure". The usual date for that is 1658, sixteen years before Leeuwenhoek's letter, but Swammerdam's words were printed only in 1737–38, long after his death, and the book does not date them. Leeuwenhoek's description was published first and gave a size. Why the globules are red, Boyle's question, waited nearly two centuries. The next frame measures the force that drives them.
