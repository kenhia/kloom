Copper conducts, diamond does not, and silicon sits in between, in a way
that changes with temperature and with the faintest trace of impurity. By
1900 electrons had been found, and physicists pictured a metal as a box of
them flowing freely. That picture could not say why a crystal of carbon,
full of electrons too, lets none of them move, nor why in some materials
the current seemed to be carried by positive charges. Quantum mechanics,
applied to the regular array of atoms in a crystal, answered both within
four years, and explained the material of modern electronics.

## Waves in a lattice

In Leipzig in 1928 **[Felix Bloch](kloom:e/felix-bloch)**, Heisenberg's first doctoral student,
asked how an electron moves through a perfectly regular lattice of atoms.
His answer, published early the next year, is now called Bloch's theorem:
the electron's wave is a plain travelling wave multiplied by a pattern
that repeats with the lattice. Such a wave passes through a perfect
crystal without scattering at all. Resistance, in Bloch's picture, comes
only from what spoils the regularity: impurities, defects and the
vibrations of the atoms.

The same waves have forbidden energies. Where an electron's wavelength
fits the spacing of the atoms, waves reflected from successive rows add
up, and no travelling wave of that energy exists. The allowed energies
fall into **bands**, separated by **gaps**; the plate computes them for a
row of atoms, the parabola of a free electron broken where it meets the
edges of the zone. In 1929 **[Rudolf Peierls](kloom:e/rudolf-peierls)** used Bloch's waves to show
that a band nearly full of electrons behaves as if it held a few positive
carriers, the _holes_, which explained the reversed Hall voltages that had
puzzled experimenters.

## Metals, insulators and the in-between

**[Alan Herries Wilson](kloom:e/alan-herries-wilson)**, a Cambridge mathematician working with Heisenberg,
drew the conclusion in 1931. A band completely full of
electrons carries no current, because for every electron moving one way
another moves the other. So a solid whose electrons exactly fill its
bands, with a wide gap above, is an insulator; one with a band partly
filled is a metal. If the gap is narrow, heat lifts a few electrons across
it, and the solid conducts a little, more as it warms: a
**[semiconductor](kloom:e/semiconductor)**. Impurities could supply carriers too, which explained
why samples of the same material disagreed so wildly. The theory of
**[electronic band structure](kloom:e/electronic-band-structure)** was complete in outline. Wilson found little
interest in it at Cambridge, turned to nuclear physics, and after the war
left science for industry, ending as chairman of Glaxo.

## A crack in a silicon rod

The practical half came from radio. At Bell Labs' field station in
Holmdel, New Jersey, **[Russell Ohl](kloom:e/russell-ohl)** had been purifying silicon for crystal
detectors. On 23 February 1940 one rod, cut from an ingot that had cooled
slowly, gave strange readings near a crack, and changed them when a desk
lamp was switched on. On 6 March he showed it to Bell's research director,
Mervin Kelly, and the voltmeter jumped to nearly half a volt when a
flashlight shone on it. Walter Brattain, watching, suspected a barrier
inside the silicon.

As the ingot froze, impurities had separated: boron above, making one end
conduct by holes, and phosphorus below, supplying electrons, which the
metallurgists could smell as phosphine when they broke the quartz tubes.
Ohl and Jack Scaff named the two kinds **p-type** and **n-type**, and the
boundary between them the **[p–n junction](kloom:e/p-n-junction)**. The history by Michael
Riordan and Lillian Hoddeson tells the story from Ohl's notebooks; other
accounts date the discovery to 1939, when the odd rod first puzzled a
colleague.

How much a trace matters is easy to work out. Pure silicon holds about
5 × 10²² atoms in a cubic centimetre. Replace one in five million with
phosphorus, 10¹⁶ per cubic centimetre, and with an electron mobility of
1,400 cm²/V·s its resistivity falls from 2,300 to about 0.0045 ohm-metres:
half a million times lower. (The arithmetic is ours, from the sourced
figures.)

## Why the junction works

Put p against n and electrons spill across into the holes, leaving a thin
layer stripped of carriers and a built-in voltage, about 0.6 V in silicon,
that stops the flow. A voltage one way lowers that barrier and current
pours through; the other way raises it and almost nothing passes. That is
a rectifier, and a reverse-biased junction is also an insulating wall
inside a chip. **[William Shockley](kloom:e/william-shockley)**'s theory of 1949 explained what
happens when carriers are pushed across a junction into the region beyond
it, and two junctions back to back, with a thin middle layer, make a
transistor.

The width of the gap decides the rest. Heat makes carriers across the gap
in proportion to a factor that falls steeply as the gap widens, which is
why pure germanium, by the table, conducts five thousand times better than
pure silicon,
and why germanium devices leaked when they should have been off. In some
metals, cooled far enough, the electrons do something stranger still: the
next frame.
