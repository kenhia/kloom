In 1886, at the Technical High School in Karlsruhe, a new professor of
physics, **[Heinrich Hertz](kloom:e/heinrich-hertz)**, was using a pair of flat coils of wire in his
lectures, and noticed that a small spark through one made a spark jump in
the other. (It was spring, in his own account; autumn, in some later
ones.) Seven years earlier his teacher **[Hermann von Helmholtz](kloom:e/hermann-von-helmholtz)** had set a
prize problem in Berlin: to show by experiment an effect in insulators
that Maxwell's theory predicted. Hertz had judged it too hard, and the
prize had lapsed unclaimed. The little spark told him how to try.

## A spark and a ring

His source of waves was two straight wires in a line, each ending in a
large plate or sphere, with a gap a few millimetres wide between their
inner ends. An induction coil charged the two halves until a spark crossed
the gap; while it lasted, charge surged back and forth along the wires
tens of millions of times a second, and the wires radiated. The detector
was simpler still: a ring of copper wire, 35 centimetres in radius, broken
by a gap that a screw could narrow to a hair's breadth. When a wave passed,
a tiny spark crossed it, visible only in a darkened room. A ring the right
size for the oscillator answered it best: the two were tuned to each
other.

![Hertz's own figure of the apparatus: two square plates A and A′ on a rod with a spark gap between them, fed by an induction coil J; a wire running off to the left from a plate P beside A; and two wire circuits, B and C, with small spark gaps, placed along the dotted line of the room's axis, marked off in metres](hertz-expt.png)

The figure is Hertz's, from his paper of 1888 on the finite speed of
electrical action. In March 1888 he fixed a sheet of zinc 4 metres high
and 2 wide to the sandstone wall of his lecture room and set the
oscillator 13 metres in front of it. The wave running to the wall and the
wave reflected from it made a standing wave, and the ring, carried along
the line between them, found places where its spark died away and places
where it was strongest. The plate draws Hertz's own reading of it: nodes
4.8 metres apart, with the first just behind the wall. (Some modern
accounts give 12 metres and waves 4 metres long; Hertz's paper gives the
numbers used here.)

## Light without the light

Waves of nine or ten metres were awkward to handle, so Hertz made a much
smaller oscillator, with waves about 60 centimetres long. With
these the whole of optics followed. A parabolic mirror of bent zinc, 2
metres high, made a beam that could be felt 5 or 6 metres away. A flat
sheet reflected it. A screen of parallel wires stopped the beam or let it
through, depending on how it was turned: the waves were polarised, as the
oscillator's straight wires said they should be. A prism of pitch, 1.5
metres tall and weighing about 12 hundredweight, bent the beam by 22
degrees. The experiments, Hertz wrote, were "adapted to remove any doubt
as to the identity of light, radiant heat, and electromagnetic
wave-motion."

The speed was harder. His first measurement, in 1888, compared waves in
air with waves along a wire and made the air faster, in the ratio of about
45 to 28, and Hertz announced the difference "without any reserve."
Physicists in Geneva, working in the great hall of the Rhône waterworks,
found the two speeds equal. Hertz conceded, in 1892, that his room had
probably fooled him, and that he "may have allowed myself to be influenced
by a desire to establish an accordance" between his measurements.

## Four equations

**[Maxwell's equations](kloom:e/maxwells-equations)**, as Maxwell left them, were some twenty equations
in twenty quantities. From the mid-1880s **[Oliver Heaviside](kloom:e/oliver-heaviside)**, a self-taught
telegraph engineer writing in a trade paper, _The Electrician_, cut them
down with vector notation to four, in the two fields alone. Hertz reached
nearly the same form in 1890 and said plainly that "Mr. Heaviside has the
priority." In today's units:

| Equation                                     | Says                                                    |
| -------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------- |
| ∇ · **E** = _ρ_/_ε_₀                         | charge is where electric lines begin                    |
| ∇ · **B** = 0                                | magnetic lines never end                                |
| ∇ × **E** = −∂**B**/∂*t*                     | a changing magnetic field makes a circling electric one |
| ∇ × **B** = _μ_₀**J** + _μ_₀_ε_₀ ∂**E**/∂*t* | currents, and changing electric fields, make magnetism  |

Hertz's own summary was the shortest: "Maxwell's theory is Maxwell's
system of equations." Heaviside, **[George Francis FitzGerald](kloom:e/george-francis-fitzgerald)** in Dublin
and **[Oliver Lodge](kloom:e/oliver-lodge)** in Liverpool, with Hertz, were the group the
historian Bruce Hunt calls the Maxwellians, who turned a hard book into a
working theory. FitzGerald had suggested in 1883 how such waves might be
made; Lodge was sending waves along wires in 1888 when he read Hertz's
papers.

## What it was good for

For Hertz the prize was philosophical: forces acting at a distance, he
wrote, were "dethroned" by simple experiments. He is often quoted as
saying the waves were "of no use whatsoever", but Wikipedia and Wikiquote
take the line from a physics textbook of 2000, with no source from his
lifetime.
He died on 1 January 1894, aged 36. That June Lodge demonstrated Hertz's
waves at the **Royal Institution** in his memory, and in 1895 **[Guglielmo
Marconi](kloom:e/guglielmo-marconi)** was sending signals across his father's estate near Bologna.
From there the trail returns to its anchor, the field itself.
