By the mid-1970s the electroweak theory had passed its first test, the
neutral current, and measurements of the weak mixing angle said where its
heavy carriers should be: the W at about 80 GeV, the Z at about 90, each
nearly a hundred times the mass of a proton. No machine could make them.
[CERN](kloom:e/cern)'s new [Super Proton Synchrotron](kloom:e/super-proton-synchrotron), which started in 1976, fired protons
of up to 400 GeV at a fixed target, but a collision with a target at rest
wastes most of the energy on carrying the debris forward, and left only
about 30 GeV to make anything. The answer was to collide two beams head
on, and to do it without building a new ring.

## Antiprotons on demand

In 1976 **[Carlo Rubbia](kloom:e/carlo-rubbia)**, with **David Cline** and **Peter McIntyre**,
proposed turning an existing proton accelerator into a collider by sending
antiprotons round the same pipe the other way. They offered the idea to
CERN and to Fermilab. Pierre Darriulat, who later led one of the
experiments, remembered that most machine experts gave it little chance,
and that the fear of Rubbia selling it to Fermilab helped CERN decide.

The difficulty was the antiprotons. Protons of 26 GeV striking a target
made about one antiproton for every million protons, in a spray of
directions and energies far too loose to collide. **[Simon van der Meer](kloom:e/simon-van-der-meer)**
had invented a way to tame such a beam, _stochastic cooling_, and it is
what the plate draws. A pickup on one side of a storage ring senses how
far the particles passing it stray from the ideal orbit. The signal is
amplified and sent straight across the ring, a shorter path than the
particles take, so that it reaches a kicker in time to nudge those same
particles back. Repeated turn after turn, it packs the beam tight. A test
ring proved it in June 1978; the experiment **[UA1](kloom:e/ua1-experiment)**, led by Rubbia, was
approved that month and a second, **[UA2](kloom:e/ua2-experiment)**, led by Darriulat, in December.
The Antiproton Accumulator built for it increased the antiproton current
several hundred thousand times.

The first collisions came on 9 July 1981, protons and antiprotons of
270 GeV each: 540 GeV head on.

## Five events in a billion

A W is short-lived. The experiments looked for one decaying into an
electron with a large momentum across the beam and, on the opposite side,
the same momentum missing: an unseen neutrino. In the run of November and
December 1982, about a billion collisions in UA1 gave five such events; UA2
had four. **CERN** announced the discovery of the W on 25 January 1983,
after seminars by both teams a few days before. UA1's first estimate of its
mass was 81 ± 5 GeV.

![Five men seated at a curved conference table with name cards before them, a map of the CERN site on the wall behind: the press conference of 25 January 1983 announcing the W, with Carlo Rubbia, Simon van der Meer, Herwig Schopper, Erwin Gabathuler and Pierre Darriulat](press-conference.jpg)

The Z was predicted to be ten times rarer. A run in April and May 1983
gave UA1 four Z decays into an electron and a positron and one into a pair
of muons, and CERN announced the Z on 1 June. UA2, which had eight
candidates clustered near 90 GeV, chose to wait for more data before
publishing, and came out about two months later. UA1's larger sample of W
decays showed, "unmistakably", the left-handedness of the weak force.

Rubbia and van der Meer shared the Nobel Prize in October 1984, barely a
year after the discovery. Darriulat thought the credit rightly theirs: the
achievement was making the particles, not detecting them.

## Counting neutrinos

The Z's precision came next. The **[Large Electron–Positron Collider](kloom:e/large-electron-positron-collider)**,
LEP, a ring 27 kilometers round, began in August 1989 by colliding
electrons and positrons at the Z's mass. The Z can decay into any pair
of light particles, neutrinos included, so the more kinds of neutrino there
are, the shorter its life, the broader its peak and the lower its height.
By the end of 1989 LEP had shown there are three. Its four experiments and
SLD at Stanford recorded some 17 million Z decays in all and put the
number of light neutrino kinds at 2.9840 ± 0.0082.

The W's mass is still argued over. In 2022 a new analysis of data from
Fermilab's Tevatron gave 80.433 ± 0.009 GeV, seven standard deviations
above the Standard Model's prediction; ATLAS measured 80.360 ± 0.016 in
2023, in line with it, and the 2025 world average is 80.369 ± 0.013.

One piece of the electroweak theory was still missing: the field that
gave the W and Z their mass, and its particle, the Higgs.
