By 1970 the quark model sorted the particles well, and the experiments at
SLAC had seen hard, point-like things inside the proton that behaved, when
struck hard, almost as if they were free. Nobody had knocked a quark loose.
A force that held quarks so tightly they never came out, yet let them
rattle about freely inside, looked like a contradiction. In 1973 a
calculation showed it was not, and the theory of the strong force,
**[quantum chromodynamics](kloom:e/quantum-chromodynamics)**, followed from it.

## Color

The first clue was a counting problem. The quark model built the Δ⁺⁺
particle from three up quarks with their spins aligned, and the Pauli
exclusion principle forbids three identical fermions in the same state.
In 1964 **Oscar Greenberg** proposed that quarks carry a hidden
three-valued property, and in 1965 **Moo-Young Han** and **Yoichiro Nambu**
made it the charge of a gauge symmetry, SU(3), with an octet of carriers
between the quarks: the **[gluons](kloom:e/gluon)**. Early in the 1970s **[Murray Gell-Mann](kloom:e/murray-gell-mann)**
named the property _color_, the three values red, green and blue, and
with Harald Fritzsch argued for a field theory of colored quarks and
gluons. Only colorless combinations, three quarks of different colors
or a quark and its antiquark, were ever seen.

Field theory itself was then out of favor for the strong force. In
quantum electrodynamics a charge is screened by the pairs of virtual
particles it polarizes around it, so its effective strength grows at short
distances; it seemed any theory must behave so. A force that grows at
short distance could not explain SLAC's free-looking quarks. Until 1973, [David Gross](kloom:e/david-gross)
recalled, a field theory was not used without apologies: as late as 1972
Gell-Mann ended a talk on quarks proposing a theory of quarks and
"some kind of glue, treated as fictitious".

## A sign

At the end of 1972 **David Gross**, at Princeton, set out, by his own
account, to prove that no field theory could explain SLAC's results, and so
to settle the question against field theory. He and Sidney Coleman proved
it for every kind of theory but one, Yang–Mills gauge theories. Gross and
his student **[Frank Wilczek](kloom:e/frank-wilczek)** set out to close that gap by calculating how
the coupling of a Yang–Mills theory changes with distance. At Harvard,
Coleman's student **[David Politzer](kloom:e/hugh-david-politzer)** was doing the same calculation for a
different purpose. In February 1973, Gross wrote, a sign error convinced
Wilczek and him that the theory was not asymptotically free, and he caught
it as he sat down to write the paper. Politzer finished at almost the same
time; the results agreed, and their papers appeared side by side in
the same issue of _Physical Review Letters_. Others, among them Gerard 't Hooft in 1972, had
come across the sign before; no one had seen what it meant.

What it meant is **[asymptotic freedom](kloom:e/asymptotic-freedom)**. Gluons carry color themselves,
unlike photons, which carry no charge. Their virtual cloud around a quark
does not screen its color but spreads it out, so the closer one looks,
the weaker the force. The plate draws the result: the strong coupling,
_α_s, against the energy of a collision, falling slowly as the energy rises.
The curve is this frame's own computation from the lowest-order formula
and the 2025 world average, _α_s = 0.1180 ± 0.0009 at the mass of the Z. It
is not a fit to data, and it understates the rise below a few GeV.

## Confinement and the gluon

Run the argument the other way and the force grows with distance. Gross
and Wilczek argued in 1973 that this would lock quarks and gluons into
colorless particles for good, which Georgi and Glashow later called
_infrared slavery_. It is believed, borne out by calculation, and still
unproven: it is one of the Clay Institute's Millennium Prize problems.

A quark pulled out of a proton therefore never emerges alone. The energy of
the stretched field makes new quark–antiquark pairs, and each quark comes
out as a narrow _jet_ of particles. If a quark could radiate a hard gluon,
some collisions should show three jets in a plane instead of two. The
electron–positron collider **[PETRA](kloom:e/positron-electron-tandem-ring-accelerator)**, at **DESY** in Hamburg, saw them in the
summer of 1979, in the TASSO, MARK-J and PLUTO experiments, and in JADE
the next year. A hint had come a year earlier from PLUTO at DESY's older
ring, DORIS.

## The proton's mass

The quarks inside a proton account for only a small part of its mass;
most of it is the energy of the quarks' motion and of the gluon field. Only
a computer can calculate that, on a lattice of points in space and time.
In 2008 Stephan Dürr, Zoltán Fodor and their colleagues computed the masses
of the proton, the neutron and other light particles this way from the
theory alone, and they agreed with experiment. Gross, Politzer and Wilczek
shared the Nobel Prize in 2004.

Quantum chromodynamics and the electroweak theory, both built on Yang and
Mills, now made up the Standard Model. Its weak carriers, the W and the Z,
had yet to be seen.
