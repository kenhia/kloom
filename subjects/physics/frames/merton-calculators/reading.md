Aristotle had said what makes things move. He had not said how to measure
motion, and in the 1330s a handful of fellows of **[Merton College](kloom:e/merton-college-oxford)**, Oxford,
set out to do it, with logic and ratios and no experiments at all. They are
now called the **[Oxford Calculators](kloom:e/oxford-calculators)**, after the _Book of Calculations_ of
one of them, Richard Swineshead, and the rule they found for a steadily
changing speed is one physics still teaches.

## Speed as a quantity

**[Thomas Bradwardine](kloom:e/thomas-bradwardine)**, a fellow of Merton from 1321, began it in 1328 with
a _Treatise on the Ratios of Speeds in Motions_. Aristotle's commentators
read him as saying that speed goes as force divided by resistance.
Bradwardine showed that this cannot be squared with Aristotle's other rule,
that nothing moves when the resistance is as great as the force: keep
doubling the resistance, and force over resistance never reaches zero. He
proposed instead that speed rises by equal steps as the ratio of force to
resistance is multiplied, in modern notation that speed goes as the
logarithm of force over resistance. It was wrong, and it was taught for
more than a century. Bradwardine became Archbishop of Canterbury in 1349
and died of the plague soon after.

His successors, among them **[William Heytesbury](kloom:e/william-of-heytesbury)**, John Dumbleton and
Swineshead, treated speed the way philosophers already treated heat or
whiteness: as a quality with an intensity, its _latitude_, that could stay
the same or change. A motion that gains equal latitudes of speed in equal
times they called _uniformly difform_. We call it uniform acceleration.

## The middle degree

Heytesbury stated the rule in his _Rules for Solving Sophisms_, a logic
textbook of the 1330s. A speed acquired or lost uniformly, he wrote,
"corresponds precisely to its own middle degree": a body that speeds up
steadily for a given time covers the same distance as one moving the whole
time at the speed it had at the middle instant. Now called the **[mean speed
theorem](kloom:e/mean-speed-theorem)**, it gives, among its corollaries, a ratio: from rest, a body
covers three times as far in the second half of the time as in the first.

| An invented example, from rest | Speed at start | Speed at end | Mean speed | Distance |
| ------------------------------ | -------------: | -----------: | ---------: | -------: |
| First 2 seconds                |              0 |            4 |          2 |        4 |
| Next 2 seconds                 |              4 |            8 |          6 |       12 |
| All 4 seconds                  |              0 |            8 |          4 |       16 |

The numbers in the table are invented, in paces a second and paces, to show
the rule at work. The Calculators argued _secundum imaginationem_, by
imagined cases, and did not claim that a falling stone accelerates this
way. The first to say so in print, as far as is known, was the Spanish
Dominican Domingo de Soto, in 1551.

## Oresme's figures

In Paris, **[Nicole Oresme](kloom:e/nicole-oresme)**, who studied under [Jean Buridan](kloom:e/jean-buridan) in the 1340s,
drew the rule. In his _Treatise on the Configurations of Qualities and
Motions_ time runs along a base line, the _longitude_, and the speed at each
instant stands up from it as a _latitude_. Uniform acceleration from rest
becomes a right triangle, and the distance run is its area. The plate draws
his proof, the first known proof of the Merton rule: the rectangle at the
middle degree has the same area as the triangle, because the corner of the
triangle above the rectangle, turned about the middle point, fills the gap
below. Draw the middle instant, and the two halves of the triangle hold one
part and three.

![A page of the Tractatus de latitudinibus formarum printed at Padua in 1486: black-letter Latin text with an ornamented initial S, and three small figures in the margin, a half-disc and a quarter-disc filled with vertical lines, and a row of vertical bars stepping down in height](latitudes-1486.jpg)

A short digest of the method, printed under Oresme's name as the
_Tractatus de latitudinibus formarum_, went through several editions from
1482, and Galileo's _Two New Sciences_ of 1638 opens its account of
accelerated motion with the same theorem. The historian Marshall Clagett
warned against the larger claims made for Oresme, among them that he found
the law of free fall before Galileo and the Earth's rotation before
Copernicus: "None of these claims is, in fact, true," though each rests on
discussion "of some penetration and originality". Oresme argued that the
daily motion of the heavens could as well be the Earth turning, and then
decided that it was not.

## Impetus

Also in Paris, **Jean Buridan**, twice rector of the university, took up
the thrown stone that Aristotle's air had to push along. The thrower, he
argued, gives the stone an internal motive force, an [_impetus_](kloom:e/theory-of-impetus), which would
carry it on "of infinite duration were it not diminished and corrupted by
some contrary force resisting it". The more matter and the more speed, the
more impetus; and a falling body speeds up because it keeps gaining it. The
idea was not his alone: John Philoponus and Avicenna had versions of it
before him. The physicist and historian Pierre Duhem made Buridan a
forerunner of Galileo. The philosopher Jack Zupko calls that too much:
Buridan stayed an Aristotelian, and transformed only details.

Centuries later **Leibniz**, who hoped to settle every argument by
calculation, called Swineshead almost the first to bring mathematics into
philosophy. What the Calculators lacked was a measurement, and **Galileo's
inclined planes** would supply one. First, though, the Earth itself began to
move, in **Copernicus**'s book of 1543.
