In 1909 the best place in the world to count alpha particles was the
physics laboratory of **[Ernest Rutherford](kloom:e/ernest-rutherford)** at the **[University of
Manchester](kloom:e/university-of-manchester)**. Rutherford, who had named the alpha particle, used it as a
probe to fire through thin metal. The atom most physicists
pictured was J. J. Thomson's, a sphere of thinly spread positive charge with
electrons dotted through it. An alpha particle should pass through such an
atom almost untouched. Within four years we knew that the atom was nearly
all empty space, with almost all its mass in a center a hundred thousand
times smaller than itself.

## Backwards from the foil

**[Hans Geiger](kloom:e/hans-geiger)** had been measuring how a foil spreads a narrow beam of alpha
particles, counting each one by the flash it made on a zinc sulfide
screen through a microscope. Rutherford suggested that
**[Ernest Marsden](kloom:e/ernest-marsden)**, an undergraduate, look for particles coming back off
the metal, though nobody expected any. Marsden found them.
In their 1909 paper Geiger and Marsden reported that about one alpha
particle in 8,000 striking a platinum plate was turned back towards the
side it came from. Heavy metals such as gold turned back more than
light ones such as aluminum, and a thin foil did it too, so it happened
inside the metal, not at its surface.

The experiment is remembered for its gold foil, but the 1909 paper tried
eight metals, and its one in 8,000 is for platinum. Rutherford's 1911 paper
quotes another figure: about one in 20,000 turned through 90 degrees or
more by a thin gold foil.

The famous line is Rutherford's own telling, from a lecture at Cambridge in
October 1936: it was "almost as incredible as if you fired a 15-inch shell
at a piece of tissue paper and it came back and hit you." It is a memory
twenty-seven years old, and the historians' account is less dramatic. In
1906 Rutherford had already seen alpha particles deflected by mica and
written that atoms "must be the seat of very intense electrical forces";
the backward scattering confirmed a suspicion as much as it overturned a
belief.

## A minute center

Rutherford's paper of May 1911 argued that so large a deflection must come
from a single encounter, which was only possible if the atom's charge sat in a tiny
center. He worked out how many particles should come out at each angle,
and in 1913 Geiger and Marsden, with a microscope on a turntable, confirmed
it: the counts followed his formula for the angle, the foil's thickness, the
atom's weight and the particles' speed. The plate draws what he
calculated: each path a hyperbola, most bent only a little, and one aimed
almost straight at the center that comes back.

The 1911 paper did not yet name it. It spoke of a "central charge",
said that the results would fit it whether it was positive or negative,
and left the electrons alone. Rutherford called it the [nucleus](kloom:e/atomic-nucleus), and
positive, in 1913. That year Niels Bohr, who had worked under Rutherford,
visitor, put the electrons on quantum orbits around it: the Bohr atom.

## What it is made of

In experiments begun during the war and published in 1919, Rutherford fired
alpha particles into nitrogen and found hydrogen nuclei flying out: the
first nucleus changed on purpose. The hydrogen nucleus, he concluded, was a
building block of every nucleus, and in 1920 he proposed a name for it:
the **[proton](kloom:e/proton)**.

Protons could not be the whole story, because nuclei weighed about twice
what their charge allowed. In his Bakerian Lecture of 1920 Rutherford
guessed at a neutral particle of about a proton's mass, and he and **[James
Chadwick](kloom:e/james-chadwick)** looked for one at Cambridge's Cavendish Laboratory through the
1920s without success. Early in 1932 Irène Joliot-Curie and Frédéric
Joliot in Paris found that a penetrating radiation from beryllium knocked
protons out of paraffin wax, and took it for gamma rays. Chadwick saw that
gamma rays could not carry that much momentum. After about two weeks of
experiments he wrote to _Nature_, in a letter dated 17 February, that the
difficulties disappeared "if it be assumed that the radiation consists of
particles of mass 1 and charge 0, or neutrons." The **[neutron](kloom:e/neutron)** won him the
Nobel Prize in 1935.

| Particle | Found            | Charge | Mass, electron = 1 |
| -------- | ---------------- | -----: | -----------------: |
| Electron | 1897, Thomson    |     −1 |                  1 |
| Proton   | 1919, Rutherford |     +1 |        about 1,800 |
| Neutron  | 1932, Chadwick   |      0 |        about 1,800 |

The neutron explained the **isotopes**, the name Frederick Soddy took up in
1913, at the suggestion of the writer Margaret Todd, for atoms that behaved identically in
chemistry but differed in weight: "the same place" in the periodic table.
Same number of protons, different numbers of neutrons.

## The puzzle left over

Protons repel each other, and a nucleus holds them about a million-millionth
of a millimeter apart. Something stronger held them, and only at close range.
Werner Heisenberg wrote down a nucleus of protons and neutrons held by an
exchange force within months of Chadwick's letter, and in 1934 Hideki
Yukawa proposed that the force was carried by a new, heavy particle. The
force, we now know, is attractive at about a femtometer and negligible
beyond about two and a half.

Rutherford's method outlived his question. Scattering became particle
physics's main instrument, and in 1968 electrons fired at protons at SLAC
bounced off hard little points inside them just as his alpha particles had
bounced off the nucleus. The nucleus also held energy that Rutherford
thought no one would ever use, and in 1938 a chemist in Berlin showed how:
fission.
