In 1543, in Nuremberg, the printer **[Johannes Petreius](kloom:e/johannes-petreius)**
finished a folio of six books with a title that promised only astronomy:
[_De revolutionibus orbium coelestium_](kloom:e/de-revolutionibus-orbium-coelestium), _On the Revolutions of the Heavenly
Spheres_. Its author, **[Nicolaus Copernicus](kloom:e/nicolaus-copernicus)**, a canon of the cathedral
chapter at Frombork on the Baltic, was seventy and dying. By one account he
held a finished copy on the day he died, 24 May. The book put the Sun near
the centre of the planets' circles and set the Earth moving round it, once a
year, while turning once a day.

## A more reasonable arrangement

He had had the idea for thirty years. Between about 1508 and 1514 he wrote
a short sketch, now called the **[Commentariolus](kloom:e/commentariolus)**, and sent copies to other
astronomers; a Cracow professor listed "a manuscript of six leaves" in 1514
whose author "asserts that the earth moves while the sun stands still".
What troubled him was not Ptolemy's accuracy but his [equant](kloom:e/equant), which moved a
planet evenly about a point that was not the centre of its circle. So, he
wrote, "I often considered whether there could perhaps be found a more
reasonable arrangement of circles", in which every motion would be uniform,
"as is required by the rule of perfect motion".

The arrangement he found did something no geocentric one could. With the
Earth as a planet, "the order and size of all the planets and spheres", as
he put it in his dedication to Pope Paul III, follow from the observations,
and the order runs with the lengths of their years: Mercury in eighty days, Venus in nine
months, the Earth in one year, Mars in two, Jupiter in twelve, Saturn in
thirty. The heavens, he wrote, are "so linked together" that no part can
be moved without disrupting the rest.

![A page of Copernicus's own manuscript of De revolutionibus: seven concentric circles drawn in ink, labelled in Latin from the sphere of the fixed stars inwards through Saturn, Jupiter, Mars, the Earth with the Moon, Venus and Mercury to Sol at the centre, with lines of handwritten text above and beside them](manuscript-heliocentric.jpg)

The plate draws the most visible gain. The Earth, on the inner circle,
moves faster than Mars. As it overtakes Mars, the line of sight from Earth
to Mars swings backwards against the far-off stars, and Mars seems to stop,
run backwards and go on. Ptolemy needed an epicycle for each planet to make
that loop; for Copernicus it was the Earth's own motion, seen from a moving
platform.

He was not the first. **[Aristarchus of Samos](kloom:e/aristarchus-of-samos)** had put the Sun at the centre
in the third century BC, as Archimedes reports. Copernicus named Aristarchus
in a passage of his manuscript that he struck out before printing, and the
historian Owen Gingerich found no sign that he owed Aristarchus the idea.

## Still circles

Copernicus kept the ancient rule that the heavens move in uniform circles,
so he could not do without small circles on circles. The Sun is not quite
at the centre of his system, and to match Ptolemy's accuracy he needed
about as many circles as Ptolemy had. Thomas Kuhn counted over thirty in
each, and judged that "there was little to choose between them in
economy", nor in accuracy. What most sixteenth-century astronomers admired,
Gingerich found, was not the moving Earth at all but the loss of the
equant. Erasmus Reinhold's _Prutenic Tables_ of 1551, computed from
Copernicus's models, came into wide use, though they were little more
accurate than the medieval tables they replaced.

## The unsigned preface

The book reached print because of a young visitor. **[Georg Joachim
Rheticus](kloom:e/georg-joachim-rheticus)**, professor of mathematics at Wittenberg, came to Frombork in
1539 with a gift of mathematical books, partly to show what the German
presses could print. He stayed two years, published the first printed account of the theory,
the _Narratio prima_, in 1540, and in 1542 took the manuscript to Petreius.
When Rheticus left for a post in Leipzig, the Lutheran preacher **[Andreas
Osiander](kloom:e/andreas-osiander)** saw the printing through, and put in front of Copernicus's own
preface an unsigned note _To the Reader_. It said that the book offered
hypotheses for computing positions, which "need not be true nor even
probable". That contradicted the book it introduced. Rheticus crossed it out
in his copy, and Bishop Tiedemann Giese asked Nuremberg's council to have
it corrected, without result. Many readers took it for Copernicus's own
view until Kepler, decades later, showed who had written it.

## The book nobody read?

The first printing, of perhaps four hundred copies, did not sell out, and
Arthur Koestler called it "the book that nobody read". Gingerich spent
thirty-five years tracing every surviving copy of the first two editions:
276 of the first and 325 of the second, many filled with the notes of the
astronomers who worked through them. Reaction was slow and mixed. In 1541
Philip Melanchthon at Wittenberg wrote of "that Polish astronomer who
makes the earth move and the sun stand still"; Tycho Brahe admired the
models and kept the Earth still. Nothing official was said in Rome until
1616, when the book was suspended pending corrections.

Copernicus had moved the Earth but kept the circles. The next frame is
about the astronomer who gave them up: **Johannes Kepler**.
