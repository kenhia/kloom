On 18 March 1905 the _Annalen der Physik_ received a paper from a clerk at
the Swiss patent office in Bern. Its title promised only "a heuristic point
of view", a useful way of looking at things, and its proposal was
radical. Light, **[Albert Einstein](kloom:e/albert-einstein)** suggested, behaves as if its energy
"consists of a finite number of energy quanta that are spatially localized
at points of space, move without dividing and are absorbed or generated
only as a whole." Each quantum of
light of frequency _f_ carries energy _hf_, where _h_ is the **[Planck
constant](kloom:e/planck-constant)**. Max Planck had used _h_ five years earlier for the energy of
the matter that gives off light; Einstein gave it to the light itself.

## Light that knocks out electrons

The strongest case in the paper was the **[photoelectric effect](kloom:e/photoelectric-effect)**. Light
shone on a metal knocks electrons out of it, and in 1902 Philipp Lenard had
found something no wave theory explained: the electrons' energy does not
depend on how bright the light is, only on its colour. A brighter light
releases more electrons, not faster ones, and light below a certain
frequency releases none at all, however bright.

With quanta it is simple. One quantum gives all its energy to one electron.
The electron spends a fixed amount, _P_, getting out of the metal and
leaves with what is left, so its greatest energy is _hf_ − _P_. Plot that
energy against frequency and every metal should give a straight line, and
every line the same slope, _h_. It was a prediction with a number in it,
and for ten years no one could test it well.

## The man who expected otherwise

**[Robert Millikan](kloom:e/robert-millikan)**, at the University of Chicago, did. A metal
surface tarnishes within minutes, and Lenard's measurements had suffered
for it. Millikan pared his metals fresh with a knife worked by
electromagnets inside the evacuated tube, "a machine shop in vacuo", and
lit them with light of one frequency at a time. His paper
of March 1916 found Einstein's straight line and from its slope the value
_h_ = 6.57 × 10⁻²⁷ erg-seconds, which he put at "a precision of about 0.5
per cent".

![Millikan's graph from his 1916 paper, drawn on squared paper: the stopping voltage of the electrons rising in a straight line with the frequency of the light, through six measured points, with his working in a box: the slope, multiplied by the electron's charge, gives h = 6.56 × 10⁻²⁷](millikan-h.jpg)

He did not believe the idea he had confirmed. In a paper of January 1916 he
wrote that Einstein's equation "cannot in my judgment be looked upon at
present as resting upon any sort of a satisfactory theoretical foundation",
and he called the light quantum "bold, not to say reckless". The story that
he set out to disprove Einstein and failed is his own later one: his Nobel
lecture spoke of a result "contrary to my own expectation", and his
autobiography of 1950 told the work as proof of the **[photon](kloom:e/photon)**. The historian
**Gerald Holton**, reading the papers, found little sign of that struggle in
them: Millikan tested an equation and rejected its physics.

The graph shows where his number came from: the slope times his own value
of the electron's charge, from his oil drops. That charge was 0.6 per cent
too low, and his _h_ is 0.85 per cent below today's; most of the gap is
the charge. The comparison is ours.

| Quantity          | Millikan, 1916   | Fixed exactly in 2019  |
| ----------------- | ---------------- | ---------------------- |
| Planck constant   | 6.57 × 10⁻³⁴ J s | 6.62607015 × 10⁻³⁴ J s |
| Electron's charge | 1.592 × 10⁻¹⁹ C  | 1.602176634 × 10⁻¹⁹ C  |

## A prize, a collision and a name

Einstein's Nobel Prize, the prize for 1921 awarded in 1922, was given "for
his services to Theoretical Physics, and especially for his discovery of
the law of the photoelectric effect": not for relativity, and for the law,
not the light quanta behind it. Holton credits Millikan's measurements with
persuading the committee.

Most physicists came round in 1923. **[Arthur Compton](kloom:e/arthur-compton)**, at Washington
University in St. Louis, scattered X-rays off the electrons in graphite and
found them coming out with longer wavelengths, by an amount that depended
only on the angle. **[Compton scattering](kloom:e/compton-scattering)** is a collision: a quantum carrying
momentum _h_/_λ_ strikes an electron, gives it some energy and bounces off
with less. The plate draws it to scale for a quantum as energetic as the
electron's own rest mass, where the wavelength doubles at 90°; for
Compton's X-rays, of about 17 keV, the shift at 90° is about 3 per cent, by
our arithmetic. Bohr, Kramers and Slater tried once more in 1924 to keep
light a wave, with energy conserved only on average; within a year,
experiments found it conserved in each collision.

The name came last. On 18 December 1926 the chemist **[Gilbert N. Lewis](kloom:e/gilbert-n-lewis)**
proposed "photon" in _Nature_, but for something else: an atom of light
that is never created or destroyed, "not light but plays an essential part
in every process of radiation". Nobody took up his theory, and within a
year or so he dropped it. The historian Helge Kragh found four earlier
uses of the word, from 1916 on, all forgotten. Compton used it at the 1927
Solvay conference, and it stuck to Einstein's quantum. Einstein never used
it.

Bohr resisted the light quantum until 1925. But he had already put Planck's
_h_ inside the atom, in 1913: the Bohr atom.
